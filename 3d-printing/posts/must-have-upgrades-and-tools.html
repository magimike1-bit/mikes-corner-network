Must-have upgrades and tools

Workshop · 7 min read

A printer, a spool of filament, and a slicer are enough to start. But a handful of cheap extras remove the most common friction: measuring, removing prints, storing filament, and fixing the little things. None of this is exotic — it's the stuff experienced owners reach for every week.

Calibration tools

Digital calipers. If you buy one tool, make it this. Calipers turn "looks about right" into "measures 20.02mm" — the difference between guessing and knowing when you print calibration cubes and fit-test parts. A feeler gauge set or a thin piece of card. For setting nozzle distance if your printer levels manually, and for checking gaps. A steel ruler. For measuring big prints and checking bed flatness. These three live next to the printer, always.

Print removal and finishing

A flexible scraper or spatula. Prints stick to beds; a thin flexible blade gets under them without gouging the surface or your fingers. Flush cutters. For trimming brims, supports, and stringing — the workhorse of post-processing. A small file set or deburring tool. For smoothing edges and cleaning up support scars. A heat gun (used carefully) melts away the fine wisps of stringing in seconds. Keep blades and cutters sharp and stored safely; they're small but they're real tools.

Filament storage

Filament absorbs moisture from the air, and damp filament prints badly. The fix is cheap: large resealable bags or airtight bins with rechargeable desiccant packs inside, and a spool or two labeled with the date it was opened. For humid climates or thirsty filaments like PETG and TPU, a filament dryer — a small heated box the spool sits in — pays for itself in rescued spools. Store filament like food: sealed, dry, and labeled.

The spare parts drawer

Keep a few consumables on hand so a worn part doesn't end a printing weekend: spare nozzles (they wear out, especially with abrasive or filled filaments), spare build-surface sheets or plates if yours is a consumable, a length of PTFE tube if your printer's hot end uses one, and a spare bed-leveling probe or limit switch if your machine is prone to that failure. These cost little and eliminate the worst downtime: waiting for shipping.

Upgrades worth doing first

Once the printer works reliably, a few upgrades give the best return. A better part-cooling setup — often just a printed duct for the existing fan — improves overhangs and bridging noticeably. A hardened steel nozzle if you print abrasive filaments, so you stop wearing out brass nozzles. A sturdier spool holder or dry box so filament feeds smoothly from storage straight into the printer. Notice the pattern: none of these are blinky lights. They're all about consistency.

Upgrades to skip (for now)

Multi-color add-ons, exotic hot ends, silent board swaps, and cosmetic lighting all have their place — but they add failure modes and tuning work for benefits a beginner rarely needs. Upgrade from a position of strength: when your prints are consistently good and you can name the specific limitation you want to fix, then the upgrade is obvious. Upgrading to fix bad prints usually just gives you a more expensive printer that prints badly.

The software side

A current slicer with profiles for your printer — most beginners do fine with the mainstream free slicers and the stock profile for their machine. A simple parametric CAD tool for designing your own parts; several have generous free tiers and gentle learning curves, and designing a bracket that fits is the moment the hobby clicks. A notes app or notebook for settings: what filament, what temperatures, what worked. The best upgrade of all is free — it's the habit of writing things down.
