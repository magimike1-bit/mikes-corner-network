How to choose your first AI agent: a plain-English buyer's guide

Choosing · 7 min read

Most "how to choose an AI agent" advice quietly assumes you are about to build something — pick a framework, wire up a canvas, write code. Most beginners are not. You probably want one annoying job handled: a weekly research summary, a first draft of the same report every Friday, your inbox sorted into piles. The real first decision is not which tool is "best." It is which of three routes fits that one job — because the routes differ in setup effort, in what they can touch, and in the limits that will actually bite you.

The short version

Pick the route before the product. If a chat app you already pay for has an agent mode, start there — it is the cheapest experiment you can run. If the same job comes up again and again with the same shape, graduate to a custom agent built for that job, or to an automation platform if the job is really "when X happens, do Y." Then, before paying for anything new, check the usage limits, not the headline price. The limits are where first agents go to die.

First, name the job — then pick a route

Write the job in one sentence with a finish line: "Compare these three supplier quotes and give me a one-page summary" is a job. "Help with admin" is not. The sentence matters because each route handles a different kind of sentence. Jobs that need judgment and web research suit an agent that thinks in the open. Jobs with a fixed trigger and a fixed outcome suit automation. Jobs you will hand to the same "assistant" every week, with the same instructions and the same sources, suit a custom agent you configure once. If you cannot write the sentence yet, no purchase will fix that — spend the ten minutes on the sentence first.

Notice what the table does not contain: programming frameworks. If you are reading a beginner guide on a site like this one, the build-it-yourself route is almost certainly the wrong first step — it asks the most of you before you have learned what you actually want an agent to do.

The limits nobody puts in the headline

Here is the detail that decides whether route one works for you. According to OpenAI's own help page, checked 16 October 2026, ChatGPT agent mode is capped at 40 messages a month on Plus and 400 a month on Pro, and it is not available on the free plan at all. Only your initial request counts against the cap — follow-up clarifications do not — but every fresh task does, including tasks that run on a schedule. Forty tasks a month is plenty for a weekly report and the odd comparison. It is not enough for "run this every morning and check my inbox twice a day." Do that arithmetic before you fall in love with a workflow.

Automation platforms count differently. On Zapier's pricing page, checked 16 October 2026, the free plan is $0 with 100 tasks a month and two-step workflows; Professional starts from $19.99 a month and Team from $69 a month. A "task" there is one successfully completed action — the trigger is free and failed actions do not count — and if you hit your limit, the account switches to pay-per-task billing at a higher rate unless you turn that off, in which case your workflows pause. None of this is hidden, exactly. It is just never in the headline, and it is the number that determines your real monthly bill.

Score your finalists in ten minutes

Once the job sentence is written and the route picked, run each candidate through these five questions and score each from 0 (no) to 2 (yes). It takes about ten minutes and beats a week of comparison-shopping.

  1. Can it reach the inputs? The job's raw material — your files, your inbox, a website, a spreadsheet — has to be something the agent can actually open, directly or through a connected app.
  2. Does the limit fit the rhythm? Multiply tasks per week by four. If that number is anywhere near the monthly cap, the route will frustrate you within a month.
  3. Can it stop and ask? A good first agent pauses for confirmation before anything hard to undo — sending, buying, deleting. If it cannot, keep it on read-and-draft jobs only.
  4. Can you see what it did? You want a trail: source links, a run history, a list of scheduled tasks you can pause. Invisible work is unverifiable work.
  5. Can you leave? If the agent vanished tomorrow, is your job documented well enough to rebuild elsewhere in an afternoon? If not, write the job sentence and instructions down somewhere of your own.

Any candidate scoring under 7 out of 10 is not your first agent, whatever its demo looked like. A tie goes to the option you already pay for — a built-in agent mode you can start today teaches you more, faster, than a marginally better tool you have to buy, configure, and learn from zero. And when a job outgrows its route — the weekly summary becomes a daily one, the draft becomes a send — that is the moment to move it up a route, not a reason to have started at the top.

FAQ

Do I need to pay anything to try a first agent? Not necessarily for automation: Zapier's free plan includes 100 tasks a month, enough to test a small workflow. Built-in agent modes are different — ChatGPT's agent mode is paid-plans only, so the trial there is a month of a subscription you may already have.

Which route is safest for a beginner? The one where the agent drafts and you approve. Start with reading, summarizing, and drafting jobs on any route, and only hand over sending or changing things once you have watched a few weeks of good behaviour.

What if my job needs two routes? That is normal. Many people end up with an agent mode for thinking work and one or two automations for the fixed chores. Start with the route that covers the job you named first; add the second only when a real job demands it.
