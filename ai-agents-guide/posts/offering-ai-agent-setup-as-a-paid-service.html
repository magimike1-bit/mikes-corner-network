Offering AI agent setup as a paid service: what to sell and what to avoid

Money · 10 min read

US$297 a month buys an agency ten client accounts on one white-label platform. US$10,000 is the top of the setup-fee range quoted in one agency pricing framework. Both numbers are real, published, and almost useless on their own — because neither one says what the client is actually paying for, or what it costs you to deliver it. This guide works through the version of this business that survives contact with the math: one job, sold as a fixed monthly fee, with the platform cost and your hours counted before the revenue.

General information only — not financial, business, or tax advice. Prices below were checked on October 25, 2026 and platforms change them without notice; confirm any price before quoting it to a client. Revenue figures are labelled scenarios, not predictions.

Step one: pick one job, not a technology

Clients pay for three things: a job done every week, one person to call when it breaks, and a result they can point at. They ignore the model, the stack, and the prompt library behind it. That finding, from an agency operator's account of what clients pay for and what they ignore, decides the whole shape of the offer. You are not selling "AI automation." You are selling one recurring job — lead follow-up, inbox triage, appointment reminders — done reliably, with you as the person who answers when it stops.

One job also caps your support burden. Every additional role you bolt onto the offer is another failure mode you personally get called about. Pick the job you can operate half-asleep, in a niche whose buyers you can actually reach.

Step two: write the offer as one sentence and a fixed fee

The operator's rule is blunt: sell the deployment and the operation, not the package, and quote a fixed monthly fee rather than hours. The one-sentence test: "For [type of business], [the agent] does [the job] every [week/day], and the result you see is [the visible outcome] — for $X a month, including me keeping it running." If the sentence needs the words "AI-powered platform" to make sense, it is describing the package, and packages are what clients ignore.

A fixed fee matters for a second reason. Hourly billing pays you less as you get faster at the job. A fixed fee for a defined job pays the same as your operation improves — and it forces you to define the job tightly enough that "operation" has edges.

Step three: run the platform math before you quote

Here is the cost side, from published pricing checked October 25, 2026. DM Champ's Agency plan is US$297 a month for ten client accounts, with an Unlimited plan at US$497 a month; the same pricing page suggests charging each client a flat US$200 to US$500 a month, and notes annual billing at half price. A separate agency framework prices the productized version higher: setup fees of US$1,000 to US$10,000 with monthly retainers of US$300 to US$1,000, and one documented model in it shows US$5,000 in monthly recurring revenue against US$380 in software and hosting costs.

Read the first row twice. At a US$300 fee, your first client covers the platform and leaves US$3 — before a single hour of your setup, support, or sales time. The platform is not the business's real cost; your hours are. So the honest break-even question is not "how many clients cover US$297" (one, barely) but "how many clients cover the platform plus my hours at a rate I would accept as wages." Put a number on your hour before you quote, multiply it by the setup and monthly support hours one client honestly takes, and treat any fee below that total as a discount you are choosing to give — not as revenue.

The framework's setup-fee model is the other pole: a four- or five-figure setup fee makes month one profitable on paper, but it raises the buyer's expectations to match, and the delivery risk sits entirely on you. A solo seller's safer sequence is a modest or waived setup on the first one or two clients — priced openly as a founding discount — in exchange for a reference and a hard look at your real delivery hours, then setup fees once those hours are known numbers instead of guesses.

Steps four and five: deploy a trial month, then operate it

  1. Set up the one job on the client's accounts. Connect only the tools the job touches, run it on real but low-stakes volume first, and agree in writing what the visible result is — the report, the inbox state, the follow-ups sent — so "it works" is checkable rather than a feeling.
  2. Run a defined trial month. Fixed end date, the full fee or a stated trial fee, and a review at the end against the agreed result. A trial with no end date is a free client.
  3. Convert to the managed fee. The monthly fee buys the operation: monitoring, fixes, adjustments as the client's business changes, and you as the one person to call. Say explicitly what is not included — new jobs are new offers, quoted separately.
  4. Review your hours monthly. Compare hours per client against the fee. The client whose quiet support load eats the margin of three others is a repricing conversation, not a badge of loyalty.

What success looks like: every client can name the job and the result, your fee covers platform plus hours at your stated rate, and no client's operation depends on you remembering something instead of a written procedure.

What to avoid selling

The package itself. Reselling access to a kit, a prompt library, or a platform login sells the thing clients demonstrably ignore — and leaves you supporting a product you did not build and cannot fix. Sell the deployment and the operation; the platform stays your tool, not the product.

"AI automation" in general. An undefined offer produces undefined work. Every vague engagement becomes custom development at a flat-fee price, which is the worst cell in the pricing table.

Open-ended custom builds as a first offer. The US$1,000-to-US$10,000 setup range exists in the framework for productized services with defined delivery. A bespoke build for a first client, priced before you know your delivery hours, converts your learning curve into the client's discount and your unpaid month.

Roles you cannot support. You are the one person to call. A job whose failure you cannot diagnose — or whose failure at the wrong hour costs the client real money — is a liability wearing a retainer.

High setup fees with no operation attached. A large setup fee for a handover-and-goodbye install charges most for the part clients value least and abandons the part they pay monthly for. If the recurring job is not in the offer, the fee is guru pricing, whatever the invoice calls it.

Who should not do this

Skip this business if you cannot be reachable when a client's job breaks, if you need the income this month (the first rows of the table above are the early business, and they are thin), or if your plan depends on the platform never changing its prices or terms. Platform dependence is structural here: your cost base, and part of your delivery, sits on a vendor you do not control, so keep the operation documented well enough to move it.

The kill criterion, set before client two

Decide the stop line now, while it is cheap. A workable version: give the offer 90 days from your first outreach. If, by that date, you do not have at least two clients paying a fee that covers the platform cost plus your logged hours at the hourly rate you set in step three, stop selling it — reprice the offer around the hours you actually measured, narrow it to the one job that ran cleanest, or close it and keep the lessons. What you do not do is sign client three at the same numbers and call the volume a strategy. The math table does not improve with hope; it improves with price, scope, or an exit.
