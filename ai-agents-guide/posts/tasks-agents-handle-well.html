The kinds of jobs agents are good at

Basics · 6 min read

Agents are sold as tools that can do anything. In practice, they're excellent at some jobs, mediocre at others, and genuinely bad at a few. Knowing which is which is the single biggest factor in whether using an agent feels magical or maddening. This guide gives you the honest map.

Where agents genuinely shine

Agents are at their best when the job is mostly made of language and the cost of a mistake is low. Five patterns cover most of the wins:

1. Research and information gathering

"Find five beginner-friendly kayak models under a reasonable price and compare their features." An agent can search, pull details from product pages, and assemble a comparison table in minutes — work that would take you an afternoon of tabs. The key word is assemble: you still need to verify the facts before buying anything (see checking an agent's work), but the legwork gets dramatically faster.

2. Drafting and writing first versions

Emails, meeting agendas, product descriptions, cover letters, lesson plans — anything where starting from a blank page is the hard part. An agent can produce a decent first draft in seconds, and editing a draft is much faster than writing one from scratch. Be honest about your own voice, though: generic AI writing is easy to spot, so plan to rewrite in your own words.

3. Summarizing long material

Long articles, meeting transcripts, reports, threads. "Give me the five key decisions from this document" is one of the most reliable things you can ask an agent to do. It saves real time, and the result is easy to check against the source because the source is right there.

4. Organizing and reformatting

Messy notes into a clean list. A list into a table. A table into a summary. Agents are excellent at reshaping information without changing its meaning — turning raw input into something structured and scannable. Expense receipts into categories, bullet points into a meeting summary, scattered ideas into an outline.

5. Comparisons and decision support

"Compare these three insurance quotes and tell me what differs." The agent doesn't make the decision for you — it lays out the trade-offs so the decision is easier. This is a great use because it plays to the agent's strength (reading and contrasting text) while keeping the judgment call with you.

Where agents are only so-so

The middle zone is real work you can give an agent, but with extra care:

  • Creative work with a specific vision. Agents can generate ideas and variations endlessly, but they can't tell which one is good. You become the taste-maker; the agent is a brainstorming partner.
  • Technical explanations. Agents explain things fluently, which makes wrong explanations sound convincing. Use them to learn, but verify anything you'll act on.
  • Multi-step projects. "Plan my move" involves dates, quotes, logistics, and judgment calls. An agent can draft the plan and gather quotes, but it can't manage the messy middle — that's still your job.

Where agents fall flat

Be blunt with yourself about these, because this is where the real damage happens:

  • Up-to-date facts. Prices, laws, schedules, and current events change constantly. Agents can search the web, but they can also mix old and new information with total confidence. Never trust an agent on something time-sensitive without checking the source.
  • Math and precise calculations. Agents can do arithmetic, but they also make arithmetic mistakes with complete confidence. For anything that matters — money, dosages, measurements — use a calculator or spreadsheet and check.
  • Anything irreversible. Sending money, publishing to an audience, deleting files, contacting people on your behalf. The rule: an agent should prepare, but you should press the button. Every time.
  • Judgment calls with real stakes. Hiring, medical questions, legal questions, anything where being wrong has consequences. An agent can help you understand the landscape, but it cannot take responsibility for the decision — because there is nobody home to be responsible.

The simple rule of thumb

Before handing a task to an agent, ask two questions: Is this mostly language? and What happens if it's wrong? If the answers are "yes" and "not much," you're in the sweet spot. If the task involves real money, real people, or real consequences, keep the agent in a supporting role — researcher, drafter, organizer — and keep yourself as the decision-maker.
