AI Affiliate Commission Tiers Explained: When a Higher Rate Actually Starts Paying

Guides · Published 2026-10-25 · Last updated 2026-10-25

Almost every AI affiliate roundup quotes two numbers for a tiered program: the rate a beginner earns and the rate a top affiliate can earn. What those roundups rarely explain is the machinery between the two numbers — what gets counted toward the next tier, which customers the higher rate actually applies to, and what an affiliate must do to keep it. Those three rules, not the headline percentage, decide whether a tier is a realistic goal or a decoration. A 50% top rate that requires 201 cumulative paid sign-ups is a different offer from a 30% rate that requires 100 leads and 100 conversions inside a rolling year, and both are different again from a program that advertises up to 50% without publishing its thresholds at all. This guide verifies five real tier structures against official program terms, checked on 2026-10-25, and turns them into a decision framework for the only question that matters: is chasing the next tier worth more than spending the same effort elsewhere?

A tier is three rules, not one rate

The first rule is the counting basis: the unit that moves an affiliate upward. ManyChat counts cumulative lifetime paid sign-ups — every qualifying paid conversion an affiliate has ever produced adds to one running total. Jasper counts two things at once inside a moving window: an affiliate must both generate 100 Affiliate Leads and convert 100 of them to Customers within a rolling 12-month period. Surfer counts referrals in bands — 0 to 10, 11 to 50, and 51 or more — and the band changes the commission on the referral’s first purchase. Same word, tier; three completely different efforts.

The second rule is scope: which sales the higher rate covers once it is reached. Jasper’s agreement is explicit that the increased 30% commission applies for the following 12 months to all net new Customers — it is a rate on future sales, earned by past performance, not a retroactive uplift on sales already made. ManyChat’s terms describe a progressive structure based on the cumulative count, with commission applying to a referral’s payments during their first 12 months, and with upgrades recalculated from the month of the upgrade and downgrades reducing commission proportionally. Where a program’s terms do not state whether a new rate reaches back to earlier referrals, that silence is itself a finding: do not model income on a retroactive reading the terms never promised.

The third rule is expiry and re-earning. A cumulative lifetime basis, like ManyChat’s, has no stated re-earning requirement in its tier table — the count only grows. A rolling-window basis, like Jasper’s, must be re-earned: the agreement states that to continue receiving the increased commission after the first 12 months at the higher rate, the affiliate must have met both milestones again in the preceding 12 months. A tier that must be re-earned is a treadmill set to a specific speed; a tier that accumulates is a staircase. Promoters should know which one they are standing on before they plan content around the higher rate.

The arithmetic of a tier jump

Tier jumps feel dramatic in percentage points and are often modest in money, because the higher rate usually applies only to sales made after the jump. Work the arithmetic on a deliberately plain illustration: suppose referrals generate $1,000 of commissionable subscription payments in a month. At ManyChat’s Gold rate of 30%, that month produces $300 of commission; at the Sapphire rate of 40%, the same payments produce $400; at Diamond’s 50%, $500. The jump from Gold to Sapphire is therefore worth $100 per $1,000 of monthly referral payments — real money, but money that scales with the sales base, not with the effort of crossing the threshold itself. An affiliate whose referrals generate $200 of monthly payments gains $20 a month from the same tier jump, which may be less than the value of the hours spent chasing the last few sign-ups to cross it.

Jasper’s jump is narrower and its gate is wider. The rate rises by 5 percentage points, from 25% to 30% — worth $50 per $1,000 of commissionable subscription price — but reaching it requires both 100 leads and 100 conversions in a rolling year, and keeping it requires repeating both. Surfer’s jumps are the steepest in this set in percentage terms — from 75% to 125% of a first monthly payment across its bands — but the commission is one-time, on the first purchase only, so the tier multiplies a single payment per referral rather than a year of payments. Comparing tier generosity without comparing duration is the most common modelling error in this category: 50% for 12 months of a customer’s payments and 125% of one month’s payment can rank in either order depending on the plan price and how long customers stay, and neither ranking is visible from the percentages alone.

Second tiers are a different animal

GoHighLevel’s structure deserves separate treatment because its second tier is not a higher rate on the affiliate’s own sales at all. The direct rate is a flat 40% on its listed recurring products; the additional 5% is paid on qualified purchases made through affiliates the first affiliate recruits, and only if the company approves that second-tier designation — approval it may deny, revoke, or modify at its discretion. That makes the second tier a recruiting income stream with an approval condition, not a volume reward. It also carries the program’s ordinary gates: a customer must remain in good standing for at least 45 days for a purchase to qualify, and payments may be held until commissions exceed $50, with commissions that do not reach $50 within 120 days at risk of forfeiture. A promoter who dislikes recruiting should value this tier at zero when comparing programs, whatever its percentage, because income that depends on a behaviour the promoter will not perform is not income.

Should you chase the next tier? A decision framework

Chase the tier when three conditions hold together: the counting basis is published and countable from the affiliate dashboard; the remaining distance to the threshold is small relative to the sales already being produced each month; and the higher rate applies to a duration that matters — a year of recurring payments, as at ManyChat and Jasper, rather than a single first payment. In that situation the tier is a genuine multiplier on work already underway.

Let the tier arrive on its own when the basis accumulates automatically, as a cumulative lifetime count does, and the current content plan is already producing steady referrals. The staircase climbs without extra effort, and diverting content toward threshold-chasing usually means producing weaker-fit referrals — the kind that cancel early, end commission eligibility, and, at programs with good-standing rules such as Jasper’s 30-day requirement, can forfeit the commission entirely.

Discount the tier to its starting rate when the thresholds are unpublished, when the higher rate must be re-earned inside a rolling window the current volume cannot sustain, or when the tier is a second-tier recruiting rate the promoter does not intend to use. Planning at the verified starting rate keeps forecasts honest; a tier reached later then improves real income instead of merely meeting an inflated one.

Compare across programs on the same basis before switching effort: rate, duration, counting basis, and re-earning rule, all four, from the official terms, with a check date. A program switch that trades a near-tier recurring rate for a higher headline one-time rate is frequently a pay cut wearing a bigger percentage.

FAQ

Does a higher affiliate tier apply to sales made before reaching it?

Read the scope rule. Jasper’s agreement states the increased rate applies to net new Customers during the following 12 months, not retroactively. Where terms are silent on retroactivity, do not assume it — model the higher rate on future sales only.

What is the difference between a cumulative tier and a rolling tier?

A cumulative tier counts everything to date, as ManyChat’s lifetime paid sign-up count does, so progress is not lost. A rolling tier counts performance inside a moving window, as Jasper’s 12-month lead and conversion milestones do, and must be re-earned to keep the higher rate.

An affiliate page says “up to 50%” but lists no thresholds. What rate should a plan assume?

The published starting rate. Colossyan, for example, publishes a 25% starting commission and rates up to 50% without publishing the tier thresholds on its program page, so a forecast built on 50% would rest on conditions that cannot be verified from public terms.

Is a second-tier commission the same as a higher tier?

No. GoHighLevel’s 5% second-tier rate is paid on purchases made through recruited affiliates, subject to approval, while its direct rate stays at 40%. It rewards recruiting, not the affiliate’s own sales volume.
