Disclose Referral Links Everywhere: Social, Email, Video, and DMs Done Right

You already know referral links need disclosing. The failures happen in the placement: the disclosure sits on a policy page while the link sits in an email, or hides at the end of a caption nobody reaches. Disclosure is a per-message job, and each platform has its own version of 'right there, before the link'.

Published 2026-10-19 · Last updated 2026-10-19

The rule underneath all the platform rules

Disclosure law for referral and affiliate links rests on one idea: if a connection between you and a product — a commission, a bonus, free access, any benefit — might change how much weight a reader gives your recommendation, and they would not naturally expect it, they have to be told, clearly, where they will actually see it, before the link does its work. Regulators express this through endorsement guidance applied to the underlying consumer-protection law, which is why “the guides are not themselves the law” is a technicality that helps nobody who ignores them.

Two consequences follow that promoters routinely miss. First, there is no small-account exemption: the requirement does not switch on at some follower count or traffic level, and a five-dollar commission triggers it exactly as a five-thousand-dollar one does. Second, a disclosure page is not a disclosure. Keeping a policy page on your site is good practice and still required as a foundation — but it does not substitute for a disclosure in the specific post, email, video, or message carrying the link. Both layers, every time.

Placement, platform by platform

Where the disclosure goes, by channel
ChannelPlacementWorkable wording
Blog / articleAt the top, before the first affiliate link; mark individual links where only some are affiliate“This article contains referral links. If you sign up through them, I may earn a bonus at no extra cost to you.”
Social postAt the start of the post or caption — #ad or “Ad:” first, not buried in a hashtag block at the end“Ad: I earn a referral bonus if you join through this link — here's why I use it…”
Short video (Reels/TikTok)Spoken in the first seconds AND as an on-screen overlay that stays visible, plus #ad at the start of the caption; use the branded-content toggle too“Quick disclosure: this is a paid partnership / I earn a bonus from this link.”
Longer video (YouTube)Stated verbally in the intro, written in the description above the fold, and the platform's paid-promotion checkbox ticked“Some links in the description are referral links — I earn a commission if you buy through them.”
EmailAt the top of the email, before any affiliate content; label sponsored sections as Sponsored“Disclosure: this email contains a referral link I may earn a bonus from.”
Podcast / audioSpoken at the start of the episode and again at the recommendation itself; written version in the show notes“Before we start: I'm an affiliate for the tool I'm about to mention.”
DMs and private messagesIn the message itself, before or with the link — private does not mean exempt“Full disclosure, that's my referral link, so I'd earn a bonus if you join.”

Read the pattern across the rows: the disclosure travels with the recommendation, in the same medium (spoken for audio, visible for visual, both for video), early enough that nobody can consume the pitch and miss the interest behind it. If a placement requires the reader to click, scroll, expand, or visit another page, it is not a placement — it is a hiding place.

The private-message trap

AI-tool referrals happen disproportionately in private: a friend asks what you use, a community member DMs for your workflow, a client gets a recommendation over chat. It feels like conversation, not publishing — but the test is not the medium, it is whether a compensated recommendation was made to someone who did not know about the compensation. Guidance on this point is explicit that disclosure applies in private conversations too, in the message, in plain words. It costs one clause — “that's my referral link, I'd earn a bonus” — and it converts the most trust-rich channel you have from a compliance risk into proof you recommend honestly even when nobody could check.

Disclosure does not kill the recommendation

The fear underneath most non-disclosure is conversion: say it and people will not click. Compliance guides collecting the evidence report the opposite pattern — transparent disclosure tends to hold or build trust, and the conversions it costs were the ones relying on the reader being misled about why you were recommending. There is also a harder arithmetic to keep in view: regulators publish per-violation penalty figures in the tens of thousands of dollars and run spot checks driven by complaints. One undisclosed campaign, screenshotted by one annoyed reader, is the most expensive “conversion tactic” a small promoter can run. Write the disclosure like you mean it, put it where the link lives, and let the recommendation earn its click honestly.

Questions promoters ask about disclosure

Is “#affiliate” in a pile of hashtags enough? No — placement is the point. At the start of the post or caption, in words a normal reader will register before they reach the link.

Do I disclose if the bonus is credit, not cash? Yes. The test is a benefit that might affect credibility, and credit, free months, and free products all qualify.

One disclosure per account, or per post? Per piece of content carrying the link. Your policy page is the foundation; each post, email, video, and message still carries its own.

Keep the evidence, not just the habit

One practice separates promoters who can prove compliance from promoters who merely practised it: keep dated copies of what you published. A screenshot of the post with its disclosure, the email as sent, the video description as it stood — saved in a folder named by campaign and month. It takes thirty seconds per piece of content, and it converts any future question, from a platform, a program, or a regulator, from an argument about memory into a document you can simply hand over. Programs that monitor their promoters — and the reputable ones state in their terms that they do — treat a promoter with records as a professional and a promoter with excuses as a risk.

Make the habit structural rather than heroic. Add the disclosure line to your post templates, your email template, and your video description boilerplate, so the default state of everything you publish is disclosed and removing it takes more effort than keeping it. Put the per-platform table from this article wherever you draft content. Once a quarter, search your own recent posts for your referral links and read the three nearest lines around each one: that is exactly what a spot check does, and doing it to yourself first is the cheapest audit in the world. Disclosure done this way stops being a legal chore you perform nervously and becomes what it actually is — a visible, repeatable proof that your recommendations were never for sale in secret.
