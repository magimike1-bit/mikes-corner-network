Referral Program Terms: The Fine Print That Decides What You Actually Earn

Guides · Published 2026-10-21 · Last updated 2026-10-21

Two programs can advertise the same headline - 'earn $25 per referral' - and pay out completely differently, because the headline is the only clause most people read. The money lives in the clauses after it: what counts as a qualified referral, how long the reward sits pending, whether a refund claws it back, the cap nobody mentions, and the expiry date that quietly deletes unspent credit. This guide names the seven clauses that change what you actually earn, in the order to check them, each illustrated from a real program's published terms checked on 2026-10-21. Terms change often; treat every example as a reading lesson, and re-check the live terms before relying on any figure.

Clause 1 and 2: qualification, and the hold before you can spend

Qualification defines the event that earns anything at all, and it is rarely 'they clicked your link.' In Breakout's published terms, a qualified referral is a referred friend who completes identity (KYC) verification - sign-up alone earns nothing. Other programs qualify on a signed order plus a first paid invoice, or on the referred customer staying past a threshold period. If your audience clicks eagerly but rarely completes verification or payment, a modest bounty on a completed action beats a generous one on an action nobody finishes.

The hold is the gap between earning and having. Breakout creates credit in a pending state, held for review for 14 days before it becomes spendable. Elementor grants credits monthly, and only after confirming the referred payment sits outside its 30-day refund window. Holds exist to let refunds and fraud checks happen first - which means a program's cash-flow reality is 'next month, if nothing was refunded,' whatever the banner says.

Clause 3 and 4: caps, and the one-per-period trap

Caps come in three shapes: per period, per referral, and per customer. Breakout caps credit at $500 per referrer per calendar month - twenty qualified referrals - after which further referrals that month earn nothing. ReferralHero caps the commission per referral at $700 or twelve months of payments, whichever arrives first, which converts an apparently open-ended recurring commission into a fixed maximum. Neither shape is dishonest; both are invisible until you read clause four of a terms page most promoters never open.

The sharpest variant is the one-per-period rule: Bitrise's terms make each customer eligible to receive and redeem only one coupon per 12-month period, regardless of how many new customers they referred. A promoter who sends five customers in a year and a promoter who sends one receive the same single coupon. Programs with no cap exist too - at least one published program states there is no upper limit on referrals - which is exactly why the cap clause, not the headline rate, should drive which programs a heavy promoter pushes.

Clause 5, 6, and 7: expiry, lock-in, and the moving floor

Expiry varies by an order of magnitude: Breakout credit dies 30 days after it becomes spendable - a genuinely short fuse that punishes anyone who lets credit accumulate - while Elementor and Bitrise both allow twelve months from issue. Transferability decides whether a reward is money or a discount on your own next bill: multiple programs state plainly that credit is non-transferable and cannot be exchanged for cash, and Breakout credit cannot be combined with a discount code on the same purchase. Price a locked credit at what you would genuinely have spent, not at face value.

Finally, the change clause: Bitrise and ReferralHero both reserve the right to modify or terminate the program or its terms at any time, in ReferralHero's case expressly without prior notice. Assume every program you promote can reprice. The practical defence is boring and effective: keep a dated copy of the terms for each program you actively promote, and re-read the cap and expiry clauses quarterly.

A ten-minute reading procedure for any new program

  1. Find the program's own terms page - not a review, not the landing page banner.
  2. Highlight the qualification sentence. Write down the exact action that earns.
  3. Find the hold and the refund language. Write the earliest date a reward becomes usable.
  4. Search the page for 'maximum,' 'cap,' 'limit,' and 'per' - record every ceiling, including one-per-period rules.
  5. Search for 'expire' and 'valid for.' Record the deadline and set a calendar reminder before it.
  6. Check transferability and combinability: cash or locked credit, and whether it stacks with discounts.
  7. Note the change clause, save a dated copy of the page, and only then compare the headline rate against other programs.

What success looks like: for each program you promote, a one-line record - earns on X, usable after Y, capped at Z, expires W, credit locked or cash - that lets you rank programs by what a realistic month actually pays, instead of by the largest number on a banner.

How the clauses stack in one realistic month

The clauses stop being abstract when they compound, so run one honest hypothetical. A promoter sends twelve referred customers to two programs in a month. In the first program, qualification requires a completed paid action, the reward sits in a pending hold for two weeks, one referred customer refunds inside the window and that reward is never issued, and the monthly cap quietly trims the top of the earnings - the promoter ends the month with roughly two-thirds of the headline arithmetic, arriving weeks later than the banner implied. In the second program, there is no monthly cap and no one-per-period rule, but the credit expires thirty days after becoming spendable and can only be spent with that provider - a promoter who was saving credit toward a large purchase watches part of it lapse, and the 'earnings' were only ever real if the promoter genuinely intended to spend that money there anyway.

Neither program cheated anyone; every term was published. The difference in outcome was created entirely at reading time, weeks before the first link was shared. That is the practical argument for the checklist in this guide: ten minutes with the terms page, before promotion, is worth more than a month of optimizing posts for a program whose cap, hold, and expiry were always going to decide the result. Keep the one-line record per program, update it when terms change, and let programs compete on what your record says they pay - not on what their banners say they might.

FAQ

What is the most commonly missed clause in referral terms?

The cap, in its three shapes: per month, per referral, and one-per-period. A program can pay generously per referral and still stop paying a productive promoter almost immediately.

How long do referral credits usually last before expiring?

Published examples span from 30 days after the credit becomes spendable to 12 months from issue. There is no standard - read the expiry clause for each program and set a reminder before the deadline.

Can a program change its terms after I join?

Commonly, yes. Major programs reserve the right to modify or terminate the program or its terms at any time, sometimes expressly without notice. Save a dated copy of the terms when you join and re-check quarterly.

Is referral credit the same as cash?

Often not. Many programs issue non-transferable credit that cannot be exchanged for cash and may not combine with discount codes. Value locked credit at what you would actually have spent with that provider.
