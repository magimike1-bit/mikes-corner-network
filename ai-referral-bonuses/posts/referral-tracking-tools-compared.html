Referral Tracking Tools Compared: Dashboards, Link Managers, and Spreadsheets

Guides · Published 2026-11-02 · Last updated 2026-11-02

Short answer: the three options in the title are not rivals. They do different jobs. A spreadsheet records what you decided and what you are owed. A link manager controls the links themselves, shortening, organising, and labelling them. A program dashboard is the only record that decides pay, because attribution and approval live with the program, not with you. Most comparison pages blur those layers into one list of tools, which is how a promoter ends up buying a platform to solve a labelling problem, or trusting a spreadsheet to settle an attribution dispute it cannot see.

Three layers, three different tools

The record layer. A basic referral tracking spreadsheet suits a small operation, per a referral tracking guide checked on 2026-10-25, and its core columns are simple: referrer, referred, date, status, and notes. That is a ledger. It holds what you recorded, in the form you recorded it, and nothing updates it except you.

The click layer. Link managers sit between your posts and the program link. Their job is link hygiene: one consistent address per placement, labels you can read at a glance, and a count of clicks on your side of the fence. They say nothing about whether a click became a payable referral.

The attribution layer. The program dashboard holds the states that matter for money: clicked, signed up, pending, approved, paid, rejected. A comparison of referral platforms on SourceForge, also checked on 2026-10-25, describes tools at this layer in different terms entirely. RefRef is described as an open source referral platform with personalised links, attribution models, fraud prevention, and automated reward disbursement. Trackdesk is described as an affiliate and referral management platform built for scale, with transparent pricing. Those are program operator tools. A promoter of other companies’ programs does not buy attribution; the program already owns it.

The spreadsheet, done properly

A spreadsheet fails in predictable ways. A referral app versus spreadsheet comparison from GetTheReferral, checked on 2026-10-25, names them: typos, outdated entries, version conflicts, and single editor limits, set against what an app adds, namely a centralised database, role based access, and real time notifications. The same comparison puts reporting in two camps: static charts and manual summaries from a sheet, against dynamic dashboards from an app. None of that makes a sheet wrong at low volume. It makes an unstructured sheet wrong at any volume.

Set one up as a ledger, not a notebook:

  1. Start with the core columns: referrer, referred, date, status, and notes, the set the tracking guide above uses for a basic sheet.
  2. Add the money columns: program name, the exact link or placement that sent the referral, the amount the dashboard shows, and the dashboard state, copied word for word (pending, approved, paid, or rejected).
  3. Reconcile on a fixed day each week. Open every program dashboard, update each status, and flag any row where your sheet and the dashboard disagree. The dashboard wins; fix the sheet.
  4. Freeze a copy each month. Save a dated copy before editing the new month, so a typo or a bad paste cannot rewrite history you may need during a dispute.

What success looks like: pick any paid referral at random and you can trace it in under a minute, from the post that sent it, to the dashboard state changes, to the payout it arrived in, using only the sheet and the dashboard.

Link managers: the click layer

A link manager earns its place when the links themselves become the problem: dozens of placements, the same program promoted in a bio, a video description, and three posts, with no reliable way to tell which placement produced a click. That is a labelling and counting job on your side. Pair it with the tagging discipline covered in this site’s UTM guide and the click layer can rank your placements honestly. What it cannot do is confirm a sign up, apply a cookie window, or approve a payout. Those decisions stay in the dashboard, which is why a link manager replaces a messy bookmarks folder, not a reconciliation habit.

Dedicated platforms: what the money actually buys

The dedicated tools named in the research, ReferralCandy, Referral Rock, Yotpo, and HubSpot CRM among them, sell automation to people who run programs: notifications when events happen, payout handling, and fraud checks. The GetTheReferral comparison makes the trade plainly. Apps replace manual summaries with dynamic dashboards and replace a single editor sheet with role based access. For a promoter, that automation mostly duplicates what each program dashboard already does for free. A platform earns its cost for a promoter in one narrower case: when you run your own referral scheme on your own product, and payouts and fraud checks are suddenly your bottleneck rather than a program manager’s.

The switching rule: when to move up a layer

No source in the research states a volume threshold, so treat the lines below as this guide’s decision framework, not as industry data. Stay on a spreadsheet while one weekly reconciliation sitting covers every program you promote and the sheet still matches the dashboards. Add a link manager when you can no longer tell, from your own records, which placement sent a click, or when the same program appears in more placements than you can label consistently by hand. Consider a dedicated platform only when you operate a program yourself and manual payout or fraud review is consuming real hours each month. Notice what is absent from that rule: a referral count at which a spreadsheet becomes wrong. Sheets do not break at a number. They break at the moment reconciliation stops happening, and that moment is visible in your own calendar long before it shows in your income.
