Two-Tier Referral Commissions: What the Second Tier Actually Pays

Guides · Published 2026-11-10 · Last updated 2026-11-10

$5 against $40. On a $100 subscription payment under GoHighLevel’s published affiliate terms, checked in November 2026, the affiliate who made the sale earns $40 and the affiliate who recruited that affiliate earns $5. Both numbers come from the same official policy page, on the same product categories. That ratio is the whole subject of this article. A second tier exists, it is real money, and it is structurally small, conditional in ways the headline rate hides. Read the conditions before spending an hour recruiting anyone.

Who should not recruit anyone

Skip the recruiting link entirely if any of these describe the offer in front of you. The program pays something for a recruit merely joining, before that recruit sells anything. Your honest list of likely recruits is people who will join but probably never promote the product. Or the second tier is the reason you are joining, and the product itself is not something you would promote on its direct rate alone.

The kill criterion, stated before any recruiting starts: if your recruits produce no qualifying sales in a period when your own content produces direct sales, stop recruiting and put the hours back into direct promotion. Recruiting effort that produces sign-ups but no sales produces, under every published structure checked for this article, exactly nothing.

The two ways a second tier is calculated

The phrase “second-tier commission” covers two different calculations, and confusing them overstates the money by a wide margin. In the first, the override is a percentage of the sale. GoHighLevel’s policy is the worked example in this site’s directory: 40% on direct referrals and 5% on second-tier referrals, across the same categories, which include its Single Location, Agency Unlimited, and Agency Pro (SaaS) accounts. In the second, the override is a percentage of the recruit’s commission. OpenPartner’s two-tier documentation defines its share that way: a percent of what the recruit earns, paid on top of that commission, never taken out of it. At a 10% share, a recruit’s $50 commission earns the recruiter $5, the recruit keeps the full $50, and the sale costs the program $55 in total commissions.

One naming trap is worth clearing. A performance tier, where your own rate rises after you sell a set volume, is not a second tier. It pays on your sales, through one person. A two-tier structure pays across two people. The site’s commission tiers article covers the first; this article covers only the second.

The worked math on published terms

Take GoHighLevel’s terms as the scenario, with every figure traceable to the policy page. Your recruit generates $1,000 of subscription payments in a month. The recruit earns $400 at the 40% direct rate. You earn $50 at the 5% second-tier rate. That $50 is also, not coincidentally, the program’s payout threshold: the policy says payments may be held until earned commissions exceed $50.00 USD, and commissions that do not reach $50.00 within 120 days may be forfeited. A second-tier-only earner at $5 per $100 recruit sale needs ten such sales before anything is payable at all, and needs them inside the forfeiture window.

The matching comparison: one direct sale of $100 earns you $40. Matching that $40 from the second tier alone takes eight $100 sales by your recruits. None of this makes the second tier worthless. It makes it a by-product. It rewards introductions you would have made anyway, between people who genuinely sell. It does not support recruiting as the main activity, because the arithmetic does not.

Two more conditions sit between a recruit’s sale and your $5. The sale must first become a Qualified Purchase, which in this program means the customer clicked the affiliate link, completed a new purchase, and kept the account in good standing for at least 45 days, with commissions awarded on a last-click basis inside a 90-day window. And the second-tier relationship itself is not automatic: a second-tier affiliate is one you recruit if the company approves that designation, and the company keeps full discretion to approve, deny, revoke, or modify designations and to decide how second-tier commissions are calculated and paid.

How long the override lasts, and how it unwinds

Duration is a term, not a promise. Under the percent-of-commission structure OpenPartner documents, the share can carry an optional limit of 1 to 120 months counted from when each recruitment starts; left blank, it lasts as long as the recruit earns, and only the recruit’s commissions pay a share, never their bonuses or bounties. Under GoHighLevel’s policy, commissions are earned only while the referred customer maintains an active, paid subscription.

The unwinding is the part recruiters rarely read. OpenPartner documents that the recruiter’s share is approved in the same step as the recruit’s commission, carries no holdback of its own, and falls with it: a refund reduces both by the same proportion, and reversing the recruit’s commission reverses the share, or claws it back if it was already paid. GoHighLevel’s clawback clause reaches the same place from the other direction: no commission on a fraudulent or non-compliant sale, and amounts already paid may be deducted from future commissions when a customer cancels, receives a refund, or disputes the charge. Your second-tier income inherits every weakness in your recruit’s sales, including the ones you cannot see.

The FTC line, in plain terms

“Two-tier is not an MLM” is a slogan, not an analysis. The United States Federal Trade Commission’s business guidance on multi-level marketing gives the actual standard, drawn from its Koscot decision: an unlawful structure is one where participants pay for the right to sell a product and the right to receive, for recruiting other participants, rewards unrelated to the sale of the product to ultimate users. The guidance is explicit that there is no percentage-based test and no safe harbour. What matters is what the compensation plan incentivizes and the rights it offers, judged on how the structure operates in practice, including its marketing, its participants’ experiences, and the incentives it creates. A plan can sell real, high-quality products and still be a pyramid scheme.

Measured against that standard, the structures documented here sit on the safer side for one concrete reason: nothing is paid for a partner simply joining. The recruiter’s share comes only from commissions the recruit earns on their own referrals, which is payment tied to sales to customers. That is a fact about these published mechanics, not a clearance of any specific program. This section is general information about the guidance, not legal advice.

The pre-join checklist

  1. Which basis? Get the answer in writing: a percentage of the sale, or a percentage of the recruit’s commission. The same headline number is worth very different money under each.
  2. Is anything paid for joining alone? If yes, walk away. That is the fact pattern the FTC standard targets, and it is the kill criterion at the top of this article.
  3. Is the second tier automatic or approved? In the directory example it is a designation the company may approve, deny, or revoke at its discretion. Recruiting done before approval may earn nothing.
  4. How long does the override run? A fixed month limit, the life of the customer’s subscription, or as long as the recruit earns are three different assets. Price your effort accordingly.
  5. What unwinds it? Find the refund, reversal, and clawback clauses, and the payout threshold and forfeiture window. Small overrides die quietly at thresholds.
  6. Would you promote it at the direct rate alone? If not, the second tier is carrying a decision it cannot support. Five dollars against forty is a bonus on a sound direct offer, never a substitute for one.
