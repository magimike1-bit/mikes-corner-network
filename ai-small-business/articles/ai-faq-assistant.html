Build an AI FAQ Assistant for Your Website

You answer the same questions every week: your hours, your prices, whether you do walk-ins. Answer them once, feed them to an AI assistant, and let your website do the repeating for you.

What it is

An AI FAQ assistant is a search-and-answer box on your website built from your own questions and answers. Unlike a traditional FAQ page where visitors scroll through a long list, visitors type their question in plain words — "do you do kids' haircuts?" — and get the specific answer, written the way you wrote it.

The difference from a general chatbot is the scope: an FAQ assistant sticks strictly to the material you provided. It doesn't try to hold a conversation or guess at things you didn't cover; it retrieves your answers. That makes it simpler, safer, and easier to trust than an open-ended chat tool. Many website platforms and help-center tools now include this feature, and standalone FAQ assistants can be embedded with a small snippet of code.

Building one is mostly a writing exercise. The heavy lifting is collecting your real questions and writing clear, short answers. The AI part — matching a visitor's wording to the right answer — is handled by the tool. Most small businesses can get a working version live in an afternoon.

Who it's for

Businesses with a stable set of repeat questions: salons ("do you take walk-ins?"), clinics ("do you need a referral?"), trades ("what's your call-out fee?"), venues ("is parking included?"). If you can list your top 20 questions without thinking hard, you have the raw material.

Skip it if your questions change constantly, your answers are always "it depends" (custom quotes, legal advice), or your website gets almost no visitors — the assistant only helps people who actually land on your site. In those cases, a phone number and a good contact form matter more.

What it costs

Many website builders and help-desk tools include an AI answer feature in their standard plans, so you may already be paying for it. Standalone FAQ tools usually offer a free tier for a basic assistant, with paid plans often roughly in the $10–$40/month range — but check the vendor's current pricing before you commit, because it changes.

The main cost is your writing time: an hour or two to collect questions and write clean answers. If your existing FAQ page is already accurate and current, you're most of the way there. Keep the answers short and plain — two or three sentences each — because the assistant will quote them directly.

Target ROI: the honest math EXAMPLE

An FAQ assistant pays off by deflecting repetitive questions from your inbox, phone, and social messages. The math is simple because the cost is mostly one-time writing.

Example: Suppose staff answer the same 30 questions a month at 10 minutes each — 5 hours, or $125 of time at $25/hr. If the assistant handles half of them, that's ~$63/month freed against a $20/month tool — an example net of about $43/month. Small numbers, but the real win is fewer interrupted workdays; track interruptions before and after.

What to actually measure:

  • How many questions per week the assistant answers
  • Whether "same question" emails and messages drop after launch
  • Which questions get asked that you hadn't written an answer for
  • Whether visitors who use the assistant go on to book, call, or buy

How to set it up

  1. Mine your real questions. Go through the last month of emails, messages, and voicemails. Write down every question asked more than once. Aim for 20–40 to start; you can always add more.
  2. Write one clear answer per question. Two to three sentences, plain words, no jargon. Include specifics (prices, times, addresses) exactly as you want them quoted. If an answer has a "but," write the exception in too.
  3. Pick your tool. Check whether your website platform already has an AI FAQ or site-search feature — many do now. If not, choose a standalone FAQ assistant with a free tier that lets you paste in your Q&A list.
  4. Add your questions and answers. Paste them into the tool, one pair at a time or by uploading a document. Group them by topic (pricing, booking, policies) if the tool supports categories — it helps the matching.
  5. Write the "I don't know" fallback. Tell the tool what to say when there's no matching answer: "I don't have that answer yet — you can reach us at [phone/email] and we'll get back to you." This is more important than it sounds.
  6. Put it somewhere visible and test it. Add the search box to your homepage or contact page. Then test it yourself with misspellings, slang, and oddly worded questions to make sure it finds the right answers.
  7. Review the unanswered questions monthly. Most tools show you what visitors asked that got no good answer. Turn the common ones into new Q&A pairs. This is how the assistant gets better over time.

Watch-outs and honest limitations

An FAQ assistant is only as accurate as your answers, and businesses change: prices rise, hours shift, services get dropped. A stale answer quoted confidently is worse than no answer, so review your Q&A list quarterly and after any business change. The assistant also can't read between the lines — a visitor asking about "the thing on your sign" won't get anywhere, and frustrated visitors need a visible path to a human. Don't let the assistant replace your contact page; it should sit next to it. Finally, keep answers short. Long paragraphs get skimmed, and the tool may quote only part of a long answer, cutting off the important exception at the end.

What to measure in your first 30 days

  • Questions answered per week and the share with a matched answer versus the fallback
  • The 10 most-asked questions — compare them against your original list
  • Change in repetitive-question emails or messages versus the month before
  • Any wrong or incomplete answers reported by visitors
  • How many unanswered questions you converted into new Q&A pairs
