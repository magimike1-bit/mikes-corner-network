AI Product Descriptions for a Small Online Store: Drafts From Facts, Not From Thin Air

Every product in a small online store needs a description, and most stores have a backlog: new stock with a supplier's two-line blurb, older items with nothing at all. AI description tools remove the blank-page problem — a draft appears in seconds from a title and some keywords. The risk is equally fast: a fluent paragraph that invents a material, a dimension, or a benefit your product does not have. This guide uses a fact-sheet-first workflow, so the AI writes from verified product details you supply, and a human checks every claim before anything goes live.

What it is

Shopify's built-in tool is the reference point for how this works in practice. Shopify's help centre explains that Shopify Magic writes product descriptions using automatic text generation: it takes details you provide, such as a product title and keywords, and generates a suggested description. The workflow lives inside the product editor — from the Shopify admin you open Products, open the product, and click the Generate text icon in the description toolbar, then enter a prompt with the features, keywords, and tone you want. Shopify's own documentation is explicit that the more detail you add, the more relevant the suggestion — and that the generated text is a draft to edit and format to match your product and brand before you save.

That last clause is the whole game. A generator given only a product name will fill the gaps with plausible inventions, because producing fluent text is what it does. A generator given a verified fact sheet — materials, dimensions, weight, care instructions, what's in the box, who it is for, what it is not for — has nothing to invent: its job shrinks to arranging your facts into readable copy, in the tone you asked for, at the length you asked for. Every workflow decision in this guide follows from that distinction.

The same tool can refresh existing copy, not just draft new descriptions: Shopify Magic can rephrase, simplify, or extend a description you have already written, which makes it useful for the quieter problem in most catalogs — dozens of adequate-but-tired descriptions written in three different voices across three different years.

Who it's for

This fits a store with more products than writing hours: a catalog of fifty to five hundred items, regular new arrivals, seasonal refreshes, or variants that each need their own page text. It also fits sellers expanding a proven product into a second marketplace or language version, where each listing needs original copy rather than a copy-paste of the supplier's text that every competitor also uses.

It fits poorly for a store with twelve products that rarely change — writing those twelve descriptions properly by hand is a weekend, once, and the result will be better. It also fits poorly for products where a wrong claim carries real consequences: supplements, cosmetics with ingredient claims, children's products, anything electrical or safety-related. There, AI drafting belongs behind professional review, or nowhere.

What it costs

Prices checked 2026-10-13. For Shopify stores, the generator itself adds nothing: Shopify states that many of its AI features, including the text generation behind Shopify Magic, are available at no additional cost on its plans. The plan underneath it is the real cost — Shopify Basic is $29/month billed yearly or $39/month billed monthly in US pricing, as read from Shopify's pricing page on 2026-10-01. General AI assistants used outside a store platform typically run around $20/month for a paid tier, and dedicated bulk-description tools charge by catalog size or generation volume; both routes add a subscription to produce drafts you still have to verify line by line.

The comparison that matters is not tool versus tool; it is draft-plus-review versus writing from zero. Reviewing a grounded draft against its fact sheet takes minutes per product. Writing the same description from a blank page takes several times longer for most owners — and the gap, multiplied by your catalog size, is the entire economic case in the next section.

Target ROI: the honest math EXAMPLE

The return is owner writing time converted into review time. It is not a promised conversion lift — nicer copy may sell better, but this site does not count unmeasured lifts as ROI.

Example: A store adds or refreshes 20 descriptions a month. Writing one from scratch takes about 25 minutes — roughly 8 hours a month, about $200 at $25/hr. With a fact-sheet workflow, drafting is near-instant and careful review-plus-edit takes about 8 minutes per product: roughly 2.5–3 hours a month, about $70 of time, plus $0 for Shopify Magic on an existing Shopify plan (or about $20/month for a standalone assistant). Example net saving: roughly $110–$130 a month, every month, plus a catalog that stops accumulating blank descriptions. The same workflow on a 10-product catalog saves under an hour a month — which is why the who-it's-for section tells small catalogs to just write by hand.

What to actually measure:

  • Minutes per finished, published description, before and after
  • Factual errors caught in review per batch (your quality signal — if it is not near zero after grounding, the fact sheets are too thin)
  • Catalog coverage: share of live products with a complete, current description

How to set it up

  1. Build the fact sheet before any prompt. For each product, write down only verified facts: name, materials or ingredients, dimensions, weight, colour and size options, what's in the box, care or use instructions, ideal buyer, and one honest limitation. Source each fact from the product itself, its packaging, or the supplier's specification — never from a competitor's listing.
  2. Write one house prompt template. Fix the structure every description follows: opening line naming the product and its buyer, a short paragraph from the fact sheet, three to five bullet features (fact-sheet items only), and a closing line with the limitation or care note. Add your tone in words ("plain, warm, no exclamation marks") and a hard instruction: use only the facts provided, and invent nothing.
  3. Generate inside the product editor where you can. In Shopify: Products → open the product → click the Generate text icon in the description toolbar → paste the fact sheet and template as the prompt → generate. The more relevant detail in the prompt, the more relevant the draft, per Shopify's documentation.
  4. Review every draft against the fact sheet, line by line. Check each claim in the draft against a fact on the sheet. Any claim with no matching fact — a material, a measurement, a benefit, a compatibility — gets deleted, not softened. Then read once for voice: does it sound like your store, and would you say it to a customer in person?
  5. Work the catalog in revenue order, not alphabetical order. Refresh best sellers first, then products with traffic but thin copy, then the long tail in batches of ten. A batch rhythm — ten fact sheets, ten drafts, one review session — keeps quality consistent in a way one-off efforts do not.
  6. Keep the fact sheet with the product record. Save it in the product's files or your catalog spreadsheet. When a supplier changes a material or a dimension, the fact sheet is where you notice — and it tells you exactly which description now needs a refresh.
  7. Label AI-assisted copy in your feed where required. If you submit product data to Google Merchant Center or similar feeds, check the current rules for AI-generated text and images in that feed and follow them; platform requirements change, and "the tool did it automatically" is not a compliance strategy.

What success looks like: every live product carries a complete description written in one voice, you can point to the fact sheet behind any claim on any page, review takes minutes per product, and the error count in your review log trends to zero — because the drafts have nothing left to invent.

Watch-outs and honest limitations

Invented specifics are the standing risk, and independent coverage of these tools says so plainly: store owners are warned to check product details before publishing, because AI should not invent sizes, materials, prices, shipping details, or guarantees. An invented claim on a product page is not a style error — it is a misdescription that produces returns, complaints, and in regulated categories, legal exposure. The fact-sheet workflow exists precisely to make invention structurally difficult rather than merely discouraged.

Search engines are the second consideration, and Google's position is more specific than "AI is banned" or "AI is fine." Google's guidance on generative-AI content says AI output can contain inaccuracies because the models predict likely text rather than retrieving facts, and it asks site owners to fact-check and review AI-generated content before publishing — product copy included. Its scaled-content rules target large volumes of low-value pages however they were produced. Fifty grounded, reviewed descriptions are an asset; five hundred unreviewed near-duplicates are the pattern those rules exist for.

Finally, sameness. If every store in your niche generates from the same prompts, descriptions converge into identical mush and the pages stop giving shoppers a reason to buy from you. Your defence is the material only you have: the fact sheet's honest limitation, the use case your actual customers describe, the detail from handling the product yourself. Put those in the template and the copy stays yours even though a machine arranged the sentences.

What to measure in your first 30 days

  • Minutes per published description versus your hand-written baseline
  • Claims deleted in review per batch — and which fact-sheet fields keep getting missed
  • Share of the live catalog with complete, current descriptions
  • Returns or questions caused by description confusion (should fall, never rise)
  • Conversion on your top ten refreshed pages versus the prior month — noted as an observation, not claimed as proof until the pattern holds
