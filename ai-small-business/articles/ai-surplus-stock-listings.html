Selling Surplus Stock With AI Help: List It Faster, Price It From the Fees Up

13.6 percent. That is the share eBay takes from the total amount of most sales before you have paid for postage, packing, or a single minute of listing time, and it is the number surplus stock pricing usually ignores until the payout arrives. Price each item from its channel fees up, let AI draft the listings so the pile actually gets listed, and set the exit rule before the first photo is taken.

Published 2026-11-20 · Last updated 2026-11-20

The fee math comes first: what a $60 item actually nets

Surplus stock has already cost its money. What remains is a smaller, colder question: for each item, which channel returns the most cash after every fee, and how much of your time does that channel eat? The channels price differently, and the differences are large enough to choose the channel for you once they are written down. Fees below were checked on 2026-11-20.

On eBay, most categories carry a final value fee of 13.6 percent of the total amount of the sale, plus a per order fee of US$0.30 on orders of US$10 or less and US$0.40 above that. Total amount means the item price plus any shipping you collect from the buyer plus sales tax, not just the sticker price. The first 250 listings each month carry no insertion fee. On Facebook Marketplace, creating a listing costs nothing, a buyer who collects and pays in person never passes through Facebook checkout, and Meta's Seller Protection Policy sets the fee on shipped checkout orders at one tenth of the whole transaction, shipping and tax included, with a minimum of US$0.80. Selling in bulk to a liquidation marketplace such as B-Stock, which runs auctions of overstock and returns to verified buyers, skips per item fees and per item labour alike, and pays for that convenience in a lower return per item.

Run the same four lines with your own typical price before you list anything, and the floor price for each channel stops being a guess. This article is general information, not financial advice.

Pick the channel per item, not per pile

Sort the pile once, against three questions. Is it bulky, fragile, or cheap relative to postage? Local pickup wins, because postage and the fee on postage never enter the math. Is it small, shippable, and specific enough that a buyer somewhere is searching for exactly it, a part, a model, a size? eBay wins, because its search reach is what the 13.6 percent buys, and the first 250 listings a month cost nothing to post. Do you hold many units of the same thing, or a mixed pile of low value items where ten minutes of listing time each would eat the return? That is the bulk lot: one manifest, one negotiation, shelf space back this week. What is left, current stock in the wrong colour or season, can go through your own store at a discount or bundled with a popular line, the oldest surplus routes there are, with no new platform fee at all.

One more cost belongs in the sort: your minutes. A general AI assistant subscription that drafts descriptions from your notes and photos runs at US$20 per month for ChatGPT Plus, checked on 2026-11-20, and eBay's own AI listing tools are built into its app and listing flow. Either way, the subscription is not the expense that decides this. The expense is verification time, and the runbook below budgets for it instead of pretending the drafts are free.

The listing runbook: triage, photograph, draft, verify, publish

  1. Triage the pile into the four channels. Local pickup, eBay, bulk lot, own store markdown. Write the channel and the floor price on a tag for each item before any listing starts, using the fee math for that channel.
  2. Photograph each item once, properly. Front, back, label or model plate, and any flaw, in that order. These photos feed the listing, the AI draft, and the condition check, so one careful pass replaces three hurried ones.
  3. Draft from the photo. On eBay, its image based listing tool takes a photo in the app and fills in a title, a description, the category, and item specifics, and can suggest a listing price and shipping cost. Off eBay, give your assistant the photos and your tag notes and ask for a title and a description built only from what the photos and notes show.
  4. Verify every AI filled field against the item in hand. Model number, size, quantity in the pack, included accessories, and condition. Anything the draft states that the item does not confirm gets corrected or deleted. The suggested price is checked against your floor, never adopted because the tool offered it.
  5. Price from the floor up. Start at the floor price from step 1. Price above it only where the item is genuinely scarce or searched for. If the honest price and the floor are the same number, the item belongs in the bulk lot, not in a listing.
  6. Publish in batches, then stop. List in batches of ten, then return to running the business. Batching keeps listing time visible as a cost instead of letting it leak across the whole week.
  7. Review on a fixed day. Once a week, look at views, watchers, and messages per listing. Attention without a sale is information: the price or the channel is wrong, and the kill criterion below says what happens next.

What success looks like: every listed item carries a floor price worked out before listing, every AI filled field was checked against the physical item, and the weekly review takes minutes because the batch, the channel, and the exit date were decided up front.

What AI drafts get wrong, and the check that catches it

An AI listing draft is built by inference from a photo, and inference fails in predictable places. It can name the wrong model variant, invent an accessory that is not in the box, describe a used item in the language of a new one, or carry a suggested price that reflects a different product entirely. eBay's own reporting on its tool is instructive here: it says over 95 percent of sellers who tried the description feature used the generated text, including those who edited it first. Editing is the normal case, not the exception.

The check is mechanical and quick. Read the draft with the item in your other hand. Confirm each specific against the label, the contents, or a measurement you take yourself. Where the draft and the item disagree, the item wins and the draft gets corrected. Where the draft states something the item cannot confirm, such as a year, a material, or a compatibility claim, delete the claim rather than softening it. A shorter listing that is accurate outsells a rich listing that earns a return, and returns on surplus stock destroy the thin margin the whole exercise exists to recover.

The kill criterion: when bulk or donation beats another week of listing

Set this before the first batch goes live, in writing: an item gets 30 days listed and one price cut of about a tenth. If it has not sold after both, it leaves the individual listings that week, into the next bulk lot if it has any resale value in aggregate, or to donation if it does not. No second price cut, no relisting to reset the clock, no exceptions for items you happen to like.

The reasoning is arithmetic, not tidiness. Every extra week an item sits listed, it holds shelf space, holds its slice of your attention in the weekly review, and returns nothing. The bulk route pays less per item and pays it once, with near zero further time. Donation pays nothing and still ends the cost of storing and reviewing the item, which is why it beats a listing that will never clear its floor. Surplus stock work succeeds when the pile shrinks on a schedule you chose, at prices you computed, instead of lingering at prices you hoped for.
