AI Training Videos for Staff Onboarding

Every small business trains the same way: a senior person stops real work and walks the new hire through the till, the opening checklist, the returns process — again. AI video tools turn a written script into a presenter-led video with an on-screen avatar and voiceover, no camera, no reshoot when a step changes. Used well, they make that repeated explanation happen once. Used carelessly, they produce a shelf of polished videos nobody learns from, on a plan whose minutes ran out in week two. This guide covers the math and the method that separate the two outcomes.

What it is

AI video platforms work from a script. You type or paste the words, choose an avatar and a voice, arrange simple slides or screen images behind the presenter, and the service renders a finished video. Synthesia, the platform most associated with training, uses a structured slide-based editor and is positioned squarely at corporate training, internal communications and onboarding; HeyGen leads with more expressive avatars and marketing-style content; Colossyan is the third name in current comparisons, aimed at interactive workplace training. A 2026 comparison review sums up the split exactly that way: Synthesia for training and onboarding, HeyGen for creator-style content, Colossyan when interactive learning is the priority.

The genuine advantage for a small business is not the avatar — it is the edit. When a procedure changes, you change a sentence in the script and re-render, instead of re-booking the person, the room and the camera. The genuine limitation is the same: a video teaches watching, not doing. Every video in your library therefore needs the acceptance test in the setup steps — a new hire performs the task after watching, unaided — or you have made documentation theatre.

Keep videos short and single-purpose: one video per task, a few minutes each, named by the task ("Opening the shop," "Processing a return") rather than by department. A library of twelve short videos gets used; one forty-minute epic gets skipped.

Who it's for

Best for businesses that hire or cross-train regularly and repeat the same explanations: retail and hospitality with seasonal staff, trades firms bringing on apprentices, clinics, salons and service shops where consistency matters — every location or shift doing the task the same way. It also suits businesses whose trainer is the owner: your knowledge gets captured once instead of living only in your calendar.

It fits poorly if you hire one person a year — a printed checklist and shadowing will beat any subscription — or if the work is mostly physical judgement that has to be felt to be learned. Video can front-load the knowledge around a hands-on skill; it cannot replace supervised practice, and for safety-critical tasks it must never be the only training a new hire gets.

What it costs

Prices checked 2026-10-16. Both leading platforms meter output, but in different units — and the unit decides your real bill. Synthesia meters minutes. As verified against its official pricing page in an October 2026 review: the free Basic tier includes 10 minutes of video or dubbing a month with the Synthesia logo retained. Starter is $29 per month, or $18 per month billed yearly, adding downloads and logo removal. Pro is $89 per month, or $64 billed yearly. SCORM export — the format learning-management systems ingest — along with brand kits and single sign-on, sits on the custom-priced Enterprise tier, so if your videos must live inside an LMS, that is a sales conversation, not a checkout.

HeyGen meters credits. From HeyGen's own FAQ: the free plan allows 3 videos a month, up to 1 minute each. Creator is $29 per month, or $24 billed annually, with 600 credits a month, videos up to 30 minutes, 1080p export and watermark removal, across 175+ languages. Pro is $49 per month with 1,000 credits and 4K export; Business is $149 per month plus $20 per additional seat. The catch is what a credit buys. An independent review of HeyGen's published rates found a minute of avatar video costs roughly 7 credits on the Avatar III model, about 16 on Avatar IV, and around 48 on Avatar V — so the same 600 credits yield anything from over an hour of finished video to about twelve minutes, depending on the avatar model you pick. An edit that re-renders a scene spends credits again.

Now the library math the reviews skip. A starter onboarding library — twelve core tasks at about four minutes each — is roughly 48 finished minutes. On a free Basic allowance of 10 minutes a month, that is five months of building. On Creator's 600 credits, rendered with the economical avatar model, it fits in a month or two; rendered with the premium model, it does not fit at all. Decide your library size and avatar model first, and the right tier picks itself.

Target ROI: the honest math EXAMPLE

The return is trainer hours — usually the owner's or a senior employee's — handed back every time someone new starts.

Example: Suppose a senior employee spends 6 hours walking each new hire through the standard procedures, at $30/hr — $180 of senior time per hire, repeated 8 times a year, roughly $1,440 annually. If a video library cuts live walk-through to 2 hours of supervised practice per hire, that frees about $960 a year, against roughly $216 a year for a Starter plan billed annually — plus your one-time scripting time, which is real: budget an hour of writing and checking per finished video. The deal collapses if nobody watches, or if procedures change so often that re-renders eat the plan — which is why the measurement list below tracks completions and update renders, not just subscription cost.

What to actually measure:

  • Senior-staff hours spent per new hire, before and after the library
  • Watch-and-perform test pass rate on each video (see setup)
  • Questions per new hire in week one — should fall as the library improves
  • Minutes or credits consumed per month, including update re-renders
  • Videos gone stale: procedures changed but the video not yet updated

How to set it up

  1. List the library before you subscribe. Write down the ten to fifteen tasks every new hire must learn, in the order they meet them. Count the minutes honestly — that list, not a sales page, determines your tier.
  2. Script from your existing procedures. If you have checklists or SOP documents, each one becomes a script almost directly. Write for the ear: short sentences, the action first, no jargon you have not defined. Read every script aloud once; if you stumble, the avatar will too.
  3. Get consent before cloning a real person. Both leading platforms offer personal avatars — a digital version of a real employee. Only create one with that person's clear, documented agreement, covering where the videos will be used and what happens when they leave. A stock avatar needs no one's permission and is the right default.
  4. Build two videos on the free tier first. Pick one simple task and one medium task. Render both at the avatar quality you would actually use, and note exactly how many minutes or credits they consumed. That consumption rate — not the plan's headline allowance — is your budgeting number.
  5. Run the watch-and-perform test. Have someone who does not know the task — ideally an actual new hire, otherwise a colleague from another role — watch the video once, then perform the task unaided while you watch silently. Success looks like this: the task completed correctly, in order, with no question the video should have answered. Every stumble is a script edit, not a viewer failure. Do not publish a video to the library until it passes.
  6. Keep the scripts as the source of truth. Store each script as a dated document next to the procedure it teaches. When a procedure changes, edit the script, re-render that one video, and log the minutes or credits the update cost. A library whose scripts live only inside the video tool becomes un-updatable the day you switch tools.
  7. Put the library where new hires actually are. One page or folder, ordered by first week, linked from your onboarding checklist — with the hands-on practice sessions scheduled beside it, so video is clearly the preparation, not the whole training.

Watch-outs and honest limitations

An avatar presenter is not your trainer, and staff can tell the difference. Use video for the repeatable knowledge layer — where things are, what the steps are, what good looks like — and keep human time for questions, judgement and supervised practice. Businesses that replace the human layer entirely tend to get compliance on paper and confusion on the floor.

Stale videos are worse than no videos. A confident on-screen presenter teaching last year's returns process trains people wrong at scale. The script log in step 6 is your control: when a procedure changes, the video is marked stale the same day, and it comes down or gets re-rendered before the next hire watches it.

Finally, mind the meter. Premium avatar models and casual re-renders are how a modest plan turns into an upgrade conversation. Fix scripts in the document, batch your changes, render once — and re-check consumption after your first full month against the rate you measured in step 4.

What to measure in your first 30 days

  • Watch-and-perform pass rate for each published video
  • Actual minutes or credits used versus your step-4 estimate
  • Senior-staff hours spent training the first hire who uses the library
  • Week-one questions from new hires, compared with the last pre-video hire
  • Any procedure change that left a video stale — and how fast it was fixed
