Building a Customer How-To Library With AI Drafts

The raw material is already sitting in the inbox: the same setup question answered four times this month, the same return explained twice this week. A how-to library starts by collecting those real questions — one published plan builds a five-article help centre from recent customer emails in the first month — and only then brings AI in, to draft from material the business already knows is true.

Published 2026-10-30 · Last updated 2026-10-30

Where the articles actually come from

Not from a brainstorm. The strongest starting point in the sources checked is deliberately small: a reply library of approved first drafts for the ten questions customers ask most often. That framing matters. The library is not a publishing project; it is the written version of answers the business already gives, checked once instead of retyped daily.

The five-article version of the same idea works from recent customer emails and puts the resulting help centre where customers can find it, linked in the site header or footer. Both approaches share the same sequence: real questions first, drafting second. An AI asked to invent a help centre will produce plausible topics. An AI handed real emails produces articles about the problems customers actually have.

Choose the first ten: mine the inbox, not your imagination

  1. Collect a month of questions. Pull recent customer emails, support tickets, and chat transcripts into one pile. Add call notes where they exist — one recommended setup pairs a writing model with a transcription tool for customer interviews, so spoken questions can be mined the same way.
  2. Count repeats. Group the pile by the task the customer was trying to complete, not by the words they used. The groups with the most items are the candidate articles.
  3. Apply the decision framework. An item earns an article when it repeats, the correct answer is stable, and a wrong answer costs the customer time or the business a return or a refund. One-off questions stay as saved replies, not articles.
  4. Cap the first batch. Five to ten articles. A small, finished, published library beats a large draft folder, and the first batch proves the workflow before it scales.
  5. Write down the source for each article. Note which emails or tickets each draft came from. That note becomes the fact base the draft is checked against, and the first place to look when the article is later questioned.

The drafting loop: AI draft, expert check, plain-English pass

  1. Draft from the source material, in your own words. The help-centre model worth copying is explicit on this point: your own articles, in your own words. Give the AI the real question, the correct answer as your team gives it, and any limits or exceptions, and ask for a structured draft — not a free invention.
  2. Keep drafts private until they pass review. In that same model, drafts stay private and nothing reaches a visitor, or an assistant drawing on the library, until you publish. Set up the workflow the same way: drafts pushed to the content system as unpublished posts, never straight to the live site.
  3. Have the person who does the job verify it. The human review step for a starter library is specific: confirm facts, tone, pricing, and promises. That review is estimated at one to two hours for a starter set — small, because the material was true before drafting began. If a draft needs heavy correction, the source note was too thin; fix the source, not just the draft.
  4. Do the plain-English pass last. Shorten sentences, put the customer’s words in the heading, and make the first line answer the question. A how-to the customer cannot follow under time pressure has failed at its only job.

One limit to plan around: each published article can be handed to an AI assistant in full, but a library that outgrows what fits in a single prompt needs a system that names what it has not read rather than guessing. Publish the library for humans first; connect assistants second, with that honesty requirement attached.

Publish like you mean it: findable, dated, owned

Link the library in the header or footer, where the five-article plan puts it — a help centre customers cannot find deflects nothing. Give every article three things at publication: the date it was verified, the name of the person who owns it, and the source note from the selection step. The date and owner look like admin. They are, in fact, the maintenance system, installed in advance.

The maintenance loop that keeps it true

A how-to goes stale silently: a price changes, a step moves, a policy is updated, and the article keeps stating the old version with full confidence. The loop that prevents it is short:

  1. Trigger review on change, not only on schedule. Any product, price, or policy change generates a check of the articles that mention it — the owner named on the article does that check.
  2. Re-verify on a fixed cadence as a backstop. The owner re-reads each article against the current process and updates the verified date, or corrects the article. An article whose date is old is treated as unverified, however confident it sounds.
  3. Feed new repeats back in. When a question starts repeating that the library does not cover, it enters the same selection count as the original ten. The library grows from evidence, in both directions.
  4. Retire articles openly. A how-to for a discontinued product or process comes down or is marked superseded. A wrong article is worse than a missing one, because customers act on it.

What success looks like at 90 days

What success looks like: the first five to ten articles are published, linked from the header or footer, each carrying a verified date and a named owner; the questions they cover are visibly dropping out of the inbox because customers find the answer first; and no article in the set states a price, promise, or step that the owner has not re-checked since it changed. The library is small. It is also true — which is the property that makes the next ten articles worth adding.
