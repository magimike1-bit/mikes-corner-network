Pricing Services With AI Help: Find Your Floor, Then Test the New Rate

48.82 dollars an hour. That is the break-even rate in a fully worked published example, 75,000 dollars of annual costs spread over 1,536 billable hours, and it carries no profit at all. Most service businesses have never run that division for their own numbers. AI can help you find the costs you forgot and stress test the result, but the floor itself is arithmetic, and the new rate still has to survive a test in the real market.

Published 2026-11-10 · Last updated 2026-11-10

Step 1: List every cost, then let AI hunt for the missing ones

Start on paper, not in a chatbot. List your annual costs in the two groups pricing guidance uses: direct costs, the labour and materials that go into delivering the work itself, and indirect costs, which include overhead such as rent, utilities, equipment and maintenance, insurance, indirect labour, and marketing. Add what you need to pay yourself. Then count your hours honestly: total working hours minus the non-billable hours spent sourcing clients, marketing, and doing paperwork. Only the billable remainder can carry the costs.

Now bring in the AI, for the one job it is genuinely good at here. An AI assistant is good at organising numbers, building simple formulas, suggesting costs that were missed, running what if scenarios, and drafting clear price list wording. The missed cost hunt is where it earns the subscription: the items it commonly flags include payment processing fees, breakages and returns, unpaid time spent on social media, and money that should be set aside for tax. Paste in your cost list with no customer names and no bank details, neither of which it needs, and ask what a business like yours usually forgets. Every suggestion it returns gets checked against your own records before it enters the list.

Step 2: Rebuild the arithmetic in a spreadsheet and read the gap

Do not let the chatbot do the final sums. It can do long chains of arithmetic with slips in them, and the published guidance on this is blunt: ask for the formula, not just the answer, rebuild it in a spreadsheet, and if the two disagree, trust the spreadsheet. The formula itself is short. Break-even hourly rate equals total annual costs divided by billable hours. Your selling rate is that figure plus your chosen profit margin.

The example is a scenario built from published figures, not a forecast for your business, and your costs and hours replace every line. Read the last line twice anyway. A gap that looks small per hour is a five figure sum across a year of billable time, and it is paid out of your own income, quietly, on every job. Count the assistant itself while you are here: Canadian web prices checked on October 19, 2026 put ChatGPT Plus at CA$25 a month before tax, Claude Pro at CA$28 a month, and ChatGPT Go at CA$11 a month, with checkout showing the final amount for your account. That subscription belongs in the cost list like any other, and a free tier is often enough for the pricing conversation itself, within its usage limits.

Step 3: Turn the hourly floor into job prices

Customers rarely buy hours. They buy a finished job, so convert the floor. The published example does it directly: multiply the hourly rate by the estimated hours, which prices a 50 hour project at 2,807 dollars at the 56.14 dollar rate. Build each fixed job price the same way, estimated hours times your floor rate, plus direct costs that sit outside the hour, such as materials and travel. If the estimate is uncertain, the job is quoted hourly or in stages, not discounted below the floor to win it.

Packages follow the same rule with the floor underneath. Ask the AI to draft a basic, standard, and premium version of your main service, or a one off job beside a monthly plan, with each tier priced above your break-even figure and the reasoning shown. Treat its ranges as starting points to adjust, not answers. What it cannot give you is the market: it cannot reliably state the going rate in your niche today, because its knowledge has a training cutoff, it cannot account for hyper local conditions, and any competitor price it gathers has to be verified by opening the source yourself, because these tools can misread pages or surface old prices. Verify market rates through human sources, peers, job boards, and your own quote history. The floor comes from your costs. The ceiling comes from the market. The AI helps you think between the two and write the wording, including a rate increase note for existing customers when the time comes.

Step 4: Test the new rate on new quotes only

A rate change announced everywhere at once gives you no way to read the result. Test it instead, with the terms written down before the first quote goes out.

  1. Scope the test. The new rate applies to new quotes only, for a fixed window, such as the next 20 quotes or 60 days, whichever ends later. Existing customers keep their current rate until the test is read.
  2. Record the baseline first. From your last comparable set of quotes, note how many were sent, how many were won, and the revenue per billable hour they produced. Without a baseline, the test result is a feeling.
  3. Log every test quote the same way. Job type, hours estimated, price quoted, won or lost, and the reason given when a customer says no. Ask the AI to summarise the log at the end of the window and surface patterns, then check its summary against the raw log.
  4. Read revenue per billable hour, not win rate alone. A lower win rate at a higher rate can still pay better, and a higher win rate below your floor is losing money faster. The test passes if revenue per billable hour rises across the window and the won work fills the calendar you actually have.

The kill criterion, set before the test starts

Write the stop line now, while it costs nothing. If, at the end of the test window, revenue per billable hour has not risen against the baseline, the new rate does not roll out. Revert to the previous rate for the work that was not winning, and work the other two levers instead: raise billable hours by cutting non-billable time, or cut costs out of the annual list. What the kill criterion never permits is drifting below the floor from Step 2 to chase volume. A business must make at least its costs to reach break-even, and every hour sold under the floor is an hour the owner subsidises. If the test passes, roll the rate out in writing, give existing customers notice and a brief reason, and set the next review for the date your costs next change. Pricing is not a number you find once. It is a floor you defend and a test you keep re-running.
