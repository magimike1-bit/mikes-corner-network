Using AI to Summarize Vendor Contracts Before You Sign: A Summary Is Not a Review

The pitch is tidy: upload the contract, get a plain-language summary, sign with confidence. The summary part is real. The confidence is the myth. A summary tells you what a document says in shorter form — it does not tell you whether what it says is acceptable to your business, and it can drop or soften a clause on the way through. Used differently, though, the same tools become genuinely useful: not as a verdict, but as a sorting machine that finds the clauses worth your attention.

Disclaimer: This article is general information only. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for legal advice about your situation. Vendor contracts create binding obligations — have a qualified lawyer review any contract before you sign it, and treat everything below as a way to arrive at that review better prepared, not as a replacement for it.

The myth: a summary is a review

A review ends in a judgment: this clause is fine, that one is not, this one needs changing. A summary ends in a shorter document. The gap between the two is where the risk sits, because a summary is judged by readability — and a liability cap, an indemnity, or an automatic obligation reads perfectly smoothly right up until it costs you money. One law-firm guide to AI vendor contracts makes the underlying point plainly: careful attention to these clauses at the outset may prevent substantial legal, operational, and reputational issues later. Later is the expensive time to find them.

There is a second, quieter problem. A summary is a rewrite, and rewrites lose things. A clause can be shortened until its sharp edge disappears, or omitted because it looked like boilerplate. That is why the workflow below has a rule with no exceptions: every flagged item must point back to its location in the original document, so you can read the actual words yourself. A summary that cannot show you where it got each claim about the contract has not done the one job you needed.

The clause table: what to extract, every time

Do not ask for a summary. Ask for a table — one row per clause type, with the contract's position stated in the vendor's own terms, and the clause's location in the document. The rows below are the standing set. The first four come from what a business should confirm before signing an AI vendor agreement: ownership and usage rights for inputs and outputs, clear indemnities, privacy and incident-response obligations, and liability limits. The rest come from the terms working review workflows extract and from pre-signing checks that go beyond the document's own text.

Two rows deserve emphasis because summaries flatten them. The support-model clause — response times set by severity — is one of the clauses procurement guidance singles out as worth building into an agreement, not merely reading. And the IP clause should cover outputs and what happens on termination, which is precisely the moment a short summary tends to skip past.

Your playbook: five positions written before you need them

A table of clauses is only half the workflow. The other half is knowing, in advance, what your business will and will not accept — written down once, as positions, before any particular contract arrives. That written playbook is what turns extraction into review: the AI compares each clause against your positions and flags the deviations.

Five positions cover the table above for most small businesses. Write yours in your own terms; these are the slots, not the answers.

  1. Data: what the vendor may keep, for how long, and whether your inputs may be used for anything beyond serving you.
  2. Ownership: your inputs stay yours; outputs are yours to use, including after the contract ends.
  3. Liability: the lowest cap you will accept, and the losses a cap must not exclude for the deal to make sense.
  4. Exit: the longest notice period you will accept — a 90-day vendor notice period is common, so treat that as a reference point, not a surprise — and a tested way to export your data.
  5. Support: the response times by severity below which the service is not worth its price to you.

This comparison model is not theoretical. A published case study of playbook-based review describes the working pattern: upload the agreement, summarise the critical terms — data retention, indemnity, liability caps, service levels — compare them against a written playbook, and mark up the deviations. The reported result was legal review turnaround reduced by 60%, with the humans spending their time on roughly the flagged 20% of the document. Read those numbers carefully: the saving came from aiming human attention, not from removing it. The lawyers in that workflow still reviewed — they reviewed the flags first.

Run the comparison, then spot-check the flags

  1. Upload the full contract to a general AI assistant with document upload, or a dedicated contract-review tool if you sign often. Give it the clause table and your playbook in the same request.
  2. Require locations. Every row must cite where in the document the clause sits — section number or heading. A row without a location is unfinished work; send it back.
  3. Read every flag in the original words. Open the contract at each flagged location and read the clause itself, not the summary of it. You are checking two things: that the summary rendered the clause fairly, and that your playbook position really is the one you want applied.
  4. Sample the unflagged rows too. Pick two or three clauses the comparison passed, and spot-check them against the original. This is the control that catches a summary which dropped or softened something it should have flagged.
  5. Run the checks no summariser performs. Ask the vendor when their data-export process was last tested. Check the exit notice period in the document against your position. Check the supplier's financial viability — a contract with a vendor who will not be operating next year is a risk no clause table shows.
  6. Sort the flags into two piles: questions you can put to the vendor yourself, and clauses that go to a lawyer unchanged. The next section draws that line.

What success looks like: you hold a one-page table where every row names a clause, its location, and either “matches playbook” or a flag you have personally read in the original text — and nothing in the pile surprises you later, because the surprises were found while walking away was still free.

What goes to a lawyer, unchanged

The decision framework is deliberately blunt. Indemnities, liability limits, and ownership of inputs and outputs go to a qualified lawyer before signing whenever the contract matters to the business — which is to say, whenever walking away from it later would hurt. These are the clauses where a softened summary does the most damage, and they are the ones the pre-signing guidance itself puts at the top of the confirm list. Send the lawyer the flagged clauses in the vendor's original wording, with your playbook positions attached. That preparation is where the AI workflow earns its keep: the lawyer starts at the flags instead of at page one, and you pay for judgment instead of for reading.

What stays with you: the vendor questions (export testing, notice periods, support response times), the viability check, and the final decision about whether the flagged terms are acceptable at the price. What never stays with the AI: the decision to sign. A summary can inform that decision. It cannot make it, and a tool that presents its summary as a verdict is asking you to skip the step this entire workflow exists to protect.

Signing day checklist

  • The clause table is complete, and every row cites a location in the document.
  • Every flag has been read in the vendor's original wording, not only in the shortened version.
  • At least two unflagged clauses have been spot-checked against the original.
  • The vendor has answered the export, notice-period, and support questions.
  • Flagged indemnity, liability, and ownership clauses have gone to a qualified lawyer.
  • Every remaining playbook deviation is one you have chosen to accept, knowingly, at this price.
