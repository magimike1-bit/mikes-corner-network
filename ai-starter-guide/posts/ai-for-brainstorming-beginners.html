AI for Brainstorming: Turning a Blank Page Into a List of Ideas to Choose From

Yes, a chatbot can get you past a blank page — but the first list it returns is a starting pile, not an answer. The useful skill is running two more rounds: narrow that pile with constraints that reflect your real situation, then challenge the survivors before you pick. The deliverable is a shortlist you chose, for reasons you can state.

Published 2026-10-28 · Last updated 2026-10-28

Why the first list feels generic

Ask a broad question and the reply tends to circle the same few obvious ideas. One explanation offered by PostRoad Marketing is practical rather than mysterious: if a prompt keeps surfacing the same three ideas, the audience description was probably too broad, and more precise context makes the output less generic. That fits how the tool is being used. “Ideas for a party” contains almost no information. Ideas for a party for twelve neighbours, outdoors, on a small budget, with two vegetarians and no oven available contain decisions — and decisions are what separate one list from another.

So do not judge brainstorming by round one. Judge it by whether rounds two and three move.

The three-round session, step by step

The method below follows a phase approach described by TheNextAI: define the goal with context, start with broad prompts, then drill down and ask for ideas from different angles or perspectives. Gemini and ChatGPT are the tools that source recommends for the generation phase, and any general chatbot can run the same sequence.

  1. Write the goal in one sentence, with context. Include who the ideas are for, what a good result would do, and any hard limit — time, money, space, skill level. Example: “I need a weekend project for two adults at home, under three hours, using tools a beginner is likely to own.”
  2. Round one — widen. Ask for a deliberately large list, and give the prompt a structure. A strong prompt, as WPForms puts it, hands the AI a role, an audience, a length, a structure, and a tone. Try: “Act as a practical project adviser. Give me 20 weekend project ideas for two adults at home. Give each idea a one-line angle explaining who it suits. Keep the tone plain, no hype.” Do not edit while the list arrives. Widening is the job here.
  3. Mark, do not rank yet. Put a star beside anything that makes you react — curiosity counts. Cross out only the impossible: wrong budget, wrong season, needs equipment you will not buy. Expect roughly a third to survive. If almost everything survives, your constraints were too soft; if nothing survives, they were stated too late.
  4. Round two — narrow with constraints. Paste back your starred ideas and add the constraints one at a time: “Keep only ideas that fit in one afternoon. Now keep only ideas that cost nothing using things already at home. Now rewrite each survivor in one sentence.” One constraint per message lets you see which limit actually does the cutting.
  5. Round three — challenge the shortlist. Ask for a different angle on purpose: “For each of these four ideas, tell me the most likely reason it disappoints, and one change that would fix that.” Then ask for the outlier: “Add one unusual idea I probably have not thought of, and say why it fits my goal.” Unusual is not automatically good. It is there to test whether your shortlist is a choice or just the familiar options in a new order.
  6. Choose, and write down why. Pick one to act on and up to three to keep. Beside each keeper, write the reason in your own words — not the chatbot’s words. A reason you cannot write is a sign the idea is not really yours yet.

What success looks like: you end with one chosen idea and a shortlist of no more than three keepers, each with a reason in your own words, plus a note of the constraint that eliminated the rest. If you still have fifteen undifferentiated options, the session has produced a list, not a decision — run round two again with a harder constraint.

The specificity fix, for when every idea sounds the same

Generic output usually has a repairable cause. Work through these fixes in order, because each one adds a different kind of information.

Name the audience precisely. Replace “beginners” with who they are, what they already know, and what they are short of. PostRoad’s point about broad audience descriptions applies here directly: precision in, variety out.

Give the output a shape. WPForms lists concrete formats worth copying: five blog ideas, each with a one-line angle, or ten title options under 60 characters. A shape forces choices. “Give me some ideas” invites a shrug in list form.

Change the angle, not just the wording. Ask for ideas from the perspective of a tight budget, then from the perspective of someone with plenty of time and no money, then from a sceptic who thinks the whole project is unnecessary. TheNextAI’s phase method treats different angles and perspectives as a distinct step, not a garnish — because a reworded prompt mostly returns the same ideas wearing new adjectives.

Feed back a rejection. “Those five are too similar to each other. Drop anything that requires buying equipment and try again” carries information a fresh prompt loses. The chat remembers the conversation; use that instead of starting over.

Choosing between the survivors: a small decision framework

Four questions to score each shortlisted idea
QuestionStrong answerWarning sign
Can it start this week?First step is obvious and smallNeeds a purchase, a permission, or a free weekend first
Does it fit the original goal?You can say how, in one sentenceIt is interesting but answers a different problem
What does it cost if it fails?An hour or two, nothing spentMoney, other people’s time, or a deadline
Would you still pick it tomorrow?Yes, without re-reading the chatIt only looked good beside weaker options

Score quickly, on paper if the list is long. The framework is not a formula — it is a way to stop the loudest idea winning by default. The lowest-scoring idea is not banned; it goes to the idea bank below, where a different week may suit it better.

Keep the winners: a one-page idea bank

PostRoad notes that prompts get you past the blank page, and that editing a draft into your own voice takes minutes compared with an afternoon starting from zero. The same applies to ideas themselves: a saved shortlist is next month’s head start. Keep one page — a note, a document, a card on the fridge — with three columns: the idea in one line, why it survived, and the constraint that would need to change before you use it. Add the date. Ideas without dates look timeless and turn stale quietly.

Delete from the bank freely. Its value is not completeness; it is that your next blank page starts at round two instead of round one.
