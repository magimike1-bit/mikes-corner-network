AI for Greeting Cards, Speeches, and Toasts: Start From Your Own Stories

Can a chatbot write a birthday card, a wedding toast, or a short leaving speech that still sounds like the person delivering it? Yes, under one condition: the material has to be yours. Feed the tool real stories and details first, and it becomes a drafting partner that shapes them. Ask it to invent the feeling from nothing, and you get a polished piece that could belong to anyone. This guide covers the story first method, the different length rules for cards and speeches, and the two checks to run before anyone hears the result.

Can a chatbot write a toast that still sounds like you?

It can draft one. Whether it sounds like you depends on what you hand it. A chatbot given only an occasion, write a toast for my sister’s wedding, warm and funny, has nothing true to work with, so it fills the space with general praise and stock jokes. A chatbot given two real stories, the place, and one thing the person actually said, has a draft worth editing. The tool is good at shape: order, pacing, and a clean closing line. It is not a source of memories, and it will invent plausible ones if you leave gaps.

That division of labour is the whole method. You supply the facts and the feeling. The chatbot supplies the structure and the first draft. You then take the draft back, word by word, before it is spoken or sent.

Start with stories, not with the prompt

Before you open the chat window, write down three real stories or details about the person or the occasion. A single specific memory does more work than a paragraph of qualities. Specific beats general: a place, a date, a thing they said. One strong story is enough for a toast, and two is plenty. For a card, one detail is usually the whole piece: the trip you took together, the favour you never thanked them for, the joke only the two of you share.

Check each item on your list twice. Is it true, exactly as you would tell it? And is it fine to share in front of the whole room, or on a card that may be passed around? Leave out any private story the person would not want told, however good it would sound. If a detail fails either test, it does not go into the prompt, because once it is in the draft, it is easy to read past it later.

Cards are short, speeches are timed: two different length rules

A card has no clock on it, so its rule is density, not duration. Two to five sentences that could only be about this person beat a full page of warm generalities. Ask the chatbot for three short options rather than one long one, then take the sentence you like from each and assemble your own version. The choosing is the writing.

A speech is measured in minutes, and minutes convert to words. Speaking runs at roughly 130 words a minute, so a three minute toast is about 400 words. The standard target for a wedding speech is three to five minutes, about 400 to 650 words, built on one story, maybe two, and ending with a clear toast. Shorter family toasts run one to two minutes each, and a thank you speech from the couple runs two to three minutes. Verify your own draft against the rate: divide its word count by 130 for a rough number of minutes, then treat the read aloud test in the next sections as the real measurement, because pauses and laughter add time the maths cannot see.

One structure serves most speeches. Say who you are in one sentence. Thank the hosts briefly. Tell the one story. Turn it toward the person the story now includes, a partner, a team, a graduate’s next step. Say the wish, simply. Then raise the glass or land the closing line, and stop.

Draft it with a general chatbot, step by step

  1. Write your story list first. Three real stories or details, checked for truth and for sharing, as described above. No list, no draft: this step is the article, and the rest is formatting.
  2. Set the length before the tone. For a speech, name the minutes. For a card, name the sentences. A length stated up front stops the most common failure, a draft twice as long as the slot it has to fit.
  3. Ask for structure, then a draft, using only your stories. A working pattern: give the occasion, your role, your stories, and the tone, then ask for a simple structure followed by a draft that uses only the material you supplied, with short sentences that are easy to say out loud and a clear closing line.
  4. For a card, ask for three short options instead. Pick the best sentences across the three and combine them yourself. Delete any line you would not actually write to this person.
  5. Rewrite the draft in your own words. Read each sentence and ask whether you would say it that way. Where the answer is no, retype the sentence the way you would say it. The facts stay, the phrasing becomes yours.
  6. Cut anything only one person in the room will understand. An inside reference that needs explaining loses everyone else. Either give it its one line of context or remove it.

What success looks like: every story in the final version is one you listed yourself, the spoken timing lands inside the slot when you read it aloud with a timer, and no sentence survives that you would be embarrassed to say in your own voice.

Two checks before anyone hears it

Check one is the invention check. Go through the draft line by line against your story list. Chatbots smooth gaps, and a smoothed gap often contains a detail that never happened: a year, a place, a quotation, a reason someone did something. If a detail is not on your list and you cannot confirm it from memory, cut it. Never deliver a touching story about a real person that the tool assembled for you.

Check two is the read aloud check. Practise out loud, with a timer, more than once. One detailed wedding guide recommends practising at least five times, once in front of someone, and printing the speech in large type or using cards, because phones dim and buzz. A lighter version still works: read it aloud twice at speaking pace, fix every sentence you stumble on, and confirm the total time. Sentences that read well silently often fail aloud, and aloud is the only version the room gets.

Two delivery rules sit underneath both checks. Do not use jokes the tool wrote about nobody in particular, and do not use a joke that embarrasses the person for laughs. And know the limit of the method: a eulogy, or a very personal speech, is best written mostly by you. Use the chatbot there for structure, not for the words.

Which route fits which occasion

A card for someone you know well: the chatbot route described above, because the personal detail is the entire value and you already hold it. A toast or short speech with a fixed slot: the same route, with the timing maths and the read aloud check doing the quality control. A form based speech generator, the kind with dropdowns for occasion and tone: reasonable when you want a fast first shape and have no stories ready, but its output needs the same invention check, and it cannot supply the story only you know. A eulogy or a speech where every word will be remembered: write it yourself, and let the tool do no more than suggest an order for your own paragraphs. The closer the occasion sits to the heart, the less of the wording should be delegated.
