AI that describes images: help for blind and low-vision readers at home

Accessibility · Published 2026-11-01 · Last updated 2026-11-01

Image-description AI is software that looks at what a phone camera sees, or at a photo you hand it, and says what is there, out loud or in text: the words on a letter, the name on a label, the layout of a room. For a blind or low-vision reader, that turns a visual task into a listening task. For a family member setting it up, the question is narrower: which tool, for which job, and where does the description stop being trustworthy?

The short verdict: start with the free dedicated apps, because their modes are built task by task. Use a general chatbot’s photo description as the flexible third option. Keep a human in the loop for anything where a wrong description costs money, health or safety.

The pattern in the table matters more than any single row. Dedicated apps split the job into named channels, so the tool knows what kind of answer is wanted before it looks. General chatbots take any image and any question, which is flexible and less predictable. Volunteers bring judgement and bring variance with it.

Your first session, step by step

  1. Install one dedicated app first. Seeing AI for text-heavy home tasks, Be My Eyes for described photos, and create the account as the person who needs assistance, not as a volunteer, where the app offers both roles.
  2. Pick the channel that matches the task before pointing the camera: Short Text for a label or a line on an envelope, Document for a full letter, Product for a barcode, Scene for the room in front of you. In Be My AI, remember the description is built from a single picture, not a live video, everything that matters must be inside that one frame.
  3. Frame the shot. Too close is the common failure: an object only partially in frame gets a partial description. For a flat document such as an A4 letter, place it on a table, rest the phone’s camera lens near the middle of the page, then raise the phone steadily, keeping it level, until it is about two feet above the document before taking the picture.
  4. Listen to the whole description once before acting on any part of it. Where the app offers it, use the follow-up route, the “ask more” questions in Be My AI, for the one detail the first pass skipped. The app can also report when a document is only partially visible or upside down; treat that message as an instruction to reframe, not as a failed tool.
  5. Verify one known item. Run the same channel on something whose contents you already know, a familiar label, a letter you have already had read, and compare. That comparison, not the app’s confidence, is your calibration.

What success looks like: a full-page letter read end to end without a missing edge, a label identified without a guess, and one follow-up question answered from the same photo, with a known-item check that matched.

AI, volunteer, or specialist help: a decision framework

  • Text with a definite right answer, a label, a menu, a letter: start with the dedicated channel. The answer can be re-run and compared.
  • A scene where judgement matters, does this outfit match, is this setting right on the dial: a volunteer call often beats a description, because a person can be asked “are you sure?” in context. Weigh the documented variance: volunteers may not have formal sight-loss training and experience differs, so keep the questions specific.
  • Privacy-sensitive material: a volunteer is a stranger by design. The standing caution from the service itself is to share only information you are comfortable sharing with strangers, which rules out bank details and similar material on a volunteer call, whatever the task.
  • Complex or high-stakes surroundings, unfamiliar travel, complicated displays: specialist human services exist for exactly this, offered as on-demand trained visual interpreters over live video. This guide quotes no price for them because none was verified from the provider’s own pages; check there before deciding.

The safety line

Descriptions are good and incomplete at the same time. A documented example from practical use: before vacuuming, an AI scan of the floor can find larger items, but it is unreliable on small ones, dryer sheets are the named miss, and it will not usually catch everything in a scene. Generalise that honestly. A description is a strong first read and a weak final authority.

So the line is this. Never let a description be the only check on medication instructions, on money being handed over beyond a single recognised note, on whether a path or road is clear, or on any appliance state where a mistake injures. Use the description to form the question, then confirm through a second channel, a known-item comparison, or a person. Set up well, these tools return real independence for the everyday tasks, letters read privately, labels identified without waiting for a visitor, and knowing exactly where they stop is what makes that independence safe to rely on.
