Uploading files and photos to an AI chatbot: what you can send, and what to check

Getting started · 8 min read

Typing everything into a chatbot gets old quickly. Uploading removes the typing: a document, spreadsheet, presentation, photo, or — on paid ChatGPT plans — an audio recording can be attached to a conversation, and the chatbot works with the contents directly. OpenAI's current help pages confirm that document, spreadsheet, presentation, text, and image uploads are available on free and paid ChatGPT plans, subject to plan-specific limits — older tutorials claiming uploads require a subscription or a browser extension are out of date. What those tutorials rarely explain is what the chatbot actually does with a file, where the limits sit in plain numbers, and how a beginner verifies that the answer about a file is right. This guide covers the whole sequence: the jobs uploads do, a safety pass before sending, a step-by-step first upload, a limits table for ChatGPT and Gemini, and the failure modes worth knowing.

The three jobs uploads do: summarize, extract, analyze

Nearly every useful upload request is one of three jobs. Naming the job in the request is the single easiest way to get a better result:

  • Summarize and transform. Turn a long document into a short, simpler summary; rewrite a short document in a different style; compare two documents; get feedback on a presentation; turn meeting audio into structured notes. The chatbot is reshaping material it has been given.
  • Extract. Pull specific things out of a file: every mention of a topic in a PDF, relevant quotes, headings and lists, a document's author or creation date, or the rows in a spreadsheet that match a condition. Extraction suits uploads best because the answer can be checked against the file line by line.
  • Analyze. Work over the material: explore a spreadsheet's trends or build a visualization from it, or judge the sentiment and tone of a document. Analysis produces new claims, so it needs the verification pass described at the end of this guide.

Photos follow the same pattern. A photo can be described, a document in a photo can be read, and objects in a scene can be asked about. OpenAI notes that marking up an image in advance — circling the part that matters — helps direct the chatbot's attention to the right area.

Before you upload: file type, size, and a privacy pass

Three checks take a minute and prevent most failed uploads. First, the file type. In ChatGPT, documents and text are supported as DOCX, PDF, and TXT — and a Google Docs file cannot be uploaded in its native .gdoc form; export it as PDF or DOCX first. Spreadsheets are supported as XLSX, XLS, CSV, and TSV, and presentations as PPTX. For images, OpenAI lists PNG, JPEG, and non-animated GIF. Second, the size, covered in the limits table below. Third, privacy: an upload is a copy of the file sent to a service, and whatever is inside it — a second spreadsheet tab, an address on a letter, a face in a photo's background — goes with it. Run the same sort-and-redact pass used for typed text: remove or cover account numbers, ID numbers, and other people's private details before sending, and never upload a file just because it was handy.

Step-by-step: your first upload

  1. Choose a low-risk file for the first attempt: a public report, a recipe saved as a PDF, a photo of a household object. Learning the mechanics on a safe file builds the habit before anything personal is involved.
  2. Run the three checks above: supported type, under the size limit, nothing inside that should not be shared.
  3. In ChatGPT, attach the file using the + icon in the prompt area and "Add photos & files" — an image can also be dragged into the text area or pasted from the clipboard. The same attach-then-ask pattern applies in other chatbots, including Gemini.
  4. Write the instruction naming the job and the output: "Summarize this document in five bullet points, keeping every date and amount exactly as written", or "Extract every deadline mentioned in this file as a list". Vague instructions ("look at this") produce vague answers.
  5. Read the answer against the original. Open the file side by side and spot-check at least two specific claims — a date, a total, a quoted line — by finding them in the source with your own eyes.
  6. Ask follow-ups in the same conversation ("now compare page 2 with page 5"), then tidy up afterwards using the guidance in the final section.

What success looks like: the answer's checkable details — dates, amounts, names, quoted lines — match the file exactly at every spot checked, and the summary contains nothing that is absent from the file.

Two practical consequences hide in those numbers. The free ChatGPT daily upload allowance — 3 files — is generous for learning and tight for a big sorting project, so batch questions per file rather than uploading one file per question. And the token cap on ChatGPT documents means a very long document can be refused or only partly used even when its file size looks modest; splitting a huge document into sections is the normal workaround.

Where uploads go wrong

  • Confident summaries, wrong details. A summary keeps the shape of a document while quietly altering a date or merging two figures. The spot-check in Step 5 exists precisely because summaries feel trustworthy.
  • Image limits are real. OpenAI lists them directly: counts of objects may be approximate, rotated or upside-down text can be misread, text in non-Latin alphabets is handled less well, precise spatial tasks such as exact positions are weak, and descriptions can simply be wrong. Enlarging text inside an image before uploading improves readability.
  • Medical images are out of scope. OpenAI states the model is not suitable for interpreting specialized medical images such as CT scans and should not be used for medical advice — a photographed rash or scan belongs with a clinician, not a chatbot.
  • Layouts and embedded pictures can be lost. On most ChatGPT plans, documents are handled by extracting their digital text, so formatting that carries meaning — and pictures embedded inside documents — may not be seen the way a human reader sees them.
  • Audio transcripts contain errors. OpenAI warns that transcripts may contain errors and speaker identification may be unreliable, so names and decisions in meeting notes deserve a check against the recording.

After the answer: verify, then tidy up

Verification is short: every number kept gets found in the file, every quote gets matched word for word, and anything the chatbot could not point to in the file gets treated as unconfirmed. Tidying up has one surprise worth knowing in ChatGPT: deleting a conversation does not delete a file saved in the Library. Files saved there are removed separately, in Library, and content from a file that was brought into a conversation can remain in that conversation — so a sensitive file needs both actions, not one. In Gemini, Google's Privacy Hub notes that with Keep Activity on, files and photos shared with Gemini are saved in Gemini Apps activity, which is where they are reviewed or deleted. The routine to build is simple: verify first, then delete the file copy and, if it was sensitive, the conversation as well.

FAQ

Can free accounts upload files? In ChatGPT, yes — document and image uploads are available on free and paid plans, with free accounts limited to 3 file uploads per day. Audio uploads are the exception, requiring a paid plan.

Why did an upload fail? The usual causes are an unsupported type (a native .gdoc is the classic example), a file over the size or token limits, or the daily or three-hour upload allowance being used up. OpenAI also notes failed attempts can sometimes count toward the rate limit, so repeated retries make the wait longer, not shorter.

Can a chatbot read a photo of a document? Yes — that is an image input, and it works best when the photo is straight, well lit, and the text is large. Expect weaker results from rotated pages and non-Latin alphabets, and check the transcription before relying on it.
