Using AI to Start a Side Project: From Idea to First Draft Without Coding

Four project forms cover most beginner side projects — a page, a book, a small tool, a game — and each one has a different finish line. Start with the stop rule, then pick a form, produce a genuine first draft in one working session, and keep the tool bill at zero until a draft exists to judge.

Published 2026-10-29 · Last updated 2026-10-29

Who should not start this week — and the stop rule

Skip this week if the idea only works as a business on day one. A faceless video channel, a store, a subscription service — those are publishing and earning plans, not first drafts, and they need a different article. This one is for a smaller, more useful ambition: a finished first version of a thing, made by a person who does not code, that can be looked at, judged, and either improved or abandoned cheaply.

Set the kill criterion before you open a single tool. Write it down, with a date:

  • The draft deadline: if no complete first draft exists after two working sessions, or by a date you pick within the next two weeks, the project stops or shrinks to a smaller form. No third session of “setting up.”
  • The spend line: zero paid subscriptions until a draft exists. The routes below start free or freemium, so a paywall appearing before you have a draft is a signal to switch routes, not to pay.
  • The judge test: at the end of session one, you must be able to say, in one sentence, what the draft is and who it is for. If you cannot, the discovery step was skipped — and that is fixable, in session two, before anything else gets built.

That is the whole discipline. Everything after this section is mechanics.

Pick the form before the tool

Tool directories work backwards for beginners: they start from software and ask what you might make with it. Start from the form instead. A directory aimed at non-technical beginners maps goals to tools — WeWeb for apps, Movable Type for books, Edify for video — which is useful once the form is chosen and confusing before it. Two questions settle the choice. First: which finish line could you reach in one session? Second: which draft would you still care about reading, opening, or playing next week? The smaller honest answer wins. A complete web page you want beats the first screen of an app you will never finish — one learning path for beginners makes exactly that the first real project: a complete page, something you actually want, not a practice exercise.

The one-session first draft, step by step

  1. Write the one-sentence brief. What the thing is, who it is for, and the one job it does. “A page that lists my grandmother’s soup recipes with the ingredient amounts converted to metric” is a brief. “A food app” is not.
  2. Do the discovery pass first. Before asking for any building, ask the AI how a project like this should be made and what every project of this form needs. Read the answer. This is the step the one-prompt builders skip, and skipping it produces a draft you cannot judge, fix, or extend.
  3. Choose the route for your form from the table above, on its free or freemium starting tier. Create the account, nothing more — no upgrades, no add-ons.
  4. Generate the smallest complete version. For a page, ask for the whole page in one go, then stop. For a book, generate the proposal and outline first, then draft the opening section only. For a tool, build the core job and nothing beside it.
  5. Steer one change at a time. The core skill, as the beginner learning path puts it, is describing what you want clearly — specific requests beat vague ones, and one change at a time beats a list of ten. After each change, look at the result before asking for the next.
  6. Save the draft outside the tool. Export it, copy it, screenshot it — whatever the route allows. A draft that only exists inside a free tier you might lose is not yet a draft you own.
  7. Run the judge test. One sentence: what it is, who it is for. Write the sentence down with the date. That sentence is the session’s real output; the draft is evidence for it.

What success looks like: a complete, saved first version — page, outline-plus-opening, working core tool, or playable round — plus your one-sentence description of it, produced without paying for anything.

Build with the AI, not through it

There is a seductive shortcut on offer: describe the whole thing once and let a builder produce it end to end. Resist it, for a practical reason rather than a purist one. A draft generated in a single leap reflects decisions you never saw being made. When something reads wrong — and something will — you have no map of which decision caused it, so the only fix available is another leap.

The alternative is unglamorous and works. Start with a small idea that solves a small problem in your own life. Do a bit of discovery and design first. Ask the AI questions before you ask it for output. Then build in pieces you inspect as they arrive. The draft ends up smaller than the one-leap version. It also ends up yours: you can point at any part of it and say why it is there, which is the difference between a project and a screenshot of one.

The trap is not any single upgrade. It is the second month: a builder here, a design tool there, a hosting plan for a page nobody has seen, each small, none of them attached to a finished draft. So keep a tally — a literal list, on paper or in a note — with three columns: tool, what it is for, and the date you would cancel it if the project stopped. A tool you cannot fill in the second column for does not get bought. And any price quoted inside a tool’s signup flow is the price that counts; check it there, at the moment of decision, rather than trusting any guide — including this one — for a number that can change.

After a draft exists: the next decision

A finished first draft earns exactly one next step, chosen deliberately. Improve it, if the judge-test sentence still excites you and you can name the one change that matters most. Show it to one person it is for, if their reaction is the missing information. Or stop, cleanly, if the draft taught you the idea was smaller than it looked — that is a successful project too, and the kill criterion you set at the start is what lets it end without drifting into a subscription you keep meaning to cancel.

What a draft does not earn is a launch plan. Turning a first version into something people pay for is a business question, with costs and break-even math of its own. Keep the two apart, and the side project stays what it should be at this stage: cheap, finished, and honestly judged.
