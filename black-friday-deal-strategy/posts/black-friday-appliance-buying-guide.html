Black Friday Appliance Buying Guide: Measure First, Then Chase the Deal

A television that doesn't fit goes back in a car. A refrigerator that doesn't fit goes nowhere — it sits in a hallway while a delivery crew leaves. Appliances are the one Black Friday category where the homework is measured in inches, and it all happens before the sale.

Every other Black Friday category forgives a mistake cheaply: the wrong size goes back in a box, the wrong model gets exchanged. A major appliance does not forgive. It arrives on a truck, on a schedule, with a crew — and if it cannot get through the front door, up the stairs, or into the space where the old one sits, the deal collapses on the driveway. The appliance itself is only half the purchase. Delivery, installation, and haul-away are the other half, and on Black Friday all three are in peak-season demand: industry reporting on the holiday period notes that the uptick in appliance demand usually starts on Black Friday and runs until Boxing Day in late December, straining order backlogs — and retailer advice from high-demand periods is blunt that not everything will be available within the timeline a shopper requires, so a long wait or a different brand may be the real choice. The strategy that survives all of this is unglamorous: measure first, confirm the logistics second, and only then chase the price.

The measure-first procedure

Complete every step before ordering — ideally before the sale week, while there is time to re-measure calmly. Keep the results together in one note: appliance type, model, dimensions, and fuel or power source, alongside every measurement below.

  1. Measure the installation space. Record the width, height, and depth of the opening or alcove where the appliance will live. Add the clearance the appliance needs in use: room for doors and drawers to open fully, and any ventilation clearance the manufacturer specifies.
  2. Measure the old appliance and the opening — not just one. Retailer delivery guidance is explicit on this point: measure both the existing appliance and the opening rather than assuming a replacement model will fit. Models change dimensions between generations, and "same size as the old one" is a guess, not a measurement.
  3. Measure the entire delivery path. Walk the route the appliance will travel and measure every pinch point: the front door, hallways, staircases, elevators in condos, tight corners, and interior doorways. This step is the one shoppers skip, and it is the one that matters most — a common reason appliance deliveries are delayed is that the appliance fits the kitchen but not the route to the kitchen.
  4. Verify the hookups. Record what already exists at the installation point: electrical outlets, water lines and shut-off valves, gas connections, and venting. A refrigerator with a water dispenser needs a water line and a shut-off valve within reach; a range needs the right fuel source; a dryer needs its vent path. Note what is present, what is missing, and what a connection kit or extra parts might be needed for.
  5. Compare the model's dimensions against both sets of numbers. The chosen model must pass two tests, not one: it fits the space (step 1) and it fits the path (step 3) — remembering that doors and handles can add depth that decides a tight doorway. If a model fails either test, it is off the list no matter what Black Friday does to its price.
  6. Clear the path before delivery day. Move furniture, rugs, and decor out of the route in advance so the crew's visit is spent installing, not rearranging.

What success looks like: a single note holding the model's dimensions, the space measurements, the path's tightest measurement, and the hookup details — with the tightest path measurement still larger than the appliance's largest traveling dimension. If that comparison cannot be made from the note, the procedure is not finished.

Delivery, installation, and haul-away: confirm before you order

These services vary by retailer, product, and location, which is exactly why they are confirmed before the order rather than discovered after it. Many major appliances are eligible for scheduled home delivery, professional installation is offered for many products — dishwashers, laundry appliances, and cooktops among them — and haul-away and recycling of the old appliance is available for many deliveries, typically for an additional fee. None of those "many"s is a promise about a specific order, so confirm each point at checkout:

  • Delivery: Is scheduled home delivery available for this model at this address, and what is the earliest realistic date — not the most optimistic one — during the holiday rush?
  • Installation: Is installation included, sold separately, or unavailable for this product? Which tasks does it cover — hookup only, or leveling, testing, and removal of packaging as well?
  • Haul-away conditions: Haul-away commonly runs on conditions shoppers learn too late. One major retailer's delivery guidance requires an adult aged 18 or older to be present for delivery, and its haul-away operates on a one-for-one basis. The old appliance should be empty and ready for removal if haul-away is selected — a refrigerator still full of food is a refrigerator that stays.
  • Fees: Get every fee named before paying: delivery, installation, haul-away, and any parts the installation will need. The sticker discount is meaningless until the full delivered-and-installed total is on the page.

The real price: haggling, fees, and financing traps

Appliances are one of the few Black Friday categories where the listed price is genuinely negotiable. In a Consumer Reports member survey conducted in 2024, 21 percent of major-appliance shoppers negotiated for a better price — and two-thirds of those who negotiated succeeded, saving a median of $123. The tactics behind the successes were ordinary: purchasing multiple items at once was the most common (used by 32 percent of successful negotiators), followed by mentioning prices offered by other retailers (26 percent) — and 19 percent used no special leverage at all; they simply asked. In a physical store, asking for a discount on a floor model also worked for a share of successful negotiators (8 percent).

If the price itself will not move, the fees might. Retail guidance and Consumer Reports both point the same way: ask whether delivery or shipping fees can be waived — many retailers already include free haul-away of the old appliance — and ask about installation-fee waivers, especially on a like-for-like replacement where the hookups already exist. A waived fee is the same money as a discount, with less resistance behind it.

The financing counter needs the opposite reflex: read it twice. Store-card appliance promotions are commonly structured as deferred interest — no interest if the balance is paid in full within the promotional period, described in retailer card terms as six months on purchases of $299 or more, with longer periods during special promotions. The trap is in the structure: under deferred interest, if the promotional balance is not paid in full by the deadline, interest is charged from the original purchase date — not from the deadline. A "no interest" offer that ends with a balance still owing can cost more than the Black Friday discount saved. If financing is used at all, the payoff date belongs on a calendar, set ahead of the deadline with room to spare.

The appliance buy-or-wait decision framework

With measurements done and logistics understood, the timing decision reduces to three questions. Is the current appliance dead or dying? A failed refrigerator or washer is not a discretionary purchase; buy as soon as a model that passes the measurement tests is available at a fair delivered price, because the cost of waiting is measured in spoiled food and laundromats, not dollars. Is this a planned upgrade? Then Black Friday week is a reasonable target — but the delivery date, not the order date, is the deadline that matters, so order early in the sale window rather than late, while delivery slots and installation appointments still exist. Is the deal only on a model that fails a test? A discount on an appliance that fails the path measurement, lacks the right hookup, or cannot be delivered when needed is not a deal at any percentage — walk away and let the framework, not the banner, make the call.

What success looks like

A successful appliance purchase ends boringly: the truck arrives on the scheduled day, the appliance travels the path it was measured for, the hookups match, the old unit leaves on the same visit, and the total paid matches the total that was confirmed before ordering — haggling wins and waived fees included. Every dramatic appliance story starts with a step in this guide that got skipped.
