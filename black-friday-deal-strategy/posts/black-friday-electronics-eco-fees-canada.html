Black Friday Electronics Eco Fees in Canada: The Line the Ad Price Leaves Out

A television in BC carries a published $4.50 eco fee at the till. In Ontario the fee is optional, set by each store, and taxed as part of the price. How the two systems work, the published BC table, and a worked example that redoes the percentage off against the real total.

$4.50. That is the published British Columbia environmental handling fee on a television in the 30 to 45 inch class, charged at the point of purchase under the EPRA schedule updated in May 2025. It will not appear in any Black Friday ad. It appears at the checkout, after the shopper has already decided the deal is good, and it is only one of several small lines that separate the advertised price from the real one. Electronics are the Black Friday category where this matters most, because televisions, computers, headphones and phones are exactly the products these fees attach to.

The complication is that Canada does not have one eco fee system. It has at least two very different ones, and which one applies depends on the province of the store, not the province on the shopper's screen.

Two provinces, two systems: Ontario discretion versus the BC schedule

Ontario first, because its rule surprises people. The Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority, the provincial regulator, states the position plainly: businesses in Ontario may choose to charge environmental fees to recover their recycling costs, and it is up to each business to decide whether to charge one and what amount to charge. RPRA does not charge the fee, does not mandate it, and does not set the amount. An environmental fee in Ontario is not a government tax, and it cannot be represented as mandatory, as a regulatory charge, or as an RPRA fee. A business can also skip the visible line entirely and build the cost into the price of the product, which Ontario's system expressly allows.

British Columbia runs the opposite design. EPRA, the Electronic Products Recycling Association, publishes a fee schedule by product category, and the fees are charged at the time of purchase. The shopper cannot negotiate them and the retailer did not invent them. Dollar Tree Canada's own fee page describes the general shape that holds across programs: the amount varies by product and province, it may be set by a recycling program, the brand owner, or a producer responsibility organization, and for in-person purchases it is charged based on the location of the store doing the selling.

One practical consequence follows. In Ontario, two stores selling the same television at the same advertised price can legitimately produce different checkout totals, because one shows a fee the other folded into its price. In BC, the fee line should match across stores, because the schedule, not the store, sets it.

Multiple products combined in a single manufacturer package under one SKU carry one fee, not one per item in the box. And Ontario has no equivalent table to publish: a 2021 CBC comparison reported by the Ontario explainer literature found fees on similar products ranging from under a dollar to more than $30, which is what a system with no set amounts looks like in practice.

Worked example: the fee, the tax on the fee, and the real percentage off

This is a worked example with labelled assumptions, not a live listing. Assume a television advertised in Ontario at $400, marked down from a crossed-out $550, an advertised saving of about 27%. Assume the store shows a separate environmental fee of $25, inside the range the 2021 comparison found. Two things happen at the till. The fee joins the bill. And because a separately listed fee counts as part of the price of the product, HST is charged on the fee itself: at Ontario's 13% HST, the $25 fee costs $28.25 with tax.

Now redo the deal math the way this site always does, against the total. The shopper pays $400 plus $25, which is $425 before tax, instead of the $400 the ad implied. Measured against the same $550 reference, the saving falls from $150 to $125, and the real discount is about 23%, not 27%. The fee did not kill the deal. It did move it by four points, which is the size of move that separates a genuine doorcrasher from a routine sale wearing a costume. In BC, run the same exercise with the schedule fee for the size class instead: $4.50 on this television, known in advance, identical at every store.

When no fee line appears, and what that means

A missing fee line does not mean a missing cost. Ontario's system gives the business two legal presentations: show the fee, or incorporate the recycling cost into the overall price, the same way materials and labour are incorporated. A store with no fee line may simply have chosen the second presentation. That has one shopping consequence worth keeping: a no-fee store is not automatically cheaper. Compare the final totals, because the store showing a $25 fee on a $400 television is charging $425, while the store showing no fee on a $430 television is charging $430.

The reverse trap also exists. If an Ontario receipt or salesperson describes the fee as a government tax or a mandatory provincial charge, that description conflicts with the regulator's own statement. RPRA directs consumers who believe a fee's purpose is being misrepresented to the Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery at 1-800-889-9768. The fee itself can be legitimate while its label is not.

The total-cost rule for comparing electronics deals

  1. Compare checkout totals, never advertised prices, for televisions, computers, phones and audio gear. The fee, where one is shown, is part of the total.
  2. In BC, expect the schedule fee and check it matches the size class. A fee far above the published amount for the category is a question to ask before paying.
  3. In Ontario, expect variation and do not reward or punish the line itself. A shown fee is a presentation choice; the total is the fact.
  4. Redo the percentage off against the total. If the recalculated saving no longer clears your buy threshold, the deal was the reference price, not the television.
  5. On a bundle, count the fee once per package SKU. A television and soundbar sold as separate items can each carry a fee; the same gear in one manufacturer package carries one.

None of this argues against buying the television. It argues for judging Black Friday electronics on the number that actually leaves the account, in the province where the till sits, with the small print counted before the crowd counts it for you.
