Black Friday Gift Card Deals: Bonus Cards, Expiry Traps, and the Honest Math

"Buy $50, get $10 free" sounds like 20% off. It is — for the part of the deal that expires first, is hardest to spend, and was designed to bring the buyer back in January. Here is how to value each kind of gift-card deal by what survives its terms.

Gift cards are the quiet Black Friday category: no queues, no doorbusters, and some of the season's most repeatable savings. They are also the category where the headline number lies most politely. Three genuinely different promotions share the "gift card deal" label, they carry different expiry law, different spending restrictions, and different real values — and the one with the biggest headline number is usually the one worth the least. Sorting them takes about a minute per offer, once the mechanics are clear.

The three kinds of gift-card deal

A discount on the card itself. The buyer pays less than face value — $95 for a $100 card, in the standard example. This is the simplest kind, because the saving is locked in at the till: the card holds full face value, and nothing about the saving depends on future behaviour. In 2025, gaming cards showed this form clearly, with 5% to 10% off PlayStation and Xbox gift cards at GameStop for Pro members and 15% off Roblox gift cards with Target Circle.

A bonus card. The buyer pays full price and receives a second, smaller card free — the classic restaurant pattern is a $10 or $15 bonus on every $50 of gift cards. In 2025, The Cheesecake Factory gave a $15 bonus card for every $50 in gift cards bought online, and Best Buy shoppers could receive a free $10 Best Buy gift card with the purchase of $50 in select specialty gift cards. The bonus is real money, but it is a different kind of money from the main card, as the expiry section below shows.

Store credit or a gift card bundled with a product. Some retailers give credit toward a future purchase when gift cards are bought from them, and some bundle a gift card with merchandise instead of discounting the product. Apple is the best-known example: in its 2025 four-day shopping event, running November 28 through December 1, eligible products came with an Apple Gift Card from $25 with AirPods 4 up to $250 with certain MacBook Pro models — with newer releases excluded, and the card usable only on a later purchase. Target ran the credit form in 2025 too, giving Circle members a $10 Target eGiftCard on $100 in select gift cards. In both forms, the "saving" is a future purchase at the same retailer, not a lower price today.

Bonus-card mechanics, in plain terms

The bonus structure is built to benefit the seller first, which is exactly why it can still be worth taking — as long as the buyer's eyes are open. From the seller's side, a bonus offer collects full price today: $100 paid in, a $25 bonus card out, versus $80 collected on a straight 20%-off sale. The bonus then brings the holder back during the quiet January-through-March stretch, and gift-card holders rarely spend exactly the card's value — in the TSG and Bank of America 2026 Consumer Gift Card Study, based on consumers' actual 2025 behaviour, 44% said they typically spend more than their card's value and another 48% spend about the same. For the buyer, the mechanics that matter are simpler: the bonus usually cannot be used in the same transaction, often cannot be used until after the holidays, and runs on a short clock. A bonus card is best treated as a coupon with a balance, not as money.

The expiry split: five years for purchased cards, weeks for bonus cards

This is the fact the whole category turns on. Under United States federal rules, money on a gift card a consumer paid for cannot expire for at least five years from the date the card was purchased, or from the last date money was loaded onto it — and if the date printed on the card is earlier, the money can be transferred to a replacement card at no cost. Inactivity fees are similarly restrained: they can be charged only after a card has gone unused for at least one year, and then only once per month. A discounted card bought on Black Friday therefore keeps its value safely into the next decade.

The free bonus card lives under different law. Gift certificates and cards distributed as part of award, loyalty, or promotional programs — where no money or other value was exchanged for that card — are exempt from those federal fee and expiration rules. Retailers use the freedom exactly as expected: the bonus promo card or holiday credit typically expires within 30 to 90 days, often by late January. The Cheesecake Factory's 2025 bonus shows the standard shape — earned in late November, redeemable only from January 1 through February 28, 2026. So one purchase produces two instruments: a purchased card protected for five years, and a promotional card that can vanish in eight weeks. Counting them at the same value is the trap.

Breakage: the discount that quietly disappears

Breakage is the industry term for card value that is paid for and never redeemed. It is why the bonus structure exists: every bonus card that expires unused converts a headline "20% back" promotion into a 0% discount that still brought a customer through the door in November. Breakage is not a rare edge case; it is the predictable outcome of short windows colliding with January. The buyer-side defense is behavioural, not legal: the moment a bonus card arrives, it gets a calendar reminder set a week before its expiry, and it gets spent on a purchase that was already planned — groceries, a regular restaurant, a household staple — rather than held for a special occasion that may land in March. A bonus redeemed against ordinary spending is worth its face value. A bonus saved for the perfect moment is worth the probability that the moment arrives inside the window, which is a much smaller number.

Read the table the way a skeptic would. The bonus offers beat the straight discount on paper — 16.7% and 23.1% against 10% — but their discount is conditional: it exists only if the bonus is redeemed, in full, inside its window, on spending that does not itself waste money. The straight discount is unconditional and survives five years of procrastination. The honest expected value of a bonus deal is therefore its headline rate multiplied by the buyer's honest probability of redeeming it. A shopper who knows a January purchase at that restaurant or retailer is already coming can count the bonus at close to full value. A shopper buying the card as a gift, where the bonus goes to someone else or gets forgotten in a drawer, should count it at close to zero — and on that basis the boring 10% discount wins easily.

When a gift-card deal beats a straight discount — and when it loses

The gift-card deal wins when three conditions line up: the redemption is certain (the card is for the buyer's own planned spending, or a recipient who shops there constantly), the timing is certain (a bonus window that covers a purchase already on the calendar), and no direct discount exists on the underlying goods. That third condition is the quiet giant: categories that rarely see straight discounts — game-platform credit, app-store credit, restaurant meals — can make a 10–15% card discount the only discount available all year, and stacking a discounted card on top of a later sale multiplies the effect. The gift-card deal loses when the goods themselves are discounted more deeply elsewhere, when the bonus window forces a purchase that would not otherwise happen (spending $60 to "save" $10 on things not needed is a $50 expense, not a saving), or when the card is bought from an untrusted source: the FTC's guidance is to buy from known and trusted sources and avoid online auction sites, where cards may be counterfeit or fraudulently obtained. Two final checks before any purchase: read the fine print for purchase, shipping, or post-purchase fees, and confirm whether the bonus can be used on sale items or only full-price ones — a bonus restricted to full-price goods during a store that is permanently on sale is worth less than its face.

FAQ

Do Black Friday bonus cards really expire that fast?

Yes, commonly. Because free promotional cards are exempt from the federal five-year rule, retailers set their own windows, and 30 to 90 days is typical — a card earned in late November may be redeemable only in January and February. The purchased card bought in the same transaction keeps the five-year protection. Always read the bonus card's own dates before counting its value.

Is a bonus card ever better than the same percentage as a straight discount?

Yes, when redemption is close to certain. A $15 bonus on $50 is a 23.1% effective discount if spent in full, which beats a 10% straight discount — provided it is spent on purchases that would have happened anyway, inside the window. The moment redemption becomes a coin toss, the guaranteed discount is worth more.

Can gift cards bought on Black Friday be combined with later sales?

Usually the purchased card can — it is a payment method, so it can pay for goods at whatever price they ring up at later, including sale prices, subject to the retailer's terms. Bonus and promotional credits are the restricted ones: they may exclude sale items, specific departments, or use in the same transaction. The terms on the specific offer decide.

What is the safest way to buy discounted gift cards?

Buy from the retailer itself or a known, trusted seller, keep the receipt and the card number recorded separately, and read the fine print for fees before paying. Avoid auction-site and resale listings for cards bought with real money at stake: counterfeit and fraudulently obtained cards are a documented risk, and a drained card has no five-year protection worth having.
