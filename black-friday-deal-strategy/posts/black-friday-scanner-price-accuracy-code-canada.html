Black Friday Scanner Price Accuracy Code: When the Till Price Beats the Shelf Tag in Canada

When an item scans higher than the shelf tag at a participating Canadian store, the Scanner Price Accuracy Code pays: free at $10 or less, $10 off above that. The three regimes, the exclusions, and the evidence routine to run before you queue on November 27, 2026.

Start with the misconception: when an item scans at a higher price than the tag on the shelf, the till wins and the shopper pays the scanned price or argues from memory. At a participating Canadian store, neither is true. The shopper has a named remedy, the Scanner Price Accuracy Code, and Black Friday is the one day of the year when it earns its keep, because Black Friday is when shelf tags, flyer prices and doorcrasher stickers change faster than staff can reconcile them. The Code is voluntary, it is managed by the Retail Council of Canada on behalf of retailers, and it has been endorsed by the Competition Bureau. It has run since June 2002, and participating merchants represent over 7,000 shops across Canada outside Quebec.

The catch is that the remedy only pays if the shopper can prove what the displayed price was. On a normal Tuesday that is easy. On Friday, November 27, 2026, with tags being swapped by the hour, proof is a routine, not an afterthought.

Three regimes: the voluntary code, Quebec, and self-checkout

Which rule protects you depends on where you are standing, literally. Outside Quebec, the voluntary Code applies at participating retailers. The Retail Council's own participant list includes Best Buy, Canadian Tire, Costco Wholesale Canada, The Home Depot Canada and Walmart Canada, plus 1,370 independent locations in the Canadian Federation of Independent Grocers. Participating shops post a sign about the Code near the entrance, and some post a notice at the point of sale as well.

Quebec runs on different footing. There, scanner price accuracy is regulated rather than voluntary, and since May 7, 2025 the maximum discount under the Quebec policy has been $15, not the $10 used by the voluntary Code. A shopper who crosses from Ottawa to Gatineau for a doorcrasher is shopping under a different regime with a different number.

Self-checkout is the third case, and it is the one Black Friday shoppers hit most. The Consumers' Council of Canada reports the Retail Council's position directly: it is impractical to put the sticker explaining the Code on a self-checkout, and shoppers who notice a discrepancy while scanning their own items can go to the customer service counter. The Code still backs the claim. The desk where it gets settled is just different.

The scope has edges, and Black Friday shoppers walk into two of them. The Code covers scanned UPC, bar coded and Price Look Up merchandise sold in stores. It does not cover individually price-ticketed items, the stickered clearance piece being the classic Black Friday example, and it does not cover goods that are not easily accessible to the public, such as prescription drugs and behind the counter cosmetics. Settlement.org's consumer summary adds two more working details: the final scanned price is the price shown during checkout after any rebates, coupons or discounts are applied, and a government regulated product such as tobacco is not eligible.

The evidence routine to run before you queue

The claim is won or lost at the shelf, before the till. Run this routine on any item where the deal matters:

  1. Photograph the shelf tag with the item in frame. The photo needs the displayed price, the product description and enough of the item to tie the two together. A price alone, with no product, proves nothing an hour later.
  2. Keep the flyer page or circle the item in the flyer. The Code's discount runs off the lowest advertised price, and a flyer price is an advertised price. On Black Friday the flyer and the shelf tag sometimes disagree, and that disagreement is exactly the case the Code exists for.
  3. Check the tag for a sticker on the item itself. If the piece carries its own price ticket, it sits outside the Code. Knowing that at the shelf saves a pointless claim at the desk.
  4. Watch the scan, item by item, on the big purchases. Even a one cent difference between the displayed price and the scanned price is the trigger the policy describes. Small errors on expensive items are still errors.
  5. Speak before you pay, not after. The cleanest claim happens while the transaction is open and the tag is still on the shelf for staff to check.

The claim, step by step, and what success looks like

  1. Name the displayed price and show the evidence. State the shelf or flyer price, show the photo or the flyer, and ask for the price to be corrected.
  2. Ask for the Scanner Price Accuracy Code adjustment by name. Staff are trained on the name. Asking for the Code turns a negotiation into a procedure: free if the displayed price was $10 or less, $10 off if it was over $10, on the first qualifying item.
  3. At self-checkout, finish or pause and go to the service counter. That is the Retail Council's own route for the self-scan case.
  4. Check the receipt before leaving. Confirm the corrected price and the discount both appear, and that identical follow-on items were charged at the corrected displayed price.
  5. If the store will not honour it, escalate with the record intact. The Scanner Price Accuracy Code Complaints Line is 1-866-499-4599. Keep the receipt, the photo and the time of the transaction.

What success looks like: the receipt shows the displayed price, not the scanned price, the free item or $10 discount appears as its own line, and the whole correction took one conversation because the evidence was captured at the shelf.

When the code does not help

Three honest limits. First, the store has to participate: the Code is voluntary outside Quebec, so check the entrance sign or the Retail Council's participant list before counting on it at an unfamiliar chain. Second, loyalty or membership pricing disputes and online prices are different problems; the Code governs scanned prices in stores. Third, the saving is capped by design: one free item or $10 off per qualifying product, not per unit in the cart. Treat the Code as what it is, a fairness backstop that rewards shoppers who watch the scan, and on Black Friday the shoppers who watch the scan are the ones the tag churn cannot quietly tax.
