Black Friday Smartphone Deals: Trade-In Math and the Carrier Lock-In Trap

A phone advertised at $0 is rarely a free phone. The bill arrives in monthly slices, the trade-in bonus arrives even more slowly, and a return-it offer can send one last bill at month 24. Here is the arithmetic that tells the truth.

The most expensive word in a Black Friday phone ad is "free." A handset listed at $0 upfront has not had its price deleted. The price has been moved — into a monthly financing charge, into a more expensive plan that the deal requires, and sometimes into a balance that comes due when the term ends. Shoppers who compare the $0 sticker against another carrier's $0 sticker are comparing the one number that carries almost no information.

Start instead with a blunt assumption: every carrier phone deal costs real money over 24 months, and the only honest question is how much, paid to whom, and on what conditions. That single shift in thinking dismantles most of the marketing before a flyer is even opened.

The trap first: where the "free" phone sends its bill

Carrier discounts usually arrive as monthly credits spread across the term, not as a cut to the price on day one. That structure has a consequence that rarely appears in the ad: leave the carrier early and the credits stop, while the remaining device balance is still owed. A discount that depends on staying for 24 months is not the same thing as a lower price, even when the two add up to the same total on paper.

There is a second trap stacked on top. Most carrier deals require a more expensive unlimited plan, so part of the "saving" on the handset is quietly handed back through the plan line every month. A shopper can win the phone math and lose the bill math in the same transaction.

Short version: the phone is financed, the discount is conditional, and the plan is part of the price.

The four numbers every carrier offer keeps on separate lines

Before any offer counts as a deal, pull these four figures onto one page:

  1. The monthly total, device plus plan together. A device charge quoted alone hides the plan the deal forces. One number, both parts, every month.
  2. The upfront payment. Small does not mean zero cost — it is still part of the 24-month total.
  3. The trade-in credit, and how it is paid. A credit spread monthly over the term is worth less, in flexibility, than the same amount taken off the price at the till, because leaving early can end the credits.
  4. The end-of-term balance on return-it offers. Programs such as Telus Bring-It-Back can list a phone at $0 upfront, but the offer carries a deferred amount tied to returning the device at the end of the term. Read that figure on the specific offer being considered and write it into the math. No honest total exists without it.

That fourth number is the one most often missing from roundups and comparison tables. An offer that cannot state its month-24 position — hand the phone back, pay a stated balance to keep it, or own it outright with nothing further due — is not ready to be signed.

Worked example: one phone, priced honestly over 24 months

PlanHub's Canadian Black Friday telco roundup, with prices as of October 21, 2026, lists a Fido offer for an iPhone 17 with 256 GB of storage: $100.66 a month, $25 upfront, a 20 GB plan, and a total of $2,440.84 over 24 months. That total can be checked line by line: $100.66 multiplied by 24 months comes to $2,415.84, and adding the $25 upfront payment gives $2,440.84. The arithmetic closes, which is exactly the test to apply to any offer — if a carrier's monthly figure, term, and upfront payment do not add up to its stated total, ask why before signing anything.

Notice what that $2,440.84 actually buys: a phone and two years of service, bundled into a single commitment. It cannot be compared against a bare handset price, and it cannot be compared against a rival's monthly device charge quoted without its plan. It can only be compared against another complete 24-month total, built the same way.

PlanHub's roundup also describes the pattern behind the headline offers: carriers cut device prices and pair them with plans that are also on sale, while the newest iPhones tend to get trade-in bonuses and lower financing rather than big outright price cuts. Translation for shoppers: on the newest model, the negotiation is happening in the credits and the financing, not in a slashed sticker price.

What a trade-in credit is worth, month by month

GizmoGrind's Black Friday phone-deal coverage puts the expected iPhone 16 trade-in range at Rogers, Telus, and Bell at $200 to $300 in bill credits, and notes that the deepest deals require a new activation plus a trade-in. Those are headline-sized numbers. Spread across a 24-month term, the arithmetic is smaller: $200 divided by 24 is about $8.33 a month, and $300 divided by 24 is $12.50 a month. That is the real monthly weight of the bonus — useful money, but not the kind that rescues an expensive plan.

Two comparisons belong beside any trade-in offer, and both have to be priced by the shopper from live listings, because they change constantly:

  • The private-sale comparison. What could the same old phone actually sell for, listed privately, in its real condition? If that figure beats the carrier credit — or comes close, with cash in hand instead of conditional monthly credits — the trade-in is a convenience purchase, not a saving.
  • The plan-premium comparison. What does the qualifying plan cost each month against a cheaper plan that covers the same usage? Multiply the monthly difference by 24. If the plan premium over the term eats the trade-in credit, the deal is a wash dressed as a win.

A trade-in also has a timing cost that never appears in the table: the old phone handed over in November cannot be sold, kept as a spare, or passed to a family member later. That option value is real, even if it is hard to price.

The lock-in test: pricing an exit at month 12

Run one scenario before signing: life changes at month 12 — a move, a lost phone, a better offer elsewhere — and the plan has to end. Under the credit structure described above, leaving stops the remaining monthly credits, and the remaining device balance is owed. Half the promised discount can evaporate at exactly the moment money is already tight.

So ask the carrier two questions and write down the answers: what balance would be owed on the device if the account closed at month 12, and how much of the trade-in credit would still be unpaid at that point? An offer that survives those answers is a deal. An offer that only works if nothing changes for two full years is a bet.

Verdict: who should take the carrier deal anyway

Carrier financing can still be the right call, under clear conditions. Take it when the complete 24-month total — device, qualifying plan, upfront payment, and any return balance priced in — beats the unlocked route that has been priced just as completely. Take it when staying with that carrier for the full term is a near-certainty, so the monthly credits will actually arrive. And take it when the trade-in credit clearly beats what the old phone would fetch sold privately, after the plan premium has been subtracted.

Skip it when the saving exists only in the headline credit, when the qualifying plan costs more than the usage justifies, or when the return-it balance on the offer cannot be found in writing. A missing number is an answer, too.
