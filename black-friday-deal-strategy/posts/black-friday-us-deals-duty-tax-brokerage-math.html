Black Friday US Deals for Canadians: Duty, Tax and Brokerage Math Before You Click Buy

The CBSA bands are $20 by mail and $40 and $150 by courier from the US, assessed on the whole shipment in Canadian dollars, plus Canada Post charges $9.95 per dutiable mail item. The threshold table, the split order trap, and mail versus courier worked examples.

A US Black Friday price is the start of a bill, not the bill. For a Canadian shopper, the final cost of an order from the United States is set by three things the product page never shows together: which customs threshold band the shipment lands in, whether it travels by mail or by courier, and what the carrier adds for doing the border paperwork. The good news is that the first two are published numbers, and the whole calculation takes about two minutes per cart, before the sale countdown starts pressuring the decision.

The threshold table: mail, courier, and the two CUSMA bands

Every figure in this section comes from the Canada Border Services Agency. All of the agency's amounts are in Canadian dollars and refer to the value for duty, the dollar amount used to calculate what is owed on goods being imported. That single definition quietly decides most outcomes, because a US$120 deal is not a $120 shipment until it is converted.

The courier bands are the CUSMA benefit, in force since the agreement took effect on July 1, 2020. The CBSA's Courier Imports Remission Order memorandum fills in the conditions shoppers miss: the duty remission runs to a value for duty of $150 or less and the tax remission to $40 or less, the goods do not need to originate in a CUSMA country, but they must be shipped from the US or Mexico and must have entered into the commerce of one of those countries before shipping. Goods merely transshipped through the US, that never entered US commerce, fall back to the $20 threshold. Some goods get no relief in any band: the CBSA names alcohol, cannabis products, cigars, cigarettes, tobacco and publications among the exceptions.

The traps inside the thresholds: whole shipment value and split orders

Trap one is arithmetic. The threshold applies to the whole shipment's value for duty, not to each item's price. Two items at $90 Canadian each, shipped together from the US by courier, are a $180 shipment, over the duty line, even though each item looks safely mid-band on its own. Black Friday carts are built item by item and assessed shipment by shipment, which is why the surprise happens.

Trap two is the workaround that does not work. Splitting one order into smaller parcels to stay under a threshold does not create separate low value shipments in the CBSA's treatment: a split transaction is not treated as several small shipments. If the split also doubles the shipping charges, the shopper has paid more to owe the same duty.

Trap three is origin confusion. A deal on a US retailer's site is not automatically a US origin shipment, and above $150 what decides the duty rate is the goods themselves. Goods that qualify as originating under CUSMA can often enter at a 0% duty rate, with tax still applying, while goods made elsewhere pay their regular rate. Canada also applies additional surtaxes to a list of US origin goods, and that list changes; check the CBSA's current list before committing to a high value US order, rather than relying on any guide's snapshot of it.

Worked examples: the same deal by mail and by courier

Worked examples with labelled assumptions, not live listings. Assume an Ontario shopper, so tax is 13% HST, and assume a US deal priced so its value for duty converts to exactly $100 Canadian.

By courier from the US: $100 sits in the $40 to $150 band. Duty is remitted, tax is not. The border adds 13% of $100, which is $13, for a landed merchandise cost of $113, before the courier's own clearance charges, which the next section covers.

By mail from the US: the mail line is $20, so the same $100 item is fully dutiable and taxable. Assume the goods carry a 0% duty rate, the friendliest case. Tax is still $13, and Canada Post adds its handling fee of $9.95 per dutiable or taxable mail item, published on Canada Post's own customs page. The same deal lands at $122.95 before shipping, $9.95 worse than the courier route on identical goods, entirely because of the route.

Now scale the cart. At a value for duty of $180 Canadian by courier, the shipment is over the duty line: duty at the goods' own rate joins the bill, and tax is calculated on the value with duty included. The lesson is not the exact rate, which depends on the product. The lesson is that the $150 line is a cliff the cart crosses one add-on item at a time, so the time to check the running Canadian total is while the cart is still editable.

Brokerage and handling: the charges that are not duty

Duty and tax are the government's lines. Brokerage and handling are the carrier's, and they are separate charges on top. On the mail route the figure is published and flat: Canada Post charges $9.95 per dutiable or taxable mail item, collects it online through tracking or at delivery, and charges nothing on a duty free, tax exempt item. On the courier route, the courier typically acts as the customs broker and charges for the clearance, and the amount varies by carrier and by service level. No carrier schedule was verified for this guide, so no figure is quoted here; the working rule is to find the clearance charge on the carrier's site before choosing the shipping speed, because ground services are where the unpleasant versions of this fee usually live. A deal that only survives at the slowest shipping tier needs its brokerage counted before it is called a deal.

The pre-click checklist and the decision rule

  1. Convert the whole cart to Canadian dollars first. The bands are assessed on value for duty in Canadian dollars, per shipment.
  2. Name the route before the band. Mail means the $20 line and a possible $9.95 handling fee. Courier from the US means the $40 and $150 bands.
  3. Price the band you are actually in. Under $40 by courier: no duty, no tax. To $150: tax on the full value. Over $150: duty at the goods' rate, then tax.
  4. Add the carrier's charge. Published $9.95 on mail; the carrier's clearance fee on courier, checked on the carrier's site.
  5. Compare the landed total with the Canadian price of the same item, including any Canadian sale running the same week. Buy the US deal only if the landed total still wins by a margin worth the return hassle, because a cross-border return is the most expensive kind.

What success looks like: no package arrives with a surprise amount owing, because every US order in the cart was converted, banded and totalled before the buy button was pressed. US Black Friday deals can absolutely beat Canadian prices. They just have to win after the border, not before it.
