Bag-building games, explained

Mechanics · 8 min read

Two of the ten tokens in your bag are the ones your plan depends on. Your first draw gives you a 2 in 10 chance of finding one. Pull a red token out and the next draw drops to 1 in 9; pull anything else and it rises to 2 in 9. That small piece of maths, worked through in a recent comparison of deck and bag building at Co-op Board Games, is the whole genre in miniature. Bag building never lets you control the draw. It lets you control the odds the draw is made from, one purchase at a time, and it punishes players who buy exciting tokens without watching what those tokens do to the ratios.

What bag building actually is

A bag-building game gives each player a bag of identically shaped pieces, tokens, cubes or tiles that feel the same to the fingers, and lets players change what is inside it. One widely used definition puts it plainly: players add identically shaped items to a bag to alter the contents and the probabilities of drawing from it. BoardGameGeek files the idea under one broader mechanism, deck, bag and pool building, because the shared loop is identical in each form. Start with a modest pool of components. Add, remove or upgrade pieces as the game runs. Draw a subset of the pool when it is time to act. Repeat, with a pool that keeps changing because of your choices.

The early reference example shows both halves of the loop, the building and the rot. In Automobiles, players draw coloured cubes from a bag and spend them on actions such as moving a car around the track, and they spend money to add cubes that unlock stronger actions. As the race rolls on, the same bags accumulate wear cubes, and a drawn wear cube limits the turn by removing a useful action. A bag is never just a record of your good decisions. It is a record of all of them.

Three cycles, three different games

The label hides a split that matters more than theme: what happens to the pieces after you draw them. In Orléans, the official rules have players draw character tiles from a Followers Bag onto their market and place them, and the cycle runs as draw, place, return under the rules, with tiles going back into the bag at the end of every round. A follower you recruit this round can be working again next round, so purchases pay off quickly and often.

The Quacks of Quedlinburg runs a different clock. All players draw chips from their bags one at a time, simultaneously, placing each chip in a pot and deciding after every draw whether to stop or keep going. The brake is a bust limit: the white cherry bomb chips every player starts with carry values, and if the combined white value in your pot exceeds seven, the potion explodes. There is no round structure to rescue a greedy draw, because the risk decision is the round.

Altiplano stretches the cycle further. Drawn tiles go into a container rather than back into the bag, and the bag is refilled only once it runs empty. A purchase there can sit out of reach for a long stretch, which rewards players who buy early and punishes anyone who stocks up late. Same mechanism on the box, three tempos at the table, and tempo decides how a new player should spend.

Two bags, verified: Quacks and Orléans

The Quacks of Quedlinburg was designed by Wolfgang Warsch, published by Schmidt Spiele in 2018, and won the Kennerspiel des Jahres that year. It supports 2 to 4 players aged 10 and up, and sessions run approximately 45 minutes across nine rounds. Every player starts with an identical bag containing white cherry bomb chips, one orange chip and one green chip, which is why the game teaches the mechanism so cleanly. Scoring softens the lesson with one sharp edge: a player whose pot explodes must choose victory points or coins, not both. Coins buy new ingredients in the buying phase, one or two chips of different colours each round, and new chips go directly into the bag for future draws. Price check, dated 4 November 2026: Board Game Bliss in Richmond Hill, Ontario listed the CMYK edition of Quacks at C$49.95, shown as sold out at the time of checking, so treat that figure as the going Canadian list price rather than a live offer.

Orléans, designed by Reiner Stockhausen and published by dlp games in 2014, is the heavier reference. The game runs 18 rounds, each player starts with a Followers Bag and four starter followers, a Farmer, a Boatman, a Craftsman and a Trader, and the number of followers you may draw is set by your marker on the Knights Track, beginning at four. The rules also let you look inside your bag at any time to count what is in there, a small permission that changes the game: the composition is public knowledge to its owner, and only the order is hidden. Check the spec line before buying, because sources disagree about the seat count. A rules aggregate lists 2 to 5 players, about 90 minutes and age 12 and up, while the Bombard Games review lists 2 to 4 players at the same 90 minutes. Both agree on the weight of the game, so plan a full evening session either way, and confirm the count on the edition you are buying. Price check, dated 4 November 2026: Board Game Bliss listed the Orléans Capstone Games edition at C$68.95.

The dilution rule, and how to shop the category

Here is the rule the definitions never give you. A stronger pool and a more consistent pool are not the same thing. Picture a pool of eight components, two of which your strategy needs. Add a ninth component that is generally useful and your overall capability rises, but the probability of drawing one of the two critical pieces falls on every single draw. You have diluted the good stuff. Seasoned builders therefore judge a pool on three dimensions at once: acquisition, which new pieces strengthen the mix; removal, which weak pieces drag the average down; and concentration, how many copies of a key piece are worth holding. Refusing a shiny purchase is a move, and in this category it is often the winning one.

Shopping follows from the same rule. First, pick your cycle before you pick a theme: bust pressure for a loud, fast table, return each round for planners who want purchases working immediately, refill when empty for players who think in long arcs. Second, ask how much of the randomness you can inspect. A game that lets you count your bag between turns rewards ratio thinking; a blind draw against a bust limit rewards nerve. Third, verify the edition on the publisher or retailer page, player count, length and whether the box is a base game, and note the dated prices above before comparing listings, because stock and editions move. This topic does not suit a numbered how-to procedure, there is no device to fix or setting to configure, so the dilution rule stands in its place: before any purchase, name what the new piece does to the odds of drawing the pieces your plan already needs.

Buy the bag whose odds you want to argue with. A deck shows you its discard pile and lets you count what is left; a bag keeps its order secret and asks you to think in ratios instead. Players who love that argument, purchase by purchase, draw by draw, tend to keep these games on the table for years.
