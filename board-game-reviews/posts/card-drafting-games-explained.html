Card drafting games, explained

Mechanics · 9 min read

Dealing cards is a lottery: the deck decides, and the players cope. Drafting is the designer's answer to the coping. Instead of being handed a hand and making the best of it, every player is shown a menu, chooses one item from it, and hands the rest of the menu to a neighbour — who makes the same choice, from a menu one item smaller, knowing that the leftovers come back around. Nobody controls the whole distribution, but everybody touches it. The result is a mechanic that feels fair in a way a shuffle never does, because a bad final hand in a draft is partly a record of your own earlier decisions, and everyone at the table watched you make them.

This guide explains what drafting actually is, sorts its three structures by the question that decides how each one feels — who can see the options before choosing — verifies the two anchor games against their published specifications, and finishes with a pick procedure and an honest account of when taking a card purely to deny an opponent is a good play and when it is just spite with extra steps.

What drafting actually is

Drafting is a way of distributing cards, tiles, dice, or other game elements among competitors from a common pool, through an ordered selection process in which players genuinely choose. That last clause is the boundary line, and reference definitions draw it firmly: drawing a card from a deck in Uno is not drafting, because there is no selection; buying the Monopoly property you happened to land on is not drafting either, because the landing was not chosen. A draft requires a pool, a choice from that pool, and the consequence that what you leave behind goes to someone else.

That consequence is the whole mechanic. In an ordinary card game, an unplayed card in your hand is simply yours and useless to discuss. In a draft, every card you decline is a card you have effectively dealt to a specific opponent — the next player in the pass direction, first, and then around the table. Drafting turns distribution into interaction without adding a single trading, negotiation, or combat rule.

Sort by visibility: closed, open, and single-hand drafting

Explainers tend to sort drafting games by theme or weight. Sort by information instead, and three structures appear, each with its own tempo and its own kind of tension.

Closed drafting — pick and pass. Each player is dealt the same number of cards, selects one (or occasionally more) to keep, and passes the rest to the next player, typically to the left. Play continues until all cards are selected or discarded. The defining feature is privacy: the pool available to the next player is unknown until the hand arrives, so each player sees only their current hand, never the full communal pool. Picks are usually simultaneous, which is why this structure scales so gracefully — seven players draft in roughly the time three do, because nobody waits for a turn.

Open drafting — the shared pool. All options sit face-up in the centre, and players take turns selecting from them; once an item is taken it is removed from the pool, or sometimes replaced. Everyone sees the full pool and every pick as it happens, which produces a slower, more openly tactical game: anticipation and adaptation are public, and a denial pick is a visible act the whole table can price.

Single-hand rotation. Only one hand exists. One player takes a card from it and passes it on, until all players have cards. Closed-drafting references give Citadels as the working example, and they flag the cost honestly: the structure is strongly biased toward the first player, so it needs thematic and balance support to stay fair. It survives because it is fast, dramatic, and easy to teach — the bias is the price of the theatre.

Sushi Go is the distilled form. Each player starts a round with a hand of 7 cards; everyone picks one card simultaneously, places it face-down, reveals together, and passes the remaining 6 to the next player. Hands shrink by one card with every pass while each player's pile of picks grows by one, until nothing is left to pass. Points are tallied, the cards are reshuffled, and two more drafts follow — three rounds in total. There is nothing else in the tin to learn, which is precisely the point: every skill the full genre uses — reading a hand for sets, remembering what was passed, guessing what will come back — is present, at a fifteen-minute price.

7 Wonders is the same engine carrying a civilisation game. Players pick simultaneously and reveal together, exactly as in Sushi Go, but the chosen cards become a permanent tableau of resources, buildings, and military strength across three ages. Two procedural details reward attention. The pass direction alternates by age — clockwise, then counter-clockwise, then clockwise — so the neighbour feeding you hands changes, and with it the value of every denial. And a hand does not draft to zero: play repeats until 2 cards remain in each hand, and those last 2 are discarded, which means the weakest cards in each hand are removed by the structure itself rather than forced onto the last seats. The game won the inaugural Kennerspiel des Jahres connoisseurs' award in 2011, and its 2–7 player range at a steady 30 minutes is the closed-draft scaling argument made by a box.

A pick procedure for a hand you will only see once

A hand in a closed draft is a one-time offer: what is not taken now travels away, and only the leftovers return. This procedure keeps the decision honest:

1. Score the hand against your tableau first. What does each card complete, start, or feed? A card that finishes a scoring set outranks a card that merely looks strong in isolation.

2. Check what the hand will look like when it returns. Count the picks between now and the hand's next visit — one per player — and assume the best cards for the seats in between will be gone. Want a card back? Take it now, or price it as already lost.

3. Only then, price the denial. If two cards are near-equal for you and one is clearly worth much more to the next player in the pass direction, take the denial. If it costs you a set, it is not a denial, it is a donation in the other direction.

4. Reveal and note what the table took. Simultaneous reveals are free information about every opponent's plan; the player collecting a visible set is telling the table how to value the next hand.

Success check: at scoring, your tableau should read as a small number of deliberate sets rather than one of everything. A draft where every pick answered step 1 before step 3 produces exactly that shape.

When hate-drafting actually pays

Hate-drafting — taking an item that is not optimal for you because it is crucial to an opponent, denying them that piece — is named in nearly every explainer and analysed in almost none. The reference analysis is blunt about the variable that decides it: player count. At two players, hate-drafting is at its strongest, because gaining a point and denying an opponent a point are the same move — every card you deny is a card a known rival, the only rival, does not get. As player count rises, the denied card usually had to survive several other picks to reach the opponent you fear, and the card you spent on denial was a real cost to your own tableau — which is why analysis of the mechanic concludes that at higher counts it slides from viable strategy toward griefing. Add the pass-direction wrinkle from 7 Wonders: you can only meaningfully deny the players downstream of you in the current direction, and that direction flips next age. Denial is a local, temporary, priced tool. Treat it that way and it wins games; treat it as a philosophy and it loses them politely.

How to read a drafting game before you buy it

Three checks, in order. First, the structure, from the comparison table: closed for simultaneous speed and gentle interaction, open for public tactics, single-hand for theatre with a first-player tax. Second, the honest seat count: closed drafters genuinely hold their playing time at the top of the player range, so a 2–7 claim on a closed draft means something a sequential game cannot promise. Third, what the draft feeds. In Sushi Go the draft is the whole game and scoring is immediate; in 7 Wonders the draft feeds resources, military comparison, and long-term building, so picks compound. Groups learning the mechanic should start where the feedback is immediate, then graduate to the compounding version.

FAQ

Is drafting the same as deck-building? No — they are near-opposites in timing. Drafting distributes existing cards among all players before or during play; deck-building has each player privately growing a personal deck during play, usually by buying from a shared market. Our deck-building guide draws the line in full. A booster-pack draft for a collectible card game is a third thing again: a draft whose prize is a deck you then play a separate match with.

Why does the pass direction change? To share both the advantage and the information. The player downstream of you sees your leavings first; alternating direction between rounds or ages means no single neighbour feeds on your rejects — or starves on them — for the whole game.

What is a gentle first drafting game? On the verified specifications above, Sushi Go: the draft is the entire game, a round is a hand of 7 cards, scoring is visible set collection, and three rounds take about 15 minutes. 7 Wonders is the natural second step for a group that wants the same pick-and-pass rhythm to build something that lasts all evening.

Drafting endures because it makes distribution itself the game: every hand is a small argument about value, conducted in silence, one card at a time. Learn to read the hand in front of you — and the direction it is travelling — and the argument starts going your way.
