Hidden movement games, explained

Mechanics · 9 min read

Where is the other player? In a hidden movement game, that question has a timetable. One or more players move their character secretly, usually by jotting the position down on a scrap of paper or a concealed log, while everyone else works from whatever clues the game agrees to drip out. Dicebreaker, in its rundown of the genre, calls the search for Mister X in Scotland Yard the best introduction to hidden movement decades after release, and the reason is the drip itself. The hidden player is never fully invisible. The game is the schedule on which they almost become visible, and each design sets that schedule differently.

What hidden movement actually is

The standard shape is asymmetric and one against many. Scotland Yard, published by Ravensburger and played on a map of central London dotted with numbered stations, gives one player the role of Mr X, a fugitive whose movements are mostly hidden, while the remaining players cooperate as detectives who move in the open. All of the detectives are visible on the board at all times. The only sign of Mr X is a historical marker, his last known location at the moment he last surfaced, which is usually where he no longer is.

Capture is physical, not voted. A detective who moves onto the space Mr X currently occupies catches him, and Mr X also loses if he moves onto a space a detective occupies. That single rule turns the hunt into geometry: the detectives win by shrinking the map until every legal move lands on one of their pawns, and Mr X wins by keeping one honest route open until the game runs out.

Three ways hunters get their clues

Compare designs by their clue system and the whole genre sorts itself. Scotland Yard uses scheduled surfacing plus a tell on every move. Mr X records each destination in his travel log and covers the entry with the ticket he used, so the detectives always learn his mode of transport and never his station, until the log forces him to place his pawn on the board after his 3rd, 8th, 13th, 18th and 24th moves. The transport tell carries real information, because the three modes trade range against predictability: taxis have nearly no range but can reach every corner of the map, Underground lines travel farther with only a handful of predictable stops, and buses fall in between. The farther Mr X runs, the more the ticket in the log narrows the search.

Letters from Whitechapel replaces the schedule with questions. Jack records his moves on a concealed notepad, and the police earn information by asking: when a police pawn searches a spot, Jack must answer truthfully whether he has been on that spot, and a confirmed visit is marked with a clue token. Each pawn can search adjacent locations one at a time and find at most one clue per turn, or it can spend the turn attempting an arrest instead, so every question has an opportunity cost measured in board position.

Fury of Dracula hides the route rather than the timetable. In the editions from 2006 onward, Dracula moves by building a trail of face down location cards, each card a stop on the map of late 19th century Europe, and the fourth edition keeps that trail at six spaces long. The hunters are not waiting for a reveal moment. They are excavating a path, and what they uncover is where Dracula has been, in order, which tells them where his options now point.

Scotland Yard, verified, editions and all

Spec verification matters here because two different games share one name. The original was first published by Ravensburger in 1983 and won the Spiel des Jahres that year. Its publication data lists 3 to 6 players, age 10 and up, and a playing time of about 1 hour, player dependent. The current standard Ravensburger edition, the 2025 edition with product number 27514 in the ClickDo spec roundup, lists 2 to 6 players, age 8 and up, and approximately 45 minutes, with a beginner friendly rules option added. Ravensburger publishes the game in Canada as well as most of Europe, so Canadian listings usually describe the modern edition, but a second hand copy may be the older one. Count the surfacing spaces on the travel log before assuming which game you own.

The setup numbers explain the balance. The detective side always fields five detective pawns, shared out among the detective players, and each pawn carries its own ticket stack of 10 taxi, 8 bus and 4 Underground tickets, kept separate per pawn even when one player runs several pawns. Mr X starts leaner and stranger: 4 taxi, 3 bus and 3 Underground tickets, plus 5 black tickets and 2 double move tickets. He also hides behind a visor while writing in the travel log, small theatre that does real work, because the log is the only complete record of the game and it belongs to the fugitive.

The ticket clock nobody mentions

Listicles describe the hunt and skip the economy that ends it. Detective tickets are finite and they are never replaced. When a detective moves, the spent ticket is handed to the Mr X player, so the hunting team literally funds its own quarry with every journey, and generous detective travel makes the fugitive's job easier. The game therefore carries a built in timer: Mr X wins if he survives through round 24, or sooner if the detectives run out of usable tickets and can no longer move. Detectives who scatter across London on Underground tickets early are not just badly placed. They are burning the clock and arming the other side, which is why experienced detective teams argue about ticket spend before they argue about geography.

Picking a hidden movement game your group will finish

Decide on four things. First, session length, honestly stated: the current Scotland Yard asks about 45 minutes, individual hunts can end in roughly 15 minutes on an early catch or stretch to an hour, Letters from Whitechapel lists 90 minutes in one source and 120 in another, so budget the longer figure, and Fury of Dracula asks 120 to 180 minutes, with the fourth edition reviewed at around three hours and priced at US$59.95 in that review. Second, debate tolerance: the detective side plays as a committee, and rules summaries of Scotland Yard flag that long detective debates slow the game down, so a group that loves a planning argument is a feature here, not a flaw, provided the hidden player enjoys the wait. Third, the record keeper: give the hidden role to the player who will keep a meticulous log, since a single misrecorded station quietly breaks the deduction for everyone. Fourth, the clue system your group actually wants: scheduled reveals for a brisk family hunt, truthful questions for detectives who want to interrogate the map, a trail of cards for a long evening of route reading. This topic does not suit a numbered how-to procedure, there is no setting to configure or device to fix, so the framework above stands in its place: match the information schedule to your group's patience before you match a theme to its taste.

The genre endures because the hidden player is doing the simplest job at the table, moving one pawn in secret, while everyone else does the hardest, turning a timetable of almost clues into a net. Pick the drip your group enjoys, and the vanishing act works every time.

— Rex Bennett, Entertainment Writer
