Party games for bigger groups

Recommendations · 6 min read

Game night with six, eight, ten people is a different animal from a quiet evening for two. The games that work at this scale are a distinct breed, and buying them requires a different eye. A brilliant strategy game can die on arrival in a room of eight, while a game that looks almost too simple can produce the night's biggest laughs. Here's how to think about it.

What a party game actually needs to do

Party games have one job that matters more than all others: keep everyone involved. At a big table, downtime is the enemy. The moment three people are waiting while two others take a long turn, phones come out and the night deflates. Good party games solve this by making turns simultaneous, or by making watching other people's turns as fun as taking your own.

The second job is onboarding speed. At a party you're teaching a mixed group — gamers, non-gamers, people who wandered in from the kitchen. If the teach takes more than a few minutes, you've lost the room before the first turn. The best party games can be explained while people are still sitting down.

The third is social fuel. A party game's real engine is the conversation it generates: the arguments, the accusations, the surprise reveals. Mechanics matter less than moments. You're shopping for a game that gives people stories to retell the next day.

Match the game to the room

Not every group wants the same kind of party game, and this is where most recommendations go wrong. Think about your specific crowd:

The competitive crew wants teams, scoring, and bragging rights. Team-based word games are the classic answer here — Codenames, where two teams race to identify their secret words from one-word clues, remains the gold standard for a reason. Splitting a big group into teams keeps everyone invested and gives the night a natural arc.

The creative crowd wants to make things. Drawing-and-guessing games like Telestrations — a mashup of telephone and sketching where drawings degrade hilariously as they pass around the table — generate the kind of laughter that has people crying. These work best with groups that don't take themselves too seriously.

The low-key hangout doesn't want a spotlight on anyone. Cooperative party games fit here: Just One, where everyone writes one-word clues to help a guesser identify a mystery word (duplicate clues get canceled), keeps the vibe collaborative instead of performative. Nobody gets put on the spot alone.

The debate club wants opinions and arguments. Wavelength, where teams guess where a hidden target sits on a spectrum between two opposites (like "hot" vs. "cold"), turns out to be a machine for generating passionate, hilarious disagreements about things nobody actually cares about.

Handling the tricky stuff

Player elimination. Avoid it at parties. A game that knocks people out early leaves them spectating for an hour, which is the fastest way to kill a game night. If a game has elimination, check whether eliminated players stay involved somehow — and if not, save it for smaller groups.

Uneven player counts. People arrive late and leave early. Games that handle a flexible player count — or at least let someone join between rounds without a full restart — are worth their weight in gold at parties. Short rounds with natural break points beat one long epic session.

The shy player. Every group has someone who dreads being the center of attention. Simultaneous-play games and team formats protect them; games built around one person performing while everyone watches do not. If your group has shy players, lean cooperative or team-based.

Building a small party-game shelf

You don't need many. Three or four well-chosen party games cover nearly every situation: one team word game, one creative/drawing game, one cooperative game, and maybe one social-deduction game for groups that enjoy bluffing and arguing (Spyfall-style hidden-role games fit here, where most players know a secret location and one player is faking it). Rotate based on the room, and don't be afraid to retire a game the group has played out — party games burn bright and fade fast, and that's fine.

One last piece of hosting advice: be the teacher, and teach with energy. A party game's success is about 60% the game and 40% the person explaining it. Learn the rules before guests arrive, start with the fun part of the explanation, and get people playing within minutes. A mediocre game taught well beats a great game taught badly every single time.
