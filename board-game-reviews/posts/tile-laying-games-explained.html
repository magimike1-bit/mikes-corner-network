Tile-laying games, explained

Hobby basics · 8 min read

One mechanic, three different games

Tile-laying games — tile placement, in the catalogues — all start from the same physical act: a flat, rigid piece goes down on the table and the board changes because of it. Board Game Rules describes the mechanic exactly that way: players lay pieces to build a shared map, expand personal boards, or trigger immediate spatial rewards, and every placement alters the board state, creating patterns, connections, or scoring conditions. That definition is accurate, and it is also why the label confuses buyers. Hidden inside it are three structures that play nothing alike.

The first structure is the shared map: everyone builds one landscape together, and every tile you place is a tile your opponents can use. The second is the personal board: your tiles form your own landscape, and other players can only hurt you at the drafting stage, by taking the tile you wanted. The third is pattern-building: tiles fill a personal grid against a fixed scoring pattern, and the puzzle is sequencing, not geography. Same handful of cardboard, three different kinds of tension. Knowing which structure a box contains tells you more about your evening than the theme printed on it.

A typical tile-laying turn, whatever the structure, runs through the same five beats: acquire a tile (draw it, draft it from a display, or buy it), evaluate the legal positions against the game's placement rules, rotate and place it, trigger whatever spatial effects the placement creates, and score anything the placement completed. The games diverge in how much weight each beat carries. In a shared-map game the evaluation beat is the game. In a pattern game the acquisition beat is. This guide separates the structures, verifies the reference design for each, and ends with a test you can run on any box in a store.

The shared map: Carcassonne and the claim decision

Carcassonne is the reference design for shared-map tile laying, and its structure explains the whole family. Each turn a player lays one tile into a common landscape, and may then place a wooden figure — a meeple — on that tile to claim a feature: placed on a road it claims the road, in a city it claims the city, on a monastery it claims the monastery, and laid down in a field it claims the farm. The crucial rule, the one that creates the game's tension, is that a meeple cannot be reclaimed until its feature is complete. Your claim tokens are a small, slowly refreshing resource, and every claim is a commitment.

The placement rules are matching rules: roads must connect to other roads, fields to fields, and city walls to other city walls, in the words of the Cardboard Empire retrospective on the game. That sounds like a jigsaw, and visually it is one — what starts as nothing becomes, tile by tile, a busy map that is different every game. But the matching constraint is also the attack surface. Because the map is shared, a placement can extend your city, block an opponent's road, or — the move beginners miss — join two separately claimed features into one, suddenly sharing (or stealing) a score that looked safe.

This is the decision no generic explainer names: placing the tile and claiming the feature are two separate decisions, and the second is usually the harder one. A tile can be legally placed and still be a bad move — it completes a feature you cannot claim because your meeples are stranded elsewhere, or it hands an opponent the exact edge their city needed. The Meeples and Wine podcast's Carcassonne tips point at the same discipline from the player's side: look for placements that score in more than one way, and save meeples for the later stages when the scoring opportunities are bigger. A claim token spent on turn three is a token that cannot answer the board on turn thirty.

The personal board: Cascadia and the drafting fight

Cascadia shows what changes when the map is yours alone. On a turn, a player drafts one of four offered pairs — a habitat tile plus a wildlife token — places the tile adjacent to a tile already in their own landscape, and places the token on a tile showing the matching animal icon. In a deliberate contrast with Carcassonne, the habitats on adjacent tiles do not need to match. Mismatching is legal; it is simply worth less, because scoring rewards the biggest single connected grouping of each habitat type, with endgame bonuses for the largest and second-largest groupings in each habitat.

Moving the map from shared to personal deletes the blocking game entirely — nobody can place a tile in your landscape — and moves all the conflict upstream, into the draft. The four pairs are public. Taking the tile you need is defence as much as construction when the same pair completes an opponent's habitat corridor. The puzzle also inverts: instead of asking where a tile may legally go, you ask which of your habitats you are committing to grow, because only the single biggest grouping of each type scores. A landscape of balanced medium habitats is worth less than it looks; the scoring table quietly rewards conviction.

Neither structure is the advanced or the simple one. They load different skills. Shared-map play rewards reading other people's plans off a common board and tolerating direct interference. Personal-board play rewards planning under a public draft and punishing nobody but yourself. Groups that bounce off Carcassonne's feature-stealing often love Cascadia's calm, and groups that find Cascadia solitary usually want the shared map back. Buy the interaction you actually enjoy, not the theme you prefer.

Pattern-building, the boundary case

The third structure barely involves geography at all. In pattern games — Azul is the name the podcast tips reach for — tiles are drafted into a personal grid where each position has a fixed place in a scoring pattern, and the skill is sequencing: planning ahead so one placement advances multiple patterns at once, as the Meeples and Wine episode puts it. There is no map, no adjacency puzzle in the landscape sense, and no claiming. Shelves and recommendation threads file these with tile-laying games because the components look identical, which is exactly how buyers end up with a quiet abstract puzzle when they wanted a map-building fight.

The honest way to treat the boundary is as a spectrum of what a placement does. At one end it changes a shared world everyone fights over. In the middle it grows a private world whose only contested resource is the draft. At the far end it fills a private pattern, and the tile is really a token in a sequencing puzzle. All three are legitimate designs; the disappointment only happens when the structure inside the box is not the structure the buyer pictured.

Two spec checks do most of the work when you are comparing boxes. First, the claim rule: in Carcassonne a meeple placed on a feature stays there until that feature completes, so the game's real resource is claim tokens, not tiles. A decision framework for the store shelf

Run these four questions on any tile-laying box before you buy it:

  1. Whose board does the tile land on? A shared map means direct interaction; a personal board means the conflict is in the draft; a fixed grid means a solo sequencing puzzle with company.
  2. What makes a placement legal? Edge-matching rules (roads to roads) create a spatial puzzle. Adjacency-only rules create a planning puzzle. The stricter the matching, the more a single tile draw can stall a player.
  3. Do you claim what you complete? If pieces claim features and stay committed until scoring, expect tension and some table talk. If nothing is claimed, expect a calmer optimisation race.
  4. What exactly scores? Completed features, biggest groupings, or pattern positions — the scoring rule is the strategy. A game that scores only your largest habitat grouping plays nothing like one that scores every completed road.

Success check for the framework: pick any tile-laying game you already own and answer all four questions from memory of its rules. If the answers describe the sessions you actually had — the arguments, the calm, the frustration — the framework is calibrated, and it will sort the next box correctly too.

FAQ

Are tile-laying and tile placement the same thing? Yes — two names for one mechanic: laying flat pieces to build a map, expand a personal board, or trigger spatial rewards.

Is Azul a tile-laying game? It is shelved as one and drafts tiles, but structurally it is pattern-building: a fixed personal grid, no map, no claiming. Buy it for sequencing, not geography.

Which structure is best for new players? Personal-board games remove blocking and feature-stealing, which many new groups experience as meanness. Shared-map games teach faster conceptually — the map explains itself — but expect the first stolen city to need a house rule about feelings.

Why do my claim pieces run out in Carcassonne? Because a meeple stays on its feature until the feature completes. That scarcity is the game: claim less often, and claim what can actually finish.
