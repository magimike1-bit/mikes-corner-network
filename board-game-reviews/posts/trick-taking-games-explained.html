Trick-taking games, explained

Hobby basics · 8 min read

The thirty-second version

A trick is a single round of cards: one card from each player, played in turn to the middle of the table. The player who plays the winning card takes the trick — collects those cards — and leads the first card of the next one. That is the entire engine. Everything that makes trick-taking games feel deep, and occasionally intimidating, comes from three additions: you usually have to follow suit, one suit may be trump, and — the part explainers skip — different games pay you for opposite things.

Following suit means that when the leader plays a card, every other player must play a card of that same suit if they hold one. Only a player with none of the led suit is free — they may throw away an unwanted card (a slough, or discard) or play a trump. Trump is a designated suit that outranks all the others: as Pagat's mechanics reference puts it, any trump card, however small, beats all cards of the other suits, and a trick containing trumps is won by the highest trump played. If no trump appears, the highest card of the suit led wins.

Notice what following suit does to your hand. You are rarely choosing your best card; you are choosing the least bad card the rules allow. Your real decisions are about timing — which of your legal cards to spend now, and which to keep for a trick that matters more. That constraint, not the card rankings, is the game.

One trick, walked through slowly

Take a four-player trick in a game where hearts are not trump and spades are. North leads the 10 of spades. East must follow suit if able and plays the king of spades. South holds no spades at all, so South is free: in the worked example carried by the trick-taking taxonomy, South plays the jack of diamonds and West the 7 of hearts. Now resolve it. If the game has no trump in play, East wins — the king is the highest card of the suit led. If spades themselves were trump, the same conclusion holds by the trump route. But if diamonds were trump, South's jack — a card that ignored the led suit entirely — would take the trick, and if hearts were trump, West's humble 7 would.

That one example teaches the three questions to ask about every trick you ever play: What suit was led? Am I holding it? And is anything on the table currently beating the card I am about to add? New players get lost when a teacher explains all the exceptions before the first trick. Play one trick face-up, ask those three questions out loud for each player, and the structure lands in a way no rules lecture achieves.

The goal flip: three families, opposite aims

Here is the gap in almost every explainer: trick-taking is not one game with variants, it is one mechanism carrying at least three opposite goals. The taxonomy divides them by what a taken trick is worth. In plain-trick games — contract bridge, whist, and spades are the textbook names — the object is tied to the number of tricks taken: tricks themselves are the score. In point-trick games — pinochle, the tarot family, briscola — the number of tricks matters far less than the value of the cards inside them; a trick full of low cards can be worth less than one fat trick. And in evasion games, hearts being the standard example, taking certain cards is a penalty: the play is identical and the aim is inverted.

The family decides the temperament you are buying. Plain-trick games reward consistency and partnership reading. Point-trick games reward valuation — knowing which trick is worth spending a trump on. Avoidance games reward mischief and memory, and they are often the best first trick-taking game for a mixed group precisely because a beginner's instinct to not win things is, for once, correct.

Ducking: the skill nobody explains

Board Game Rules states the principle plainly: winning every trick is rarely the goal, and players must manage when to take control, when to duck out of contention, and how to exhaust specific suits. Ducking means playing a card that deliberately loses a trick you could have won — following suit with your lowest card instead of your highest, or declining to trump in. It feels wrong to new players, who read every trick as a contest. It is, in most deals, the mark of someone who understands the game.

Three situations cover most good ducks. First, the trick is worth nothing to you: in a point-trick game it holds no valuable cards, or in a plain-trick game your partner is already winning it, and adding your high card wastes it. Second, winning means leading: the trick winner leads the next trick, and sometimes leading is a liability — you would be forced to open a suit you wanted an opponent to open. Third, conservation: your high card in a suit is worth more later, after opponents have shown whether they still hold that suit. A player who spends winners on worthless tricks arrives at the deciding tricks holding only apologies.

The mirror skill is just as important: noticing when an opponent ducks. A player who follows suit with a suspiciously small card while holding back is telling the table something about the rest of their hand. Trick-taking games are information games wearing a card game's clothes, and ducking is how the information leaks.

Teaching the first hand: a tested procedure

This teach sequence works because it introduces one rule per trick instead of all rules before any trick:

  1. Deal a short hand face up. Four players if you have them; play the first three tricks with all hands visible on the table.
  2. Trick one: teach the trick itself. Leader plays, everyone plays any card, highest card of the led suit takes it and leads next. Nothing else exists yet.
  3. Trick two: add following suit. Now each player must play the led suit if they hold it. Ask the three questions out loud: what was led, do you hold it, what is winning so far?
  4. Trick three: add trump (if the game has it). Show one small trump beating a large plain-suit card, using the rule that any trump, however small, beats the other suits.
  5. State the goal in one sentence, naming the family. 'Most tricks wins' for plain-trick, 'the cards in tricks are points' for point-trick, 'avoid taking these cards' for an evasion game.
  6. Turn the hands face down and redeal. The full game now contains no rule a player has not already used.

Success check: before the real hand starts, any player at the table can resolve a hypothetical trick unaided — given a led card, a hand, and the trump suit, they can say which cards they may legally play and which card on the table is currently winning. If they can do that, teach is done; if they cannot, repeat trick two, because following suit is the step that actually failed.

Choosing your first trick-taking game: a decision framework

Choose by temperament first, tradition second. If your group likes direct accumulation and clear progress, start in the plain-trick family, where every won trick visibly counts. If they like valuation and bluffing about what a trick is worth, point-trick games give them richer decisions per card. If they are competitive but new, or the group spans ages, an evasion game like hearts forgives beginners — caution is a strategy there, not a mistake. And whatever the family, prefer a game whose trump is fixed or simply determined while the group learns; a trump suit decided by auction or bidding adds a whole second game before the first card is played, which is a wonderful thing in month two and a wall in week one.

FAQ

What does 'follow suit' actually mean? Play a card of the suit that was led, if you hold one. Only when you hold none may you discard or trump. Breaking the rule while holding the suit is reneging, and it is the one cheat the genre treats seriously.

Does a small trump really beat an ace of another suit? Yes. Any trump card, however small, beats all cards of the other suits; between two trumps, the higher one wins.

Who leads the next trick? The winner of the previous trick, in the standard structure: lead, follow, resolve, winner collects and leads.

Is bridge a good first trick-taking game? Bridge is a plain-trick game, but its bidding system is a second game layered on top. Learn the trick structure in a simpler plain-trick game first; the cards will already make sense when the bidding arrives.
