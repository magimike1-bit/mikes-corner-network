What complexity ratings actually mean

Hobby basics · 6 min read

Every board game box is covered in numbers: player counts, playtimes, age ratings, and — once you start browsing hobby sites — "weight" scores. These numbers are genuinely useful, but only if you know what they're actually measuring. Misread them and you'll buy games that don't fit your group. Here's how to decode the box before you buy.

Weight: how heavy is the thinking?

The most important number in the hobby isn't on the box at all — it's the weight rating on BoardGameGeek, the hobby's big community database. Weight is a community-voted score from 1 (very light) to 5 (very heavy) that answers one question: how much mental effort does this game demand?

A weight around 1 to 1.5 means a game most people can learn in minutes and play casually — the gateway-game zone. Around 2 to 2.5, you're in the hobby sweet spot: real decisions, but nothing that melts brains. From 3 up, expect longer teaches, deeper strategy, and a real learning curve. Above 4, you're in enthusiast territory: games that reward — and require — serious study.

Two caveats. First, weight is voted by hobbyists, so it skews: what's a "2" to a veteran might feel like a "3.5" to your family. Second, weight measures complexity, not quality or fun. A weight-1.2 game can be a masterpiece and a weight-4.5 game can be a chore. Use weight to match a game to your group's appetite, not to judge whether a game is good.

Playtime: the box lies (a little)

The playtime on the box is best understood as the time experienced players take once everyone knows the rules. Your first play will almost always run longer — sometimes dramatically so. A good rule of thumb: add 50% to the box time for a first session with new players, and don't forget to budget the teach on top of that.

Also note what the box time assumes about player count. Many games scale with players: a game listed at "60–120 minutes" might mean 60 with two players and 120 with five. If your group is usually on the high end of the player range, plan for the high end of the time range.

Player count: the range vs. the sweet spot

The player range on the box tells you what's possible, not what's best. Almost every game has a sweet spot — a count where the design clicks — and it's often narrower than the printed range. This is where community wisdom helps: BoardGameGeek's player-count polls show, for each game, how many players voters call "best" vs. merely "recommended." Before buying a game for a specific group size, check the "best with" number for your count, not just whether your count appears on the box.

This matters most at the extremes. A game that plays 2–6 might be fantastic with four and awkward with two. If your group is almost always exactly three people, buy games that are beloved at three — not games that merely allow it.

Age rating: a rough floor, not a promise

The age on the box (usually something like "Ages 10+") is a manufacturer's guideline, and it mostly reflects reading level and attention demands rather than a tested minimum. Plenty of younger kids handle "10+" strategy games; plenty of adults bounce off "8+" games that are secretly fiddly. Treat it as a hint about the game's cognitive load, and calibrate against the specific kids or adults you know.

Teach time: the hidden number

No box lists this, but it's the number that determines whether a game night succeeds. Roughly speaking, teach time tracks with weight: a weight-1.5 game might take five minutes to teach, a weight-3 game more like twenty or thirty. When you're evaluating a game, look for reviews or videos that mention how long the teach took — and be honest about your group's tolerance. A group that groans at a ten-minute explanation will not enjoy a game that needs thirty, no matter how good the game is.

Putting it together: the pre-buy checklist

Before you buy, run through these five questions:

1. Weight vs. appetite. Does the weight match what your group enjoys? If your table's favorite game is a light card game, a weight-3.5 epic is a gamble no matter how good the reviews are.

2. Time vs. schedule. Box time plus teach, adjusted for first-play slowness — does it fit the evening you actually have?

3. Sweet spot vs. your count. Is your player count in the game's "best with" zone, or just inside the printed range?

4. Teach vs. patience. Can you get this game explained before your group loses interest?

5. Theme vs. room. Will your specific people care about what this game is about? (Our first-game guide digs into this one.)

Answer those five honestly and you'll dodge the vast majority of bad purchases — not because you'll always pick winners, but because you'll stop buying games that were never meant for your table in the first place.
