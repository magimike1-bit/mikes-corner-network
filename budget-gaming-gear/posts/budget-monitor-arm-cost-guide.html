Budget gaming monitor arm: what the upgrade actually costs

Buying guides · 7 min read

C$89.99. That was the non-deal price of a mainstream gas-spring monitor arm on Amazon.ca when prices were checked on 31 October 2026 — and it is the last number that matters, not the first. Before any arm belongs in a cart, three published checks decide whether it can hold the screen at all. A pole arm that passes them can cost less than half as much. A dual mount can undercut two singles. The saving is real, but only after the fit is proven.

The three checks before any arm: weight, holes, desk

Start with weight, and weigh the right thing. One comparison of single, dual and triple arms checked for this guide puts the point plainly: weight range is the most important spec, ahead of screen size. The weight that counts is the monitor without its stand, because the stand stays in a cupboard. Gas-spring arms only balance screens inside their rated range. Too heavy and the screen sinks. Too light for the arm — a small panel on an arm rated from 7 lb upward — and it can float up instead of staying where it was left.

Second, the holes. Every arm in that same comparison uses a VESA pattern of 75×75 mm or 100×100 mm: four screw holes in a square on the back of the monitor. Measure the distance between the holes, in millimetres, before ordering. Monitors without VESA holes need an adapter, which is a second purchase the headline price never includes.

Third, the desk itself. VIVO's manual for its pole arm sets a maximum desktop thickness of 3.25 inches for both its C-clamp and its grommet mounting options. A thicker desktop, a desk with a frame rail exactly where the clamp needs to sit, or a glass top can each end the project before it starts. Measure the thickness at the back edge, where the clamp will actually go.

Why bother? Because the target is specific. Canadian occupational-health guidance from the CCOHS puts the top of the screen at eye level, at a viewing distance of 30–60 cm. A stock stand that cannot reach eye level is the problem an arm is bought to solve — and an arm that cannot fit the screen, the holes, or the desk solves nothing at any price.

Pole vs gas-spring: the two designs at two prices

A pole arm is a vertical post with an arm that slides and locks along it. Height is set once, with a clamp or collar, and then largely left alone. A gas-spring arm uses a pressurised strut that counterbalances the monitor's weight, so the screen can be raised, lowered and swung with one finger, as often as the day demands.

That difference — set-and-forget versus move-it-daily — is what the price gap buys. Nothing else.

One screen, two screens: costed per screen

Two screens change the arithmetic completely. Two HUANUO gas-spring singles at the checked non-deal price come to C$179.98. The axGear dual stand checked at Best Buy Canada costs C$80.99 for both screens — C$40.50 per screen — with 22 lb of support per side, VESA 75×75 mm or 100×100 mm, and 15.5 inches of height adjustment along its pole.

The dual stand is not the better arm. It is a pole design, so each screen is set and then left, and its screens top out at 27 inches against the HUANUO's 34. What it proves is narrower and more useful: for a two-screen desk where neither screen moves after setup day, paying for two gas springs buys a feature that gets used once.

Cost per year makes the same point over time. Spread over five years — a cautious life for a steel arm with no electronics — the HUANUO single works out to about C$18.00 a year, and the axGear dual to about C$16.20 a year for two screens, or C$8.10 per screen per year. These are divisions of the checked prices above, not predictions: an arm that is outgrown by a heavier monitor in year two costs its full price for those two years, which is why the weight headroom in the first section matters more than the yearly figure.

The traps that add a second purchase

  • Buying by screen size alone. A 32-inch screen inside the size range can still exceed the weight limit, and a screen inside the weight limit can lack VESA holes. Size is the one check that proves the least.
  • Forgetting the adapter. No VESA holes means an adapter bracket on top of the arm price — or a return. Check the back of the monitor first; it takes a minute.
  • Paying for daily movement that never happens. A screen that is positioned once and never moved again gains nothing from a gas spring. The pole arm's weakness is also its discount.
  • Ignoring the minimum weight. Gas-spring arms have a floor as well as a ceiling. A very light screen on a strong arm may not stay down — the comparison checked for this guide flags exactly that failure on a 7–25 lb arm.

Monitor arm decision checklist

  • Monitor too low on its stock stand, and books or a riser have already failed? An arm is a reasonable purchase. Otherwise, the free position fixes come first.
  • Screen weight without its stand inside the arm's published range — both above the minimum and below the maximum? Proceed. Outside it? Different arm, not a hopeful order.
  • VESA holes at 75×75 mm or 100×100 mm, desktop at or under the clamp's published thickness? Proceed. No holes? Price the adapter into the same budget line before comparing arms.
  • Screen moves daily between positions? Gas-spring. Set once? Pole — and for two static screens, price one dual mount before two singles.
