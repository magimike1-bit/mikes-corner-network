Cost per year for budget gaming peripherals: cheaper is not always cheaper

Buying guides · 8 min read

$20 a year. That is what a $30 gaming mouse actually costs if it lasts a year and a half — the worked example published in the cost-per-year coverage checked for this guide. The same coverage prices a $120 mouse lasting four years at $30 a year. So the cheap mouse wins by $10 a year, and the expensive mouse wins on everything the arithmetic leaves out: downtime, replacement hassle, and faults like double-clicking and drift arriving mid-cycle. Both halves of that sentence matter. This guide gives you the formula, runs it across a whole desk, and shows where a small repair part changes the answer.

One formula for the whole desk

The formula fits on one line:

Cost per year = (purchase price + any replacement parts) ÷ years of use you actually get.

Three honest inputs, and the third is the hard one. Price you know on the day. Parts you can price before you buy. Years of use is an estimate, so use a range, not a hope. Headsets make the point: the average gaming headset lifespan is put at between two and six years in one source checked for this guide, and anywhere from 2 to 5 years in another. A range that wide means the same headset can be a bargain or a mistake depending on which end of it your unit lands on — which is exactly why the formula should be run at both ends before you buy, not once at the optimistic end.

The worked example, assumptions labelled

Every price and lifespan in this section is an assumption for the example, chosen to be easy to check against your own shelf prices — not a quote, and not a prediction for any specific product. Replace each number with the price and the lifespan you honestly expect, and redo the division.

  • Mouse A: assume $35, lasting 2 years → $35 ÷ 2 = $17.50/year.
  • Mouse B: assume $70, lasting 4 years → $70 ÷ 4 = $17.50/year. Identical. When the yearly figures tie, buy the one that fits your hand — the arithmetic has nothing left to say.
  • Headset C (fixed mic): assume $60, lasting 2 years → $30/year.
  • Headset D (detachable mic): assume $90, lasting 3 years, plus one $15 replacement part in year 3 that extends it to 4 years → ($90 + $15) ÷ 4 = $26.25/year. The pricier headset wins only because the cheap part existed. That conditional is the whole lesson, and it matches the direction of the sourced comparison above, where a $150 detachable-mic headset lasting four years beats a $110 fixed-mic model replaced after two over a five-year span.

Run your own version in two minutes:

  1. Write the shelf price of the cheap option and the step-up option.
  2. Under each, write a pessimistic lifespan and an optimistic one — for a headset, the sourced 2–6 year range is a reasonable outer frame.
  3. Divide price by years for all four combinations. If the step-up option only wins at its optimistic lifespan and loses at its pessimistic one, the saving is a gamble, not a saving.
  4. Add any replacement part you would realistically buy — a cable, pads, a mic — to the price, and add the extra time it buys to the years, then divide again.

What success looks like: four yearly figures on paper, and a decision you can explain in one sentence without using the word “premium”.

Wear parts that reset the clock

The cheapest year of peripheral life is usually bought as a part, not a product. The pattern to look for at purchase time — before you need it — is whether the thing that will wear out is separable. A detachable microphone can be replaced on its own, where a failed fixed microphone means replacing the entire headset. A headset whose cable detaches can survive the cable. Ear pads, mouse feet and similar wear items follow the same logic when the maker sells them as parts.

Two checks keep this honest. First, confirm the part is actually sold, and at what price, on the day you buy the product — a theoretically replaceable part that is never in stock resets nothing. Second, stop repairing when the part costs a large share of a replacement product and the rest of the unit is also worn: at that point the formula, run again with real numbers, will tell you the new product is the cheaper year.

When the cheapest option really does win

Often enough that this section is not a formality. The sourced mouse example ends with the cheap mouse ahead on yearly cost — $20 against $30 — and for a light-use desk, a spare, a travel kit, or a category you are trying for the first time, the low yearly figure plus the low money at risk is the right answer. The step-up argument depends on heavy use arriving and on faults appearing mid-cycle; a peripheral used a few hours a week may never collect either cost. Buy for the use you actually have, costed per year, and let heavy users make the heavy-user argument.

The traps that break the math

  • Optimistic lifespans, applied selectively. Giving the expensive option its best-case years and the cheap option its worst-case years manufactures the conclusion. Same treatment, both options, both ends of the range.
  • Ignoring the battery clock. A wireless headset's years are partly counted in charge cycles — 300 to 500 in the sourced figure — so a daily-charge habit spends the lifespan faster than the calendar suggests.
  • Counting a repair part you would never buy. If you know you will replace the whole unit when the pads flatten, the pads do not belong in your calculation.
  • Forgetting the hassle is real but not infinite. Downtime and replacement trips are genuine costs, named in the sourced mouse example — but they do not justify any price gap. Put a rough dollar value on them once, write it down, and reuse it.
