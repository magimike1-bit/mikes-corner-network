Budget Phone Fast Charging, Decoded: Watts, TurboPower, PPS and the Charger Nobody Includes

Buying criteria · 6 min read · Published 2026-11-14 · Last updated 2026-11-14

Every budget phone on this site ships its battery story as one big number: 5,000 mAh. Capacity is the size of the tank. Charging watts are the width of the hose, and on this tier the hose widths differ by almost double while the tank stays the same, which is why two phones with identical battery sizes leave the wall at completely different speeds. Worse, the headline watt figure is rarely the watt figure a buyer actually gets, because the fast part of the claim often lives in a charger that is not in the box. This guide reads the charging spec the way it should be read: watts first, protocol second, box contents third, and the price of the missing charger counted as part of the phone.

At 25W and above, a short top-up during breakfast returns a usable chunk of battery, and a full charge stops being an overnight only event. Below that line, charging speed is the compromise the price quietly made.

Two systems: universal PD/PPS versus brand protocols

Phone charging splits into two families. The universal family is USB Power Delivery, and Samsung's modern phones speak it, in the PPS variant, which lets the charger adjust voltage and current in fine steps. The practical virtue of the universal family is interchangeability: one good PD PPS adapter can fast charge a Samsung phone today and a different brand tomorrow. Google's Pixel 9a is the other universal speaker on the wider market, at 23W wired with PD 3.0 and 7.5W wireless charging, behind the 25W line but open to any compliant charger.

The brand family is Motorola's TurboPower, and its rule is printed on Motorola's own product page: to experience TurboPower fast charging, a Motorola TurboPower 30W charger or higher is required, and it is sold separately. Motorola also states the ceiling plainly: a higher wattage charger will not increase the phone's maximum charging capability. The phone decides the most it will take; the charger either meets that or it does not. The same logic quietly governs the universal family. A 100W laptop adapter plugged into a 25W phone delivers 25W at most, and a basic 10W or 15W adapter from an old drawer delivers its own figure instead, which is how a fast charging phone ends up slow charging: the phone was never the slow part.

What the missing charger costs: the price check

When the box holds a cable and no adapter, the adapter is a purchase, and its price belongs in the phone's price. Price check dated November 14, 2026, from Samsung Canada's own store: the 25W Power Adapter in the adapter only configuration lists at $29.99 CAD, and the version with a cable lists at $49.99 CAD. Samsung's listing confirms the adapter meets the spec the phones ask for, PD 3.0 with PPS at a maximum of 25W.

Count it honestly. A Galaxy A17 5G bought at the top of this site's range leaves little room, and adding $29.99 for the adapter that unlocks its advertised speed is a real percentage of the deal. The Moto G Power (2025) carries the same asterisk through Motorola's separate TurboPower charger. Two phones at the same shelf price are not at the same price if one needs a thirty dollar brick to perform as advertised. This site's reviews already argue that charger assumptions change which phone wins; charging guides should be just as blunt. Budget the adapter, or buy the phone whose speed works with the chargers already in the house.

The pre-buy charging check

  1. Read the watts on the spec sheet, not the marketing line. 25W wired is this tier's fast line; 45W on the A36 5G is the strongest figure in the table.
  2. Name the protocol. USB PD with PPS means universal chargers work. A brand protocol like TurboPower means the brand's own charger is the key.
  3. Ask what is in the box. Cable only means the adapter price joins the phone price. Price it the same day, from the maker's own store where possible.
  4. Check the cable at the Samsung 45W tier. That tier calls for a 5 amp rated cable; a random cable can cap the speed the adapter paid for.
  5. Count wireless separately. The Moto G Power (2025) offers 15W wireless, which none of the compared Samsung models confirm; its wired figure trails the A36 but the pad on the nightstand may matter more.

What success looks like: the phone, the adapter and the cable form a chain where every link meets the phone's rated watts, and the total paid, adapter included, is the number compared against the next phone. Anything less means the buyer paid for a speed the setup cannot deliver.

The short version

On equal 5,000 mAh batteries, charging is a systems purchase, not a phone purchase. The A36 5G's 45W is the pace setter if a PPS adapter and a proper cable join it. The Moto G Power (2025) answers with 30W wired plus the tier's rare wireless option, gated behind a TurboPower brick. The A17 5G's 25W is adequate and universal, provided the $29.99 adapter is counted in its price. Pick the chain, not the headline.
