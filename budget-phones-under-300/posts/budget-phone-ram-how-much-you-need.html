How much RAM does a budget phone need? Read the physical number, not the headline

Buying criteria · 7 min read

RAM is the spec that decides how a budget phone feels in month eighteen — whether switching from maps back to a half-written message keeps both alive, or one of them reloads from scratch. It is also, right now, the most creatively marketed number on the box. The Motorola Moto G (2025) is advertised in the United States with "up to 12GB of RAM." Motorola's own Canadian page for the same phone says it has 4GB of memory. Both statements trace back to the same manufacturer, and the gap between them is the entire subject of this guide: the first number counts something the second one doesn't, and only one of them is RAM.

Two numbers wearing a trench coat: physical RAM vs. boost

Physical RAM is hardware — memory chips the processor works in directly. It is fixed at manufacture and can never be increased. "Boost" RAM is a software arrangement that borrows part of the phone's storage and treats it as overflow memory. Motorola calls its version RAM Boost; Samsung calls its version RAM Plus. Neither manufacturer hides the mechanism — you just have to read the footnotes instead of the headline.

Motorola's footnote for the Moto G (2025) states the configuration plainly: "4GB physical RAM + up to 8GB RAM Boost (2GB Default | 8GB Max)." A second footnote explains that RAM Boost "requires use of phone's internal storage as virtual memory, decreasing storage capacity" and that the feature is "on by default unless turned off." Add the physical figure to the maximum boost and you get the retail headline: 4 + 8 = "up to 12GB of RAM with RAM Boost," which is exactly how Best Buy US's feature list for this phone phrases it. Samsung's support page describes RAM Plus the same way — it "uses a portion of your internal storage as virtual memory," with options typically from 2GB to 8GB — and then reaches the verdict the marketing never quotes: "RAM Plus is not a substitute for upgrading to a device with more physical RAM." That sentence is Samsung's, about Samsung's own feature.

Why isn't borrowed storage a substitute? Because storage is built for keeping data, not for being worked in. Overflow memory helps the phone park more apps instead of closing them, which is a real benefit — but an app recalled from storage-backed memory has to be read back before it responds, while an app still resident in physical RAM is simply there. The boost raises the ceiling on how much can be parked; the physical figure decides how much is instantly alive. When the two are added into one advertised number, the buyer cannot tell which they are getting. Read them separately, and always compare phones on the physical figure alone.

It also frames the real buying question, which is not "how do I find 8GB under $300?" — at Canadian retail, the honest 8GB phone in this comparison is the CA$679 Pixel 9a — but "what does 4GB physical actually feel like, and can I live with it?" The next two sections answer that from your own usage rather than from a tier chart.

What the tiers actually feel like

The unit of RAM pain is the reload: you switch back to an app and it starts over — the page refreshes, the form empties, the video re-buffers. The mechanism is documented and mundane. Croma's explainer puts approximate sizes on it: a typical app consumes around 100MB of RAM when it launches, a game up to 1GB, a resource-demanding game around 1.5GB, and the operating system takes its portion before any of them. When physical memory runs short, Android compresses what it can (the zRAM stage), and when that fills, it force-closes apps to make room. A reload is that force-close, discovered later.

So the arithmetic of a 4GB phone is not mysterious. After the system takes its share, a few full-size apps — a browser with real tabs, a social app, maps, a camera session — are enough to push older apps out. Light use (calls, messages, one app at a time, a little browsing) genuinely fits; Android Authority's own framing concedes 4GB for exactly that pattern. Layered use — navigating while messaging while a photo uploads — is where 4GB starts billing you in reloads, and where 6GB, the recommended budget minimum, earns its keep. The pressure is also rising from above: the same Android Authority analysis notes premium phones now ship 12–16GB partly because on-device AI features need their models resident in RAM. Features trickle down; RAM requirements do not trickle back up. A phone bought at the floor today meets heavier apps every year it is kept.

The ten-minute reload test: size your next phone from your current one

Generic tiers cannot tell you which side of the line your usage sits on. Your current phone can, in about ten minutes:

  1. Set up a realistic chain. Open the apps you genuinely rotate through in a normal hour — for many people: messages, browser with a few tabs, maps, camera, one social app, email.
  2. Use each one briefly, in order. Type a line in the message, scroll the page, start a route, take a photo. Half a minute each is enough; you are loading them the way your day does.
  3. Now walk the chain backwards. Return to each app in reverse order without closing anything, and count the reloads: an app that reopens exactly where you left it survived; one that restarts, refreshes or loses your place was evicted.
  4. Score it. Zero or one reload across the chain: your usage fits your current RAM tier, and the same physical tier in a new phone will feel fine. Three or more reloads: you are RAM-bound today — a new phone at the same physical figure will reproduce the frustration with a newer case around it.
  5. Check your current phone's physical RAM (Settings > About phone) so the score attaches to a number. Your result reads: "my usage needs more than X GB physical," which is a shopping specification no headline can argue with.

What success looks like: a reload count and a physical-RAM floor, both measured on your own apps. If the count says you are RAM-bound, the decision is made — spend on physical RAM (a 6GB-or-better model, or a step up the range), not on a bigger boost figure.

The buying rule

Compare physical to physical, treat boost as a minor comfort feature, and let your reload count arbitrate. Concretely: a "12GB" phone that is 4GB physical plus 8GB boost is a 4GB phone — price it, and judge it, as one. A phone advertised with a single unqualified RAM figure and no boost branding is stating its physical number; that honesty is worth noticing. And if your ten-minute test says you outgrew 4GB, no setting will rescue a new 4GB purchase — Motorola and Samsung both ship their boost features on by default precisely because the overflow helps at the margins, and both manufacturers' own documents stop well short of calling it more RAM. The under-$300 shelf is thinner on 6GB physical options than the recommendation says it should be, which makes the test more valuable, not less: it tells you whether the compromise is one your usage will ever notice.

Common questions

Is 4GB of RAM enough in 2026? For calls, messages and one-app-at-a-time use, yes — that is the pattern independent guidance assigns to it. For layered everyday use, the recommended budget floor is 6GB physical. Run the reload test; your count settles which description matches you.

Does turning RAM Boost or RAM Plus up to maximum fix a slow phone? It lets the phone park more apps instead of closing them, at the cost of some storage (Motorola states the boost decreases storage capacity; Samsung asks for at least 10GB free). Samsung's own support page states it is not a substitute for more physical RAM.

Can I add RAM to a phone later? No. Physical RAM is hardware, fixed at manufacture — the only later adjustment is the size of the storage-backed boost, which is the lesser of the two numbers.

Why do listings disagree about the same phone's RAM? Because some quote the physical figure (Motorola Canada: 4GB) and some quote physical plus maximum boost (US retail: "up to 12GB"). When two numbers appear for one phone, the smaller one is the physical RAM.

Our take: Judge every budget phone by its physical RAM alone: the shelf's volume models carry 4GB under headlines as high as 12GB, the independent floor for budget phones is 6GB, and the manufacturers' own footnotes side with the smaller number. Ten minutes of reload-counting on your current phone tells you whether the gap matters to you.
