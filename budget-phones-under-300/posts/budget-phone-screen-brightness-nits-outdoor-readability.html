Budget phone screen brightness: what the nits number actually buys you outdoors

Buying criteria · 6 min read · Published 2026-10-31 · Last updated 2026-10-31

The biggest brightness number on a budget phone's box is the one you will almost never see. A phone advertised at 2,700 nits, or 3,000, reaches that figure on a small patch of screen, for a few seconds, while playing HDR video. The screen you squint at on a sunny sidewalk — a map, a camera preview, a transit app — runs on a different, much smaller number. Shopping by the headline figure is how a buyer ends up with a phone that looks spectacular on the spec sheet and washes out at the bus stop.

Three brightnesses, one screen

Brightness is measured in nits — candelas per square metre — and it decides visibility in difficult light. SamMobile's explainer puts the modern range at roughly 400 nits for budget devices to over 2,000 nits for premium flagships. Inside that range, manufacturers quote up to three different measurements, and listings rarely say which one they are using.

Typical brightness is what the slider gives you indoors: roughly 600 to 1,000 nits at 100% on current phones, per Android Police's breakdown of the three tiers. High Brightness Mode (HBM) is the outdoor boost the phone applies itself in strong light, typically 1,200 to 1,600 nits on phones that publish it. Peak brightness is the HDR figure: power funnelled into a small section of the panel for highlights in video — 4,000 to 6,000 nits on current flagships.

The gap between the second and third numbers is physics, not fine print. When the entire screen is lit, power is shared across every pixel, so full-screen brightness tops out around 1,000 to 1,600 nits even on the best phones. A small bright patch can be driven far harder — briefly. Sustaining that level across the panel would overheat it; OLED material degrades when it is run at extreme brightness for more than a few seconds. The peak figure is real. It is just answering a question about HDR highlights, not about sunlight.

Panel type matters alongside the number. An AMOLED screen lights each pixel individually and switches pixels off for black, while an LCD runs a backlight behind the panel that stays on. Brightness by itself improves none of the other quality factors — not colour accuracy, not resolution. It does one job: keeping the content visible when the light around you is working against it. For that job, Android Police's testing puts the useful full-screen range for sunlight readability at roughly 1,000 to 1,500 nits.

Its 800-nit HBM sits below the 1,000-to-1,500 full-screen range that reads comfortably in direct sun, and the lab measurement of its auto mode — 735 nits — lands lower than the HBM rating. That does not make the screen unusable outdoors; shade, angle and the contrast of an AMOLED panel all help. It does mean the A17's "1,100 nits" listing figure overstates its sunlight performance by roughly a third, and a buyer whose day is spent outdoors — delivery work, construction, cycling directions — should treat HBM as a primary spec, not a footnote.

The 60-second outdoor readability check

Spec sheets settle most of this, but the last check is yours, and it takes about a minute in a store or on a phone you already own:

  1. Turn auto-brightness on. HBM is an automatic mode — with the slider set manually, many phones never engage the outdoor boost, and you will judge a lower number than the phone can actually produce in sun. Phones use light sensors to drive this adjustment, so give it a few seconds under bright light to respond.
  2. Open the apps you use outside. Maps, the camera preview and a messaging thread — not the home screen, which is mostly dark wallpaper on an AMOLED panel and flatters the result.
  3. Face the screen toward the light, not away from it. Read a line of map text at arm's length. If you have to shade the screen with a hand to read it, that is the HBM figure telling the truth.
  4. Compare two phones on the same screen. Put the same map view on both, side by side, same angle. Differences in the HBM column are visible; differences in the peak column are not, because neither phone is showing HDR highlights.

What success looks like: map text and the camera preview stay readable at arm's length in direct light with no hand-shading. A phone that passes this check will not embarrass you outdoors whatever its peak figure says; a phone that fails it will not be rescued by one.

Bottom line: buy on HBM, ignore the peak

The decision framework is short. Buy when the published HBM reaches roughly 1,000 nits or better and the rest of the phone fits — that is the range where sunlight readability stops being a daily negotiation. Accept the compromise knowingly at 800 nits HBM, the A17's figure: fine for mixed indoor-outdoor use, a real limitation for outdoor-first work, and a fair trade only when the price reflects it. Skip the spec entirely when a listing quotes only a peak figure with no HBM or typical number beside it — a seller who will not separate the three numbers is telling you which one is flattering. Nits are a real measurement. They are just three measurements wearing one name, and only one of them goes outside with you.

Our take: Peak nits describe HDR highlights on a small patch of screen; HBM describes your phone at the bus stop. On the current shelf that is 800 nits for the Galaxy A17 5G (735 measured in auto mode), up to 1,200 for the A36 5G, and a 1,800-nit class figure under the Pixel 9a's 2,700-nit headline. Compare the second column, run the 60-second check, and let the peak number sell somebody else a phone.
