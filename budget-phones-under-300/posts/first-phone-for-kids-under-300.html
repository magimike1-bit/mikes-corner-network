A first phone for kids under $300: controls to set up before handover

Family · 10 min read

A child's first phone is two purchases disguised as one: a handset cheap enough that its loss is a lesson rather than a disaster, and a set of controls configured before the handover, while setting them up is still a gift-preparation task instead of a negotiation. Under $300 covers both comfortably — capable Android handsets and refurbished options sit well inside it — provided the money is allocated in the right order: phone, case and screen protector, then a low-cost plan, with nothing left needing to be spent on control software, because both major platforms include parental controls free. This guide covers the buying checks, the pre-handover setup procedure for both platforms, and the honest limits of what controls can do.

The pre-handover setup procedure

Every step below happens before the phone is given. Retrofitting controls onto a device a child already considers theirs converts a setup task into a conflict; the same settings installed beforehand are simply how the phone works.

  1. Create the phone under a child account with the correct birthdate — Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link. Never your own adult account: age-based protections, purchase approval and content ratings all key off the account's age, and an adult account silently disables most of them.
  2. Turn on purchase approval (Ask to Buy on Apple; purchase approvals on Family Link) so every download and in-app purchase needs your approval. Note the limit of the Android version: approvals cover purchases through Google Play billing, not every payment path, which is why no payment card should be stored on the device itself.
  3. Set downtime or bedtime hours. The phone locks itself outside agreed hours; an overnight lockout, paired with charging outside the bedroom, is the single highest-impact setting for sleep.
  4. Set content ratings and web limits — age-appropriate app ratings, explicit content blocked, safe search on, and on iPhone the Communication Safety features that are on by default for under-18 accounts.
  5. Enable location and test it from your phone. Find My or Find My Device on, location visible to you, SOS and emergency contacts configured and rehearsed once with your child.
  6. Set a screen-time passcode different from the unlock code, and do not type it in front of an observant child. On Android, understand the supervision terms: once a child reaches 13, supervision arrangements change and require the teen's agreement to continue under the platform's rules — a conversation to have at 12 and a half, not on the birthday.
  7. Fit the case and screen protector. Before day one, not after the first crack — this is a financial control as much as a physical one.

What success looks like at handover: a phone that asks you before it buys anything, goes dark at the agreed hour, shows its location on your screen, carries its emergency contacts on its lock screen — and a child who has been shown every one of those settings and told why, because controls explained in advance are house rules, while controls discovered later feel like surveillance.

What the controls will not do

Honesty here protects both the budget and the relationship. Parental controls are a speed bump, not a wall: they do nothing on a friend's unsupervised device, they fade against a determined teenager with a video tutorial, and their largest risk is the false confidence that replaces conversation. Canadian guidance for parents makes the same surrounding points — read the privacy policy before creating the child's account, audit camera, microphone and location features, ask the school what it expects, and add up the apps and subscriptions, not just the handset price. The settings buy time and prevent accidents. The ongoing part — a charging spot outside the bedroom, a promise that anything frightening online can be reported without punishment, a scheduled review where limits loosen as responsibility is demonstrated — is parenting, and no phone under or over $300 ships with it.

The family agreement that sits behind the settings

Write down, with your child, the small agreement the settings enforce: the phone charges outside the bedroom, purchases always ask first, anything frightening or confusing online gets reported without punishment, and the rules will be reviewed together at an agreed date when they can loosen. Keep it to a page, sign it like it matters, and keep the copy. The document does two jobs the software cannot. It makes the controls legible as family rules rather than secret surveillance — a distinction children feel precisely — and it creates the scheduled moment where demonstrated responsibility is rewarded with freedom, which is the incentive that makes the whole structure work. Technology sites can sell you the handset and the carriers the plan; this page is the part of a first phone that only the family can supply.

FAQ

New budget phone, refurbished flagship, or hand-me-down? All three are defensible under $300. A hand-me-down is cheapest and most sustainable; a refurbished phone adds a warranty and a certified battery; a new budget phone adds the longest update runway. Decide by remaining update years and battery health, in that order.

How much data does a first plan need? Less than sales staff suggest. With downloads and video kept to Wi-Fi by the controls you just set, a small data allowance covers messaging, maps and coordination — the phone's actual job. Prepaid and budget-brand plans make the ceiling a feature, not a limitation.

When do the controls come off? Gradually, by demonstrated responsibility, and announced in advance — the review conversation in the setup procedure is where loosening is earned. A phone that arrives with rules and visibly relaxes them teaches the skill the phone was supposedly bought to teach.

Our take: Spend the $300 in order — handset with years of updates, case on day one, cheap plan — and spend the hour before handover on the child account, purchase approval, bedtime lockout and location. The controls are free on both platforms; the only scarce resource is setting them up while it is still easy.
