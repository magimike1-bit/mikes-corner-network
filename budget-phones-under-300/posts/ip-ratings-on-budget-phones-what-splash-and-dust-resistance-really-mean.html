IP Ratings on Budget Phones: What Splash and Dust Resistance Really Mean

Durability · 7 min read · Published 2026-10-29 · Last updated 2026-10-29

An IP rating is a lab result in two digits, not a promise that a phone is waterproof. The first digit rates protection against solids such as dust. The second rates protection against liquids. An X in either position means that axis was not tested. Once those three rules are clear, the budget shelf gets easier to read — and the marketing around it gets harder to overstate.

The two digits, decoded

Take IP54. The 5 is the solids score and the 4 is the liquids score. Change one digit and the claim changes with it, even though the two letters stay the same. That is why comparing only the second digit, or treating any IP label as a single pass-or-fail grade, misses the point.

It also explains a common shelf problem. A phone described as splash-resistant with no IP code attached has made no test claim that can be checked in this table. Treat an unrated phone as unrated. A vague phrase is not a lower rating; it is no rating.

IP68Dust-tight in the table used hereImmersion at 1.5 metres for 30 minutesA deeper version of the same kind of immersion test

Two comparisons matter most under $300. First, IP54 to IP64 is a dust step, not a water step: both carry a splash-level second digit. A buyer choosing IP64 expecting stronger water protection has read only half the code. Second, IP67 changes the kind of water test, from splashing to immersion at 1 metre for 30 minutes. That is a real difference. It is also a bounded one, with a depth and a time attached.

Is IP54 good enough for rain?

On the definitions above, rain sits closest to the splash test: water arriving from any direction, rather than the phone sitting under water. So IP54 answers the rain question better than it answers a dropped-in-a-puddle question. A puddle, a sink or a bath is immersion territory, and IP54 makes no immersion claim.

That distinction is the useful part of the rating. Match the second digit to the accident you actually fear. Occasional rain and a wet counter point one way. A phone that works near water all day points another way, and even then the next sections apply.

Lab new, not month fourteen

Ratings come from controlled lab tests on new devices. Read that sentence literally. The result describes a new phone, in controlled conditions, for the depth and duration named in the test.

A phone that has been dropped, cracked or opened for repair is no longer the same physical object that was tested. Its seals and surfaces have changed in ways no listing can quantify. The sensible rule is not to assign it a new, lower rating — there is no source for one — but to stop relying on the old rating as if damage changed nothing. After visible damage, treat water caution as the default again, whatever the box said.

Age works more quietly in the same direction. The test result was recorded when the device was new. Nothing in the rating itself promises that the result describes the same handset years later.

Salt water, pools and hot liquids: still no

Even the highest IP rating does not make a phone waterproof, and saltwater, pool water and hot liquids are to be avoided regardless of rating. That warning is easy to skip because the test numbers sound so precise. Precision is not permission.

The immersion figures name fresh test conditions, a depth and a time. They do not turn a phone into equipment for the sea, a pool or a hot shower. If a listing photograph shows a phone under a tap or in a pool, read the photograph as marketing. Read the code, and the warning that comes with it, as the claim.

The warranty sentence to check before you rely on a rating

A rating and a warranty are different documents. One describes a test. The other describes what the maker will repair or replace, and under what conditions. A buyer who needs to know how liquid damage is treated has to read the warranty terms for that exact model — the IP code alone does not answer that question, and this guide does not claim it does.

Do that check before an accident, not after one. Find the liquid-damage wording in the model's warranty, keep the rating in its proper place as a test result, and let the two sit side by side when you decide how much risk to take near water.

Check a rating before you buy: four steps

  1. Find the full code. Look for both digits on the manufacturer's specification for the exact model. No code means no rating to check.
  2. Read each digit separately. Note the dust result and the water result as two claims. If either position is an X, that axis was not tested.
  3. Match the water digit to a scenario. Splash, or immersion at a stated depth for a stated time. Write the scenario beside the code so the claim stays bounded.
  4. Read the warranty's liquid wording. Confirm how that model treats liquid damage before deciding how much the rating is worth to you.

What success looks like: a two-digit code from the exact model's specification, a plain sentence for what each digit tested, and the warranty position found in writing — three separate facts, none of them stretched into a waterproof promise.

Buying rule: a tiebreaker, not a headline

Between two otherwise equal budget phones, a verified IP67 is a better water claim than a verified IP54, and a verified rating of any kind is more checkable than an unrated splash-resistant phrase. Use that ordering at the end of a shortlist, not at the start of one.

Battery, storage, update support and price still decide most budget purchases. A rating protects against a specific kind of bad day. It does not improve the phone on every other day, and it never removes the need for care around water. Buy the phone that fits daily use first, then let a properly read IP code break a genuine tie.

Our take: Read both digits. IP54 is limited dust plus splashes, IP64 adds a dust-tight first digit without changing the splash test, and IP67 is immersion at 1 metre for 30 minutes in a lab test on a new device. No rating makes a phone waterproof, and saltwater, pool water and hot liquids stay off limits at every level.
