Is 5G worth it on a budget phone in Canada? Bands, speed caps, and when LTE is enough

Connectivity · 7 min read

Five years ago, 5G on a budget phone was a premium to deliberate over. In Canada in 2026, the deliberation has moved: mainstream budget phones ship with 5G as a standard feature — Best Buy Canada listed the 5G Galaxy A17 128GB at $219.99 CAD when checked on October 17, 2026 — so paying extra purely to "get 5G" rarely makes sense. What still deserves attention is everything underneath the logo: whether the specific variant carries the bands Canadian networks actually use, and whether the plan attached to the phone caps speeds at a level LTE already reaches. Get those two checks right and the 5G question answers itself.

The short answer for 2026

Do not pay a premium for 5G, and do not reject a good budget phone for having it. Do verify two things before buying: the exact model's band list includes n66 and, ideally, n78; and the plan's speed tier is understood, because on capped discount plans the plan — not the radio — sets the ceiling. A Canadian-carrier device page makes the first check easy: Videotron's support page for the Galaxy A17 5G lists its 5G bands as n66, n71, and n78 and marks the phone 5G certified and VoLTE certified. A budget phone bought through a Canadian carrier channel with that band set is properly equipped for every major network in the country. A budget phone bought as an imported variant deserves the full checklist at the end of this guide, because band gaps are exactly where grey-market savings turn into coverage problems.

Canada's 5G bands in plain language

Canadian 5G runs on three bands that matter to a buyer, and each does a different job. Reporting on Rogers' network describes the division clearly: low-band spectrum provides broad coverage and improved building penetration, while mid-band spectrum adds speed and capacity. Rogers built its network by combining 600 MHz low-band spectrum with 3500 MHz and 3800 MHz mid-band spectrum, and the other national networks follow the same architecture.

The published band inventory for Canada lists Bell and TELUS on n66, n71, and n78, with Rogers adding n38 and n41 alongside the same three. It also notes that Canadian deployments are in the FR1 sub-6 GHz range, with millimetre-wave deployment anticipated but uncertain — which is why no Canadian budget buyer needs to think about mmWave at all. Community discussion among Public Mobile users, who ride the TELUS network, fills in the practical texture: n66 is generally available everywhere that has 5G, n78 corresponds to the 5G+ layer on coverage maps, and the low bands appear only in some markets. Translation for a buyer: n66 is the non-negotiable, n78 is the worthwhile extra, and n71 is a bonus for rural and in-building reach.

The plan matters as much as the phone

Here is the fact most 5G marketing leaves out. Public Mobile's own help pages define its subscriptions by speed tier: 3G-speed subscriptions reach up to 3 Mbps, 4G-speed subscriptions up to 100 Mbps, and 5G-speed subscriptions up to 250 Mbps — and all of its subscriptions run on the TELUS 5G and LTE networks. Public Mobile also states plainly that a phone on a 3G-speed or 4G-speed subscription may show the 5G logo, because the device is using the 5G network's reliability at the subscription's speed. The logo, in other words, reports the network; the tier sets the speed.

The mirror image is also true, and Public Mobile's community makes the point repeatedly: a 5G-speed plan's 250 Mbps cap can be approached over LTE, so a 4G phone on a 5G-speed plan captures much of the benefit without any 5G radio at all. For budget buyers the conclusion is liberating. If the choice is between a slightly better phone on a capped plan and a slightly plainer 5G phone, the plan's tier and data amount will shape daily experience more than the radio generation. Spend on the plan attributes that are felt every day — data, coverage, price — and treat the phone's 5G support as a compatibility check rather than a feature to chase.

When LTE is genuinely enough, and when 5G earns its keep

LTE is enough for the classic budget pattern: calls, messaging, browsing, navigation, music streaming, and standard-definition video, on a plan whose speed tier caps out at or below what LTE delivers well. A buyer in that pattern who finds a discounted LTE-era phone with a better screen, battery, or update policy than its 5G rivals should take the better phone. 5G earns its keep in three situations: keeping a phone for many years, since networks and plans are standardizing around 5G tiers; living or working in a congested urban area where the mid-band n78 layer adds capacity; and using the phone as a hotspot or a primary home connection, where sustained throughput matters. Notice that the first reason — longevity — is the strongest one for most budget buyers, and it costs nothing extra when 5G is already standard in the segment.

Before buying an imported budget phone: a band checklist

  1. Find the exact model number on the listing, then find that model's band list on the manufacturer's or a carrier's device page — Videotron's Galaxy A17 5G page is the template for what a verifiable listing looks like.
  2. Confirm n66 is present. Without it, 5G on the Bell and TELUS networks is largely theoretical, whatever the box says.
  3. Prefer n78 as well for city speed and capacity, and treat n71 as a rural-reach bonus.
  4. Check VoLTE certification for the intended network where a carrier publishes it, because voice support on imported variants is a separate question from data bands.
  5. Run the return-window test: in the first days, confirm the phone holds 5G in the places it is actually used — home, workplace, commute — and return it inside the window if it lives on LTE where coverage maps promise 5G.

Success looks like this: a band list naming n66 and n78 for the exact model number, and a phone that shows 5G in daily locations on the chosen network. Anything less is a phone whose headline feature exists only in the listing.

Frequently asked questions

Does a 5G phone need a 5G plan to show any benefit?

The phone and the plan are separate levers. Public Mobile states that phones on lower speed tiers can still connect to the 5G network at the tier's capped speed, and that accessing 5G network speeds requires a compatible phone, current software, and 5G coverage where available. The largest speed step usually comes from the plan tier, up to the 250 Mbps cap on its 5G-speed subscriptions.

Will a US budget phone work on Canadian 5G?

Mainstream US models generally carry n66 and n78, which covers Bell, TELUS, and Rogers per the Canadian band inventory. "Generally" is doing work in that sentence: verify the exact variant's band list, because regional variants of the same model differ.

Is mmWave something a Canadian buyer should look for?

No. Canadian 5G deployments are sub-6 GHz (FR1), and the band inventory describes mmWave deployment in Canada as anticipated but uncertain. It is not a buying criterion here.

Our take: In Canada in 2026, 5G is worth having and not worth paying a premium for — it is already standard on phones like the $219.99 CAD Galaxy A17 5G. The decisions that matter are verifying n66 and n78 on the exact variant, especially on imported stock, and understanding the plan's speed tier, since a capped plan (Public Mobile: up to 250 Mbps on 5G-speed subscriptions) can make LTE and 5G feel identical.
