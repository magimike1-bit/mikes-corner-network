Repair or replace? The half-value rule for a broken budget phone

Buying criteria · 5 min read

Money note: this is general information to structure a decision, not financial advice. Repair quotes vary by shop and model — the decision rule below works with whatever quote you are actually given.

Repair-or-replace advice is almost always written for someone holding a thousand-dollar flagship, where the maths forgive almost any repair. Under $300, the same advice inverts. The screen did not get cheaper because the phone did — a display replacement quoted in the general professional range can consume half of what a budget phone is worth, at which point you are not repairing a phone, you are buying half of a new one and keeping the old battery. This guide runs the standard rules honestly at budget prices.

Gate one: the half-value rule

Two formulations of the same rule circulate in repair guidance. Asurion's version: if repairing your phone costs half as much as buying a new one — or more — consider upgrading, especially if the device is more than three years old and reaching the end of its software update lifecycle. A repair-store framework sharpens it against current value rather than new price: repair when the fix costs less than about half of what the phone is worth today, the phone still gets security updates, and only one thing is wrong; replace when the repair exceeds half the value, the phone is unsupported, or multiple parts are failing. For a budget phone, use the stricter of the two — half of current value — because budget phones shed value fastest.

The worked example (why budget maths bite)

Asurion puts a general professional screen replacement at $79 to $350 US depending on the model. Run three quotes through the rule for a phone bought new at $280, now worth roughly $140 used:

The total-cost lesson sits in the middle row: at flagship scale a $150 screen is a rounding error; at budget scale it is more than the phone's entire remaining value. This is also why the quote's composition matters. A cracked-screen guide lists the cost drivers plainly: the model and screen type (OLED costs more than LCD, foldables more again), and whether it is just the glass or the full display assembly that is damaged. And if the touchscreen itself does not respond — not merely cracked glass — that can signal deeper internal damage, which raises both the diagnosis cost and the chance the quote grows after opening.

Gate two: is there update support left to buy?

A repair buys time, and time is only worth buying if the phone stays safe and usable through it. Both frameworks above carry the same second condition in different words: still receiving security updates, and not at the end of its software lifecycle. A $79 screen on a phone with two years of updates left is a genuinely cheap two years. The identical repair on a phone whose updates have ended is renting a device that gets less safe every month — the honest comparison is then against a new budget phone with a full support runway, not against the repair quote alone. This site's software-update guide explains how to check where a given model stands; check it before the shop visit, not after.

The decision framework, in order

  1. One fault or several? A single cracked screen, tired battery or dead port is the repair case. Screen plus battery plus water damage is three quotes wearing a trench coat — replace.
  2. Get the real quote, itemised. Glass-only or full assembly? The gap between those two numbers is often the whole decision.
  3. Run gate one. Quote under half the phone's current value: proceed. Over: replace, unless the new-equivalent comparison and gate two both argue strongly the other way.
  4. Run gate two. Updates remaining, in years. Under a year of support left converts most "repair" verdicts to "replace at the next failure."
  5. Count the hassle honestly. Replacement carries the transfer work (the checklist on this site sequences it) and the old phone's trade-in or resale value, which offsets the new price. Repair carries none of that — credit it, but do not let convenience outvote a failed gate one by a wide margin.

This is a decision guide, not a how-to: there are deliberately no repair steps here, because opening a modern phone is a shop decision, not a kitchen-table one. What success looks like: you can state, in one sentence, the quote, the phone's current value, and its remaining update runway — and the verdict follows from those three numbers without further deliberation.

Common questions

Is a battery swap different from a screen? Usually kinder maths: it is the classic single, bounded fault on an otherwise healthy phone, and both frameworks treat batteries alongside screens and ports as the repairs that most often pass. It still has to clear the same two gates.

What about fixing it to sell? Run gate one against the repaired phone's resale value instead of its broken value. A repair that lifts resale by more than its cost is rational; one that merely returns the phone to its pre-crack value usually is not, at budget prices.

The phone still works with the crack. Do nothing? That is a legitimate third option for a cosmetic crack with full touch response — with two cautions: glass damage can worsen, and the unresponsive-touch symptom above converts "cosmetic" into "structural" the day it appears.

Why the flagship framing survives — and why to ignore it here

Repair guidance anchors on flagships because the industry does. When the comparison device costs $1,000 to $1,300 or more new, as Asurion notes of current flagships, almost any single repair is trivially worth it, and the interesting question becomes which shop to use. Advice written in that world exports badly: its rules of thumb still work — the half rule is the half rule — but its instincts ("a screen is always cheaper than a phone") stop being true somewhere around the $300 mark, quietly, without the articles noticing. The worked example above is the correction. Nothing about the phone changed; only the anchor did.

There is a second, quieter flagship assumption worth discarding: that a repaired phone returns to a long, supported life. Flagships carry long update commitments, so the repair buys years almost by default. Budget models vary far more in how long their makers keep them updated, which is why gate two is a gate and not a footnote on this site. Before the shop visit, find your model's update standing — the software-update guide on this site walks through where makers publish it — and write the remaining years next to the quote. Quote, current value, update runway: the decision is those three numbers, and any advice that uses fewer of them is guessing on your behalf.

The environmental argument, kept in its place

Repair is usually the lower-waste choice, and that counts for something real. It does not, however, repeal arithmetic: a $150 repair on an unsupported phone worth $140 is not rescued by being virtuous, because the likely outcome is a second purchase within the year — two transactions, two devices' worth of footprint, and the repair's cost spent twice. The genuinely green version of this decision is the same as the genuinely frugal one: repair when the gates pass and the phone will serve for years, replace when they fail and choose a replacement with a long support runway so the next decision is years away.

Our take: Under $300, quote first and sentiment second. Half of current value and a live update runway — a repair that fails either gate is a down payment on the replacement, whether you call it one or not.
