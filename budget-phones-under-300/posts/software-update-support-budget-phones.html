How long budget phones actually get software updates (and why it's the hidden spec)

Buying strategy · 6 min read

Battery size is on the box. Camera megapixels are on the box. The one spec that decides how long your phone stays safe to use — software-update support — is nowhere on the box, and it varies wildly under $300. Two phones at the same price can have support lives years apart. Here's what each budget lineup actually promises, what it means when support ends, and how to factor it into the price.

The spec nobody lists on the box

Every Android phone gets two kinds of updates: OS upgrades (new Android versions with new features) and security updates (patches for vulnerabilities). Security updates are the ones that matter most — they're what keep a phone safe for banking, email, and payments. A phone that stops getting them isn't just outdated; it's increasingly unsafe to use for anything sensitive.

Manufacturers set their own commitments, and the gap between the best and worst in the budget segment is enormous: up to 7 years at the top, as little as 1–2 years of OS upgrades at the bottom. That gap can matter more than any camera or battery spec.

Two warnings from the fine print. First, Motorola's support is poorly documented: one recent G-series announcement didn't even name the shipping Android version or any update commitment. Treat Motorola's numbers as best-case and expect slow rollouts. Second, these are promises, not guarantees — but makers that publish a number are held to it far more often than ones that stay silent.

What end of support actually costs you

When updates stop, three things happen in sequence:

  • Security risk climbs. Unpatched vulnerabilities accumulate, and attackers target exactly those gaps — a phone past its security-update window grows steadily less safe for sensitive tasks.
  • You're forced to replace the phone sooner. A Moto G 2025 buyer is realistically shopping again around 2028, when security coverage ends — while a Galaxy A16 buyer at a similar price keeps going into the next decade.

This is why a phone's "real price" isn't the sticker. It's the sticker divided by the years you can safely use it.

The cost-per-supported-year math

Take security-update coverage as the usable life of the phone:

  • Moto G 2025 (~$200, ~3 years of security coverage): roughly $67 per year of safe use.
  • Galaxy A16 5G (~$200, 6 years of updates): roughly $33 per year of safe use.

Same upfront price, half the annual cost — and two extra replacement cycles avoided. The pattern holds across the segment: the cheapest phone with the shortest support is frequently the most expensive phone per year. Before buying, divide the price by the security-update years. If a seller can't tell you the update commitment, treat that as information too.

How to check any model's remaining support before you buy

  1. Search "[model name] software update policy." Samsung and Google publish theirs; Motorola's is harder to find — absence of a published policy is itself a warning sign.
  2. Count from the launch date, not today. A "3 years of updates" promise on a phone launched 18 months ago means 18 months remain.
  3. Distinguish OS upgrades from security updates. A phone with 2 years of OS upgrades but 3 years of security patches is safe to use for 3 years — the OS number is the misleading one.
  4. Check when the model launched. Buying last year's budget phone at a discount is fine if the update clock started recently; it's a trap if you're buying into the tail of a short policy.

Our take: Under $300, software support is the spec most buyers ignore and the one that most affects total cost. The Galaxy A16 5G's six-year commitment at ~$200 makes it the value benchmark of the segment; Motorola's G line wins on price and features but loses on longevity. Divide price by security-update years before you buy — the "cheap" phone with two years of support is usually the expensive one.
