Camping lantern and campsite lighting basics: read the runtime before the lumens

Gear basics · 7 min read

The biggest number on the box is the least useful one. A lantern advertised at a very high lumen count and a run time in the hundreds of hours is quoting two figures that never happen at the same time, and the run time may not even be measured the way the lantern beside it was measured. The decision that actually shapes a campsite after dark is simpler and more useful: which light does which job, and which of the printed specs can be trusted. Get those two answers right and the specific product matters much less.

First, fix the runtime problem: two clocks, one shelf

Run time used to be measured generously. Under the old approach, a headlamp's clock ran until it could no longer produce usable light, defined as the light of a full moon at 2 metres. The newer standard ends run time at 10 percent of the light's original brightness. REI's headlamp guide works the example in full: a particular 350-lumen headlamp that measured 40 hours under the old standard measures 2 hours under the new one. The light did not get worse. It still provides diminished illumination afterwards. The clock simply stops earlier and more honestly.

The standard behind the new clock is ANSI/PLATO FL 1, developed by PLATO, a trade organization for portable lighting manufacturers. FL 1 sets firm rules for measuring lumens, beam distance, impact resistance, and waterproofing, and it fixes the end of rated run time at 10 percent of original brightness. A light that starts at 300 lumens reaches the end of its rated run time at 30 lumens, which is still brighter and more usable than the light of a full moon. The same shift explains the Petzl TIKKA example REI cites: a headlamp that once showed 60 hours at max power now advertises 2 hours, at a higher 300 lumens instead of 200.

Two practical consequences follow. First, run times are only comparable between lights measured under the same standard, so look for the FL 1 marking before comparing numbers across brands. Second, some brands add a Reserve run time beyond the FL 1 figure, based on being able to see 4 metres ahead instead of 2, so a box can carry two run times that are both honest and quite different. Neither figure is the marketing trick. The trick is comparing one brand's old-standard number against another brand's FL 1 number and calling the first light better.

Two lights, two jobs

A lantern and a headlamp are not smaller and larger versions of the same tool. Lumens gauge the total quantity of light emitted in all directions by a source, which is exactly what a lantern is for: glowing evenly over a table or a tent. A headlamp adds the second spec, beam distance, which tells how far usable light is projected in metres. Lumens describe how brightly a light glows at its source; beam distance describes how far it goes. Campsite lighting works when each tool is assigned the jobs its specs describe.

The table also explains why one bright lantern disappoints. A well diffused 300-lumen lantern can make camp feel brighter than a 600-lumen spotlight, because lumens measure output at the source while the light that lands on the table is what the cook actually uses. Diffusion, height above the table, and the right mode do more for a campsite than a larger headline number.

Colour is a working spec, not a mood

Light colour is printed on better packaging as a Kelvin figure, and it splits by task. Warm white, around 2700 to 3500 K, feels restful and reduces eye strain, which suits the tent and the last hour of the evening. Neutral light, around 4000 to 4500 K, makes food prep and map reading easier, which suits the kitchen end of the site. Neither is better in general. Each is better at a job, the same as the beams above.

Red mode earns its place on the headlamp for a physiological reason: red light does not cause pupils to shrink the way white light can, so it is good for nighttime use, when a camper needs to find a zipper or a water bottle without resetting everyone's night vision. One more output detail is worth knowing before a purchase. Regulated lights hold a steady brightness level instead of gradually dimming, which reads as quality until the batteries are exhausted and the light goes dark abruptly. Unregulated lights dim gradually, which is an early warning. On a site with a spare set of batteries in the tent, either behaviour is manageable. It only becomes a problem when it is a surprise.

Power source follows the trip, not the product

With the jobs assigned, the power question answers itself by trip type. For car camping, size and weight generally are not concerns, so the choice is about charging discipline. Rechargeable lights with built-in batteries are simple and sealed, but when one is empty it needs a USB power source. Models with removable rechargeable batteries allow a charged spare to be swapped in within seconds, and some lanterns also run on AAA batteries as a backup. Rechargeable batteries tend to lose power while sitting idle, so alkalines, which hold their charge well, make good backups in the glove box.

Cold changes the ranking. Alkaline batteries lose power quickly in temperatures below 20°F, while lithium batteries perform well in the cold. To extend alkaline life on a cold trip, carry the batteries under clothing during the day and sleep with them inside a sleeping bag at night. One caution travels with that advice: never use lithium or lithium-ion batteries in a light unless the manufacturer states that the specific light is designed for them. The wrong cell can damage the light it was meant to save.

Fuel lanterns still hold one genuine advantage: light intensity. When the job is lighting an entire large campsite, gas lanterns, burning liquid fuel, propane, or butane, put out more light than battery models. The costs are written just as plainly. Fuel lanterns need ample ventilation and must never be used in small, enclosed places. They generate considerable heat, which demands caution around children and flammable materials, and they are relatively noisy, heavy, and bulky. LED lanterns run the other way: long battery life, very good output, rugged, quiet, and exhaust-free, and because LEDs generate no heat, they are safe around kids. Candle lanterns sit at the far end of the scale, with soft light adequate for close-up tasks but minimal output, and one firm rule: a lit candle inside a tent, even inside a lantern casing, is a risky practice, and candle lanterns stay a safe distance from tent fabric.

The spec check: five steps before any camp light comes home

  1. Assign the jobs first. List the tasks the light must cover from the table above, table, tent, trail, overnight, and note which of them belong to a lantern and which to a headlamp before looking at any product.
  2. Check the runtime standard. Look for an FL 1 marking, then read the run time at the setting the light will actually use. Run times vary dramatically by setting, so never plan on maximum run time at maximum intensity. A lantern that runs 8 to 12 hours on medium is worth more than one that blasts on high for an hour, because medium is where most of the night is spent.
  3. Match the beam and the colour to the job. Flood for camp tasks and reading, spot for distance, adjustable for both. Warm white for the tent, neutral for cooking and maps, and a red mode if the light will be used after others are asleep.
  4. Choose the power source by the longest, coldest trip on the calendar. Built-in rechargeable for short trips with charging access, removable or replaceable cells where a swap beats a cable, and cold-rated thinking for any trip that could drop below 20°F. For outdoor use, a rating of at least IP65, protected against rain and dust, is a sensible floor.
  5. Weigh it only if the trip carries it. Compact lanterns around 4 to 5 ounces suit backpacking and small tents, larger lanterns of 10 ounces and up suit car camping and family tents, and most headlamps with batteries weigh less than 7 ounces either way.

What success looks like: every light at the site has an assigned job, the run time on each box was read at the setting and standard the trip will actually use, the low and red modes cover the overnight hours without waking the tent, and the power source still works on the coldest night of the trip, with a backup that holds its charge while it waits.
