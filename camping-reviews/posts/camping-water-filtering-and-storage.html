Camping water filtering and storage: how to filter and store drinking water while camping

Water · 9 min read

Filtered water is not automatically safe water, and safe water does not stay safe in a dirty bottle. Those two facts organise everything that follows. Camping water is a system with three jobs: pick the clearest source available, treat it with a method — or a pairing of methods — that covers what is actually in it, and then keep the treated side clean in storage. Skip the second job's fine print and a filter gets credited with protection it does not provide. Skip the third and the treatment is undone at the bottle cap.

Filtered is not the same as purified

The three groups of organisms behave differently, and REI's treatment guide separates them cleanly. Protozoa such as Cryptosporidium and Giardia travel in a hard cyst that resists certain chemicals, but their larger size makes them easier to filter out. Bacteria such as E. coli, Salmonella, and Campylobacter can be removed by water filters. Viruses such as hepatitis A, rotavirus, and norovirus are smaller and difficult to filter out — removing or neutralising them is purification, a step beyond filtering.

The CDC's backcountry guidance draws the practical conclusion. Boiling is the most reliable way to kill germs. If boiling is not possible, the next best option is to use a filter first — especially if the water is cloudy — and then use another treatment method. A standard filter on its own is not effective against viruses. That single sentence is the one most product pages leave out, and it decides how the methods below get combined.

How much water to plan for

Quantity planning starts from a simple rule of thumb published by Coghlan's: about half a gallon — roughly 2 litres — of water per person per day for drinking, plus additional water for cooking, washing dishes, and personal hygiene. Worked through for a real trip: four people on a three-day weekend need 4 × 2 litres × 3 days = 24 litres of drinking water alone, before a single pot is filled or dish washed. That number is the planning floor, not the total, and it explains why most groups treat water at camp or refill daily rather than carrying the whole supply. Where a treated supply is stored at the site, the storage system in the procedure below has to hold the drinking share plus the cooking and hygiene share the group actually uses.

Step by step: from source to storage bottle

  1. Choose the source. Prefer flowing water over still water. Scoop from near the surface, where the water is clearest.
  2. Deal with cloudiness first. Let sediment settle, or strain the water through a bandanna as a prefilter. Clearer water passes through a filter more swiftly and clogs it less, and sediment impairs treatment effectiveness generally.
  3. Wash or sanitise hands before water duty. REI's guide is blunt about the reason: many illnesses blamed on bad water are actually caused by poor hygiene. Use hand sanitiser before handling treated water, before food preparation, and after contact with a natural water source.
  4. Filter into the dirty-side container only. Keep two clearly designated sets of containers — dirty and clean — and never let untreated water touch the clean side.
  5. Close the virus gap where it matters. If boiling is practical, boil: a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet. If not, follow the CDC sequence — filter first, then disinfect — and follow the disinfectant's label, including its full contact time, before drinking.
  6. Store treated water on the clean side only. Cap the clean containers, keep them separate from the dirty set, and pour rather than dipping cups or hands into stored water.
  7. Carry a backup method. REI's advice is to always carry a backup treatment system; chemical treatment offers extra security at negligible weight if the filter fails, clogs, or freezes.
  8. Keep the site out of the source. Camp, dispose of human waste, and carry cleaning water at least 200 feet from water sources, and never toss soapy water directly into a source — disperse it on soil instead.

What success looks like: every container at camp is recognisably dirty-side or clean-side, treated water was filtered and then — where boiling was not used — disinfected for the label's full contact time, hands were sanitised before anyone handled the clean containers, and a backup treatment method is in the pack if the filter stops working.

Questions that come up at the water source

Is filtered water the same as purified water? No. Filtering removes bacteria and protozoa; viruses are smaller and difficult to filter out, and dealing with them — by boiling, disinfection, UV in clear water, or a filter-plus-disinfectant combination — is purification.

What happens if a water filter freezes? A hollow-fibre filter that freezes can crack, and a cracked membrane no longer filters to its rating. REI's filter guide suggests storing a filter that can freeze in a sleeping bag overnight — with the honest caveat that this does not help if daytime temperatures stay below freezing.

Does cloudy water just slow the filter down? It does more than that: sediment impairs treatment effectiveness, which is why settling or prefiltering murky water is a treatment step and not a cosmetic one.

Looking after the filter itself

A filter's rating describes a membrane in good condition, so condition is part of the system. Use the cleaning plunger supplied in the kit to backflush after trips and whenever flow slows — a clogging filter is usually a dirty one, and clearer source water and prefiltering stretch the interval. Protect the membrane from its two enemies: impact and ice. The freezing risk deserves its place in the packing routine on any shoulder-season trip, because the damage is invisible — a filter that froze and cracked looks exactly like a filter that did not, and only one of them is still removing 99.99999% of bacteria. When in doubt on a cold trip, the backup chemical treatment stops being a backup and becomes the method, which is the least dramatic ending a water system can have.
