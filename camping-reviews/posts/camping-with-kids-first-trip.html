Camping with kids: a first-trip plan that works in order

Family camping · 8 min read

A first camping trip with kids fails in predictable places, and almost none of them are on the gear list. Gear lists are what the guides agree on; what they leave scattered is the sequence — the order of decisions that keeps one bad hour from defining the whole trip and, with it, whether the kids ever ask to go again. Assembled from the strongest first-trip guidance, the system has four parts: rehearse at home, keep the first trip short, arrive with daylight to spare, and run the first hour on site like the riskiest hour of the trip — because it is.

Part one: the backyard rehearsal is not optional

Wildera's family camping checklist names skipping the practice run as one of the common first-trip mistakes, in the same breath as overpacking and ignoring the forecast: try the tent and the stove at home, before the trip depends on them. The rehearsal does three jobs at once. It exposes broken poles, missing pegs, and dead stove fuel while the hardware store is still an option. It teaches the adults the pitching sequence while nobody is tired, hungry, or losing light. And it gives the kids a first night under tent fabric in the safest possible setting, so the strange sounds and the zipper routine are familiar before they are also dark, distant, and exciting. A family that has slept one backyard night arrives at the campground with the two hardest unknowns — will the gear work, will the kids sleep — already answered.

Part two: one or two nights, close to home, a site the adults already know

The same checklist caps the first trip at one or two nights: long enough to feel like an adventure, short enough to survive if things go wrong. Little Adventure Shop adds the location half of the rule — for a first trip, pick a campsite the adults are already familiar with, and avoid sites too close to rivers, roads, or cliff-top edges. Both halves serve the same principle: a first trip should contain exactly one novelty (sleeping out) rather than five. A known campground removes the adults' orientation work — where the water is, where the toilets are, which loop is quiet — so their attention is free for the kids, and a short trip means any mistake has a near expiry date. Endurance is not the goal of trip one. A second trip being requested is the goal of trip one.

Part three: arrive early, eat simple, pack in bins

Thor Industries' first-trip guidance is blunt about timing: arrive as early as the site allows, with cushion to park, set up, and prepare a meal before dark. Every campsite's check-in time differs, so the plan is built backwards from sunset, not forwards from departure. The same guidance keeps the food deliberately boring — simple menus, one-pot meals, ingredients prepped and chopped at home — because a first trip's kitchen should produce dinner, not a project. Packing follows the same philosophy: lidded storage bins that organize gear by function and travel without sliding around, plus one small bin per child, filled by that child with toys, art supplies, a flashlight, or whatever matters to them. The child's bin is not indulgence; it is the piece of the trip the child controls, and it lives in one designated place instead of migrating through the tent all weekend.

Part four: the first hour gets jobs, not freedom

Little Adventure Shop names the hazard precisely: the first hour at a campsite is the one needing extra caution, because while the adults concentrate on pitching the tent, excited kids wander off to explore before anyone has their bearings. The fix the guide offers — and the one worth systematizing — is employment. Kids with jobs stay in the work zone; kids without them drift. Grade the jobs to the child:

JobWhat it involvesWhy it works
Peg passerCarries and hands over tent pegs one at a time, counts them aloudKeeps the youngest inside arm's reach of the pitching adult for the whole setup
Pole assemblerConnects shock-corded pole sections and lays them ready in orderA repeating, seated task that lasts exactly as long as pitching takes
Sleep-system unpackerUnrolls sleeping bags and pads inside the pitched tent, arranges pillows and sleep toysBuilds the child's own territory — and their investment in staying in it at bedtime
Marker scout (after setup)Walks the orientation loop with an adult and picks the landmark — a tree, a post, the site's own flag — that identifies the tent from a distanceConverts wandering energy into the safety skill itself: being able to find home base

The marker deserves its own sentence, because the same guide recommends pitching near something memorable precisely so kids can relocate the tent. Orientation is a group activity done together, right after setup — explore together, in the guide's phrase — not a solo adventure conducted during it. Wildera's mistake list supplies the last two first-hour traps: cold sleepers (insulation under kids matters more than the bag on top of them; extra pads are worth it) and the over-planned day (one or two activities is enough, a point Little Adventure Shop echoes — the novelty of the tent and outdoor cooking is itself the entertainment, and days do not need cramming).

The attitude clause, and the pack-down

Thor's guidance ends on the least mechanical and most honest note: things rarely go as planned, and when the adults keep an upbeat, can-do attitude, the kids follow their lead. That is not a platitude on a first trip; it is the mechanism by which a rained-out afternoon becomes a story instead of a verdict. Wildera closes the loop at the other end — a last walk around the site, trash packed out, tents dried before storage or dried at home to prevent mould, and the kit restocked immediately, so the second trip starts from ready rather than from a pile of damp gear in the garage. Do the pack-down properly and the answer to "can we go again?" is a date, not a project.

What success actually looks like on trip one

Set the bar before departure, because a first trip judged by adult standards — miles covered, sights seen, gourmet camp cooking — will read as a failure even when it worked. The standard the guidance points to is smaller and more durable: the kids slept in the tent, ate the simple dinner without a negotiation, held a real job during setup, found their way back to the tent by its marker, and asked at least once when the next trip is. Five small yeses beat one heroic day, because trip two is where family camping actually starts — with known gear, a known site rhythm, and children who already think of themselves as campers. Everything in the four-part system exists to protect that outcome: the rehearsal removes the gear surprises, the short familiar trip removes the endurance test, the daylight arrival removes the dark-and-stressed setup, and the first-hour jobs remove the one genuinely risky window. Judge trip one by whether it earns trip two, and almost every family that runs the sequence finds that it does.

FAQ

How long should a first trip be? One or two nights — long enough to feel like an adventure, short enough that a rough night expires quickly (Wildera).

What is the single most skipped preparation? The practice run: pitching the tent and lighting the stove at home first. It is on the named-mistakes list because families keep skipping it.

How early is "arrive early"? Early enough to park, pitch, and cook before dark, with cushion — built backwards from sunset and the site's check-in time, not from when the car happens to be loaded (Thor Industries).
