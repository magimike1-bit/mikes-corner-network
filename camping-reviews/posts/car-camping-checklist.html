The car camping checklist: pack enough, not everything

Gear · 6 min read

Car camping's whole advantage is that you don't have to carry anything — the car does it. The classic beginner mistake is treating that as permission to bring the entire garage. You end up spending the weekend managing stuff instead of camping. This checklist is the middle path: enough to be comfortable, not so much that setup takes three hours.

Shelter and sleep

  • Tent with its rainfly, poles, and stakes (count them before you leave — missing poles are discovered at the worst possible time).
  • Ground tarp or footprint under the tent — extends the tent floor's life and adds a moisture barrier.
  • Sleeping bags rated for the temperatures you'll actually face, not the season in general. A cold snap in September is still a cold snap.
  • Sleeping pads. This is the comfort item beginners underestimate most. A pad insulates you from the cold ground as much as it cushions you. Cheap foam works; inflatable pads are more comfortable.
  • Pillow — a real one from home beats a rolled-up jacket every time, and it costs you nothing to bring.

Kitchen and food

  • Camp stove + fuel and a lighter or matches in a waterproof bag. Test the stove at home before the trip.
  • Cooler + ice for perishables. Freeze water bottles instead of buying bagged ice — they double as drinking water as they melt.
  • Pots, pan, and basic utensils — one pot and one pan covers almost every camp meal. Add a cutting board, a knife, tongs, and a spatula.
  • Plates, bowls, cups, and cutlery — reusable, obviously. A dedicated "camp box" that lives in the garage saves repacking every trip.
  • Food storage that seals. Hard-sided bins with locking lids keep food organized and, just as important, keep smells contained. In wildlife country, food and scented items (sunscreen, toothpaste, soap) go locked in the car or a provided locker at night — never in the tent.
  • Dish soap, sponge, and a wash basin — plus a bag for trash. Leave-no-trace isn't a slogan; it's how you keep sites open for the next camper.

Clothing (the layer rule)

Temperatures swing hard between afternoon and 3 a.m. Pack in layers: a base layer, a warm mid-layer (fleece or wool), and a rain shell. Bring one more warm layer than you think you need — cold ruins trips faster than anything else. Include a warm hat and extra socks; wet feet and a cold head are miserable at midnight. Keep one set of clothes reserved for sleeping so you're never crawling into your bag damp from the day.

Light, tools, and camp comfort

  • Headlamps (one per person) plus a lantern for the picnic table. Headlamps win because your hands stay free.
  • Camp chairs — the single best comfort upgrade for the money.
  • Mallet or hammer for tent stakes, plus a few extra stakes.
  • Paracord or clothesline for a drying line and general fixes.
  • Multi-tool or camp knife.
  • Duct tape — wrapped around a water bottle so you don't pack a whole roll.

Safety gear (non-negotiable)

  • First-aid kit with the basics: bandages, antiseptic, pain relievers, tweezers, and any personal medications.
  • Weather awareness. Check the forecast the morning you leave and before bed each night. Have a plan for severe weather — a tent is not shelter from lightning. Know where the nearest sturdy building or vehicle is.
  • Fire safety. Keep fires small and inside the provided ring, never leave one unattended, and drown it completely before sleep — stir the ashes, add water, stir again until cold to the touch. During dry spells, check for fire bans before you go; if a ban is in effect, a camp stove is your cooking plan.
  • Water. Bring more drinking water than you expect to use, and know whether your campground has potable water taps before you count on them.
  • Navigation. Phone maps fail when the signal does. Download offline maps or bring a paper map of the area.

What to leave behind

The usual overpacking culprits: the full spice rack (salt, pepper, and oil cover 90% of camp cooking), a different outfit for every day (you're camping, not attending events), heavy hardcover books (one paperback), the Bluetooth speaker (your neighbours didn't come to hear your playlist — see our campground etiquette guide), and duplicate tools. After each trip, note what you never touched and drop it from the list next time.

Pack the box the same way every trip and the checklist becomes automatic. The goal isn't minimalism — it's knowing that everything in the car earned its spot.
