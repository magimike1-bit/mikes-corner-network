DIY Camp Kitchen Box: Build a Chuck Box That Fits Your Trunk

Projects · 7 min read

Why do the published plans disagree by a foot?

Search for chuck box plans and the dimensions scatter. One documented build finishes at 22 in wide by 14 in deep by 16.5 in tall. The Lee Valley Camp Kitchen/Chuck Box Plan (U-Bild, item 11L0269, US$13.95, checked Oct 25, 2026) finishes at 30 in long by 19 in deep by 17 in tall — a box half again as wide. Neither is wrong. Each author sized the box around their own stove, their own pots, and their own vehicle, then published the result as if the measurements were the design.

That is the first mistake, and it happens before any wood is cut: copying a plan's dimensions instead of its method. The second mistake is its mirror image — building a generous box that holds everything, then discovering the loaded result is a two-person lift. The third is treating the trunk as an afterthought, so a box that works beautifully on the workbench will not pass through the opening of the car it has to travel in.

Job summary. Difficulty: moderate — straight cuts and butt joints, no joinery tricks. Time: a weekend build, most of it in layout and finishing. Hire-a-pro line: there isn't one for the box itself; if the cuts cannot be made square at home, having the sheet goods broken down accurately at the lumber counter is the step to hand off. Everything below is ordered to prevent the three mistakes above: measure first, choose a size on purpose, build second.

Measure first: your stove, your kit, your trunk opening

One published guide gives the right instruction and then stops short of a method: measure the camping gear first, especially the stove with fuel, before cutting anything. Here is that instruction turned into a procedure — do it with the actual gear on a table, not from memory or product listings:

  1. Set up the stove exactly as it travels. Fuel attached or stowed the way it rides in the box, lid and windscreen in their travelling position. Measure length, width, and height of that whole bundle. The stove is the least compressible item in the kitchen, so it sets the minimum interior in all three directions.
  2. Stack the cook kit the way it will live in the box. Pots nested, plates and utensils in their piles. Measure the tallest stack and the widest item; a box that fits the stove but not the pot stack has failed at the same job.
  3. Measure the trunk or cargo opening, not the cargo floor. The opening is the narrowest point the finished box must pass through, at the angle it will actually be lifted in. Write that number down and treat it as a hard ceiling on the box's two smaller dimensions.
  4. Sketch the interior before choosing a plan. Lay the stove and kit measurements inside a rectangle and see which published size — or which size between them — contains the gear with the compartments still usable. This sketch, not a plan's headline dimensions, is the build specification.

Success check at this stage: three written numbers — stove bundle, tallest kit stack, trunk opening — and a sketch that fits the first two inside a box that clears the third. No numbers, no cutting.

A plan is worth following for its joinery, its cutting schedule, and its compartment layout — all of which transfer. Its outside dimensions transfer only when the gear behind them matches yours.

Cut list and materials for a one-sheet build

Both documented approaches build from the same stock: a single 4 × 8 ft sheet of 1/2 in plywood. The hardware list is short — hinges for the countertop, latches that close securely, magnetic closures, wood glue, and screws. The finish on the documented compact build is shellac over sanded plywood, which suits a box that will be wiped down after every trip.

Two design features are worth copying from the sources regardless of size. First, a sturdy lid that doubles as a workspace: the compact build's flip-down countertop is the working surface the whole kitchen is organised around, and a lid that is only a lid wastes the box's best face. Second, latches that hold the box shut under load — a kitchen box travels full of loose metal, and a latch that pops open in the trunk redistributes the contents across the cargo area.

Lay the cut list out on the sheet drawing before cutting. A one-sheet build has no spare panel hiding in the stack; a mis-cut carcass side is a second trip to the lumber store, and the sheet layout is where that gets prevented.

Assembly order that avoids the usual mistakes

  1. Dry-fit the carcass first. Stand the panels up at the sketched dimensions and set the stove bundle inside before any glue. This is the last cheap moment to discover the interior is a quarter-inch short.
  2. Glue and screw the carcass, then check it against the trunk opening. Carry the empty box to the car and pass it through the opening now, while a fix is still a trim rather than a rebuild.
  3. Fit the compartments around the gear, not the plan's drawing. Set dividers where the measured stacks actually sit. Seven compartments is what the compact build uses; the right number for this box is the one that keeps the stove, the pot stack, and the utensil pile from trading places in transit.
  4. Hang the countertop lid and fit the latches under load. Load the box, close it, and latch it. Latches fitted to an empty box can sit in slightly the wrong place once the contents press outward.
  5. Sand and finish last. The documented build is sanded and shellacked after assembly; finishing panels beforehand puts a film exactly where the glue joints need bare wood.

Success check: the loaded, latched box passes through the trunk opening without tilting gymnastics, the countertop opens flat into a usable work surface, and the stove comes out and goes back in without unpacking anything else first.

Loaded weight and the one-person carry test

Only one of the documented builds reports a loaded weight: about 35 lb for the compact 22 in box, fully packed. Treat that figure as the reference point it is. A box built eight inches wider, with the extra gear that width invites, will weigh more — plywood, stove, fuel, pots, and food staples all scale with volume, and none of them announce the total until the first lift.

So weigh the finished box loaded, on a bathroom scale, before the first trip. Then run the carry test that actually matters: one person, box lifted from the ground, carried the distance from driveway to car, and loaded through the opening without a rest stop. If that test fails, the fix is not stronger intentions — it is splitting the load. Heavy, dense items such as fuel and the stove can travel in a separate small tote, leaving the box as the organiser for everything lighter. A chuck box that needs two people to move is a storage crate, not camp equipment.

First trip out: what to move, shrink, or leave home

The first trip is the box's review. Note which compartment was opened constantly and which was never touched, what rattled, and what had to be dug for. Move the daily items to the top and the countertop side, shrink any compartment that travelled half-empty, and leave home whatever did not get used — the box earns its trunk space by what it organises, and every unused item inside it is weight carried for nothing. Then re-run the carry test with the revised load. The build is finished when the box, the gear list, and the one-person lift all agree.
