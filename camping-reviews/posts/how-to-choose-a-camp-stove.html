How to choose a camp stove: match the burner to the menu, not the BTU headline

Gear · 7 min read

The biggest number on the box is close to useless. Shoppers compare camp stoves by total BTUs, buy the highest figure in the budget, and then discover the stove scorches rice, empties its cylinder by Saturday, or sulks on the first cold morning. Heat output is a maximum, not a cooking style. The job summary for this purchase is simpler than the marketing: match the stove to the menu you actually cook, the number of people eating, and the coldest night you camp in. Get those three right and almost any reputable stove works. Get them wrong and no BTU figure rescues the trip.

Difficulty of choosing well: low, once the categories are separated. The cost of choosing badly is a second purchase, because stove categories do not stretch. A backpacking burner cannot become a family stove, and a two-burner car stove will never ride in a pack.

Two documented stoves, one honest table

Start with the two ends of the range, using manufacturer numbers only. At one end sits a small upright canister stove, the MSR PocketRocket 2. At the other, a two-burner propane car stove, the Coleman Classic. The specs below are the manufacturers' published figures, checked 1 November 2026 against the MSR spec sheet and Coleman's product page.

Read the burn-time row carefully, because it reverses the usual assumption. The little stove and the big stove both quote roughly an hour of full-power burning from one standard fuel container. The difference is where the heat goes: one pot, boiled fast, or two pans cooking at once. That is a menu difference, not a power difference.

Two more categories complete the shelf. Integrated canister systems pair the burner with a matched pot and a built-in windscreen; REI's stove guide describes them as the fastest way to boil water, designed for boiling rather than simmering, and heavier and more prone to tipping than a plain canister stove. Liquid-fuel stoves burn white gas from a refillable bottle, sit low and stable, and keep performing in cold weather and at high elevation where canisters fade. Their price is work: priming before each light, pumping, and periodic maintenance.

Decide by menu, group, and coldest night

Run three questions in order. Each answer removes a category, so the shelf narrows before brand names enter the discussion.

1. What does dinner look like? Boil-only food, coffee, oatmeal, dehydrated meals, points to an integrated system or a small canister stove. Real cooking, where a sauce has to hold at a simmer while something else fries, points to a stove with genuine flame adjustment. REI notes that most canister stoves simmer well and adjust easily; integrated systems treat simmering as an afterthought. Two pans at once settles the question outright: that is the two-burner car stove's entire reason to exist.

2. How many people eat from one stove? One or two people fit a single burner and a single pot. A family cooking pasta and sauce simultaneously does not, no matter how high the single burner's output climbs. Pot support matters here too: REI warns that a small canister stove's arms may not hold large pots securely, which is a hard physical limit, not a preference.

3. How cold is the coldest night? Canister fuel is a blend of isobutane and propane held under pressure, and REI is direct about the failure mode: in cold weather a canister can depressurize and produce a weak flame. A pressure regulator improves matters, and keeping the canister warm helps keep pressure up, but a stove bought for winter camping or high elevations belongs in the liquid-fuel category, which maintains full performance in those conditions. Shoulder-season campers near freezing should treat the regulator as a requirement, not an upgrade.

Before first use: the checks that prevent the bad first night

  1. Fit your largest pan at home. Set the pan you actually cook in on the supports. It should sit level with no wobble. Success looks like this: the pan stays put when nudged gently, empty, on a flat counter.
  2. Check every connection before lighting. REI's usage guidance starts here for any stove: inspect fuel lines, valves and connections for leaks or damage before the first flame, and set the stove on the most level surface available.
  3. Carry a second way to light it. A built-in piezo igniter is convenient and can still fail; REI advises carrying stormproof matches regardless. A stove that cannot light is a cold dinner, not an inconvenience with a workaround.
  4. Plan the fuel, not just the stove. Work from burn time, not hope. Using the figures in the table, a weekend of two dinners and two breakfasts fits comfortably inside one 227 g canister for a small stove used mostly at a boil, but a near-empty canister loses performance, per REI's fuel guidance, so a part-used canister from last season is the classic false economy. Start the trip on a fresh one and keep the part-used one for home emergencies.

The three ways this purchase goes wrong

Wrapping a windscreen around an upright canister stove. This is the dangerous mistake, and buying guides rarely flag it. REI states it plainly: a windscreen should not be used with an on-canister stove, because it can trap excessive heat and lead to the fuel exploding. Shield the stove with a pack, a cooler or your own position instead, or choose a design with a built-in screen. Never improvise the wrap.

Buying for the brochure menu. A two-burner stove bought for gourmet intentions, then used for two seasons of boiled water, is dead weight and dead counter space on every trip. The honest menu from the last three trips is the specification sheet that matters.

Cooking where the stove should never be. REI's first usage rule carries no exceptions: do not cook inside a tent or an enclosed space, because of carbon monoxide poisoning and fire risk. A vestibule in the rain is still an enclosed space for this purpose. Cook outside, sheltered by position and gear, or eat the no-cook backup.

Safety note: this guide is general information drawn from manufacturer specifications and retailer guidance checked on 1 November 2026. Follow the instructions supplied with your stove, keep fuel away from heat in storage and transport, and check local fire restrictions before every trip, a stove that is legal in one place may be the only cooking method allowed, or restricted, in another.
