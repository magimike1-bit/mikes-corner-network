How to clean and store a tent after a trip: the dry-first routine that prevents mildew and coating failure

Gear care · 9 min read

The one rule everything else hangs on: never store it wet

REI's tent-care guide ranks its rules without much suspense: make sure the tent is 100 percent dry before storing it, because no tent-care rule is more important. MSR's support guidance explains what the clock actually is — it can take as few as 24 hours for mildew to start forming on a wet, crumpled tent, and that mildew stains, smells, and compromises the waterproof coating. Two separate damage tracks run off the same damp bundle. The visible one is mildew itself. The slower one is chemical: MSR names it hydrolysis, the premature breakdown of the waterproof coating under prolonged exposure to moisture, which leaves the coating layer soft, sticky and no longer waterproof. REI describes the end state the same way — over time, moisture chemically breaks down coatings, and damp fabrics grow the mildew that harms polyurethane coatings in the meantime.

The warranty footnote makes the stakes concrete rather than sentimental: MSR states that mildew and moisture damage are not covered under its Limited Warranty. A tent ruined in storage is a tent the owner pays to replace. That is why this guide is organised as a routine with a deadline, not a collection of tips: the first day home is when the outcome is decided, and everything after that — washing choices, storage container, storage place, the pre-trip check — is about not undoing a dry tent or rescuing a damp one in time.

Triage first: how much cleaning does this tent actually need?

The most common care mistake after a wet bundle is the opposite one: over-cleaning a tent that only needed drying. MSR is explicit that a tent only needs cleaning if it has an offensive odour or becomes heavily soiled, and that most loose dirt comes off with water pressure from a regular garden hose. REI sets the routine cadence: clean after any long trip, especially one with exposure to sand, fine dust, bird droppings or tree sap, and give the tent a gentle cleaning once per season if the pattern is frequent short trips. One triage warning belongs in the table's margin: the coating test is done by feel and sight on the inside of the fabric, where REI says peeling usually shows first. A fly that is merely dirty washes clean; a fly whose coating comes away tacky under a thumb is a different problem wearing the same colours, and washing it harder accelerates the failure instead of reversing it.

The first day home: the dry-first procedure

Run this on the day of return — the 24-hour mildew figure from MSR is the deadline the whole sequence is built against:

  1. Unpack the tent the day you get home, whatever the weather. Even a well-ventilated tent accumulates condensation, often under the floor and the rainfly (REI), and a tent packed up wet at the site must be dried as soon as you get back.
  2. Shake it out and clear the debris. REI's breakdown routine starts with shaking the tent out and cleaning out dirt and debris before anything else. Fjallraven's version of the same step: brush the inside to remove sand, twigs and leaves.
  3. Pitch it or hang it, in the right place. Sunny day: set it up in the yard. Wet weather: hang it in a garage or a non-carpeted room (REI). Space-limited: drape it or hang it until dry — REI adds there is no such thing as too much drying time, and the tent can be set up indoors or in a shaded outdoor spot. Fjallraven's drying standard is a well-ventilated area, preferably outdoors, until fully dry.
  4. Dry the hidden surfaces deliberately. Turn attention to the underside of the floor and the underside of the fly — the two places REI names as condensation collectors — plus seams and corners, where a tent can feel dry on its panels while moisture still sits in the folds.
  5. Handle the poles the careful way while they are out. Start in the middle when breaking down a shockcorded pole, so tension distributes evenly along the cord, and push rather than pull when removing a pole from the tent (REI). Never whip a pole to snap its sections together — a chipped section is a weakened pole.
  6. Clean the small metal parts if the trip involved sand or salt. Fjallraven singles out beach camping: clean the poles and zips too, because sand and salt are bad news for metal parts.
  7. Only then, triage and wash if the table says wash. Drying comes first because a wash adds a full second drying cycle; a tent that goes into storage washed but damp has been made worse, not better.

Success check: every panel, the floor underside, the fly underside, seams and corners are dry to the touch with no cool or damp patches, there is no musty smell when the tent is held closed for a moment, and the zips run freely. If any one of those fails, the tent goes back up or back on the line — it does not go into storage on a promise.

Washing, when the triage says wash

The method both manufacturers converge on is boring on purpose. Hand wash only: MSR's heavy-duty version is the tent pitched, washed with warm water, a sponge and a mild, non-detergent soap, rinsed well and dried fully while pitched or hung; REI's basic version is a non-abrasive sponge, cold water and non-detergent soap, scrubbing soiled areas gently by hand. Three prohibitions carry no exceptions in either source. Never machine-wash or machine-dry: REI warns that a washer — especially a top-loader with an agitator — can stretch or tear fabric, mesh and seams, and that dryers do the same and add damaging heat; MSR adds dry cleaning to the banned list. Avoid the strong household shelf: MSR names dishwashing liquid, detergent, bleach and similarly concentrated solutions as coating removers, and REI's list — harsh dish soaps, bleach, spot removers, laundry presoaking products — adds a second reason beyond coating damage: most household soaps are perfumed, and perfumed fabric attracts insects, rodents and bigger creatures, while the soaps also impair the durable water repellent (DWR) coating. And rinse completely: soap left in the fabric is residue the next damp week works on.

A seam note for tents that need resealing as part of the wash day: plan the space and the clock before starting. REI notes that most tent sealing products need 24 hours to dry completely, so the tent needs a designated, debris-free place where it can stay spread out for a day — and the sealer must match the fabric: most rainflies are polyester or nylon with a polyurethane coating, a few are silicone-infused nylon that never needs recoating, and each material requires a sealer formulated for it.

Storage as a system: container plus place

Storage advice usually stops at “store it loose.” The full system has two halves — the container and the place — and both halves have a wrong answer:

Two habits extend the system between trips. Keep boots outside the tent or in the vestibule during the trip itself, because the dirt, grit and pebbles that fall onto the floor are positioned to abrade and puncture it (REI) — storage care starts at the campsite. And pitch on a footprint: REI notes that a footprint protects the floor from abrasion, and because it does not extend beyond the floor's perimeter, it will not collect rainwater the way a generic ground cloth can. Less floor damage and less trapped water both mean less work in the post-trip routine.

Before the next trip: the airing check

Storage is not quite the end of the routine. Fjallraven's closing step is to take the tent out of storage a day or two before heading out, air it thoroughly, check that no mould has formed, confirm all the parts are present, and check the zips — which may need a little oil or wax to run smoothly again. That timing is the point: a mould patch, a missing stake bag or a seized zip found two days before departure is a cleaning job; the same discovery at the trailhead is a ruined trip. While the tent is up, REI's use-phase checks ride along cheaply — inspect seams periodically for damage, and keep UV exposure in mind across the season, since ultraviolet rays degrade canopy and rainfly fabrics over time and a tent left pitched in direct sun for extended periods ages faster for it.

FAQ

Can a tent go in the washing machine on a gentle cycle? No. Both REI and MSR prohibit machine washing outright; REI's reasoning is mechanical — agitators stretch and tear fabric, mesh and seams — not a matter of cycle choice.

How long can a tent stay packed wet? Treat 24 hours as the outer limit: MSR states mildew can start forming in as few as 24 hours on a wet, crumpled tent. Unpack and dry the same day whenever possible.

Is a light dusting of dirt worth a wash? Usually not. MSR's threshold is an offensive odour or heavy soiling; loose dirt comes off with a garden hose, and Fjallraven's short-trip standard is a brushed interior and a lukewarm rinse of the outside.

The fly feels sticky inside. Can that be saved? A sticky or soft coating is hydrolysis — breakdown, per MSR, not dirt. REI's path for a peeling coating is cleaning off flakes and recoating worn areas, but a gooey coating with a foul odour points to checking for a replacement rainfly instead.

What about the zips? Be gentle and never force one: REI's technique for a stuck zipper is to hold the track with one hand and gently back the slider up, wiggling it side to side until the trapped fabric frees. Clean zips after sandy or salty trips (Fjallraven), and check them again at the pre-trip airing.
