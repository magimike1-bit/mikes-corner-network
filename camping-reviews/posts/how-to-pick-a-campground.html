How to pick a campground (without guessing wrong)

Planning · 5 min read

Most disappointing camping trips come down to one thing: the wrong campground for the trip you actually wanted. A family with small kids and a group of backcountry hikers need completely different places — and the campground itself won't tell you which one it is. Here's how to figure it out before you book.

Start with the trip you want, not the campground

Before you look at a single listing, answer three questions:

  • How far do you want to drive? A gorgeous park three hours away is worse than a decent one forty minutes away if you only have a weekend. Long drives eat into setup time and arrive-at-dusk starts are miserable.
  • What are you actually going to do there? Swimming, fishing, hiking, sitting by a fire, letting the kids run — different campgrounds are good at different things. A park with a great beach and nothing else is perfect for swimmers and boring for everyone else.
  • How self-sufficient are you? First-time campers usually do better with flush toilets, drinking water taps, and a camp store nearby. Experienced campers may happily trade all that for quieter, more private sites.

Read the facilities list carefully

Campground listings use shorthand that beginners misread. A few terms worth knowing:

  • Electrical vs. non-electrical sites. Electrical sites give you a power hookup — handy for medical devices, charging, or a fan. They're also closer together and noisier on average.
  • Comfort stations vs. vault toilets. Comfort stations have flush toilets and showers; vault toilets are outhouses. It matters more than you think on a three-day trip.
  • Potable water. Not every campground has drinking water at the site. If it doesn't, you'll be carrying water in — check whether there are taps elsewhere in the park.

Site types: what "pull-through," "radio-free," and "walk-in" really mean

If you're tenting, pay attention to site dimensions and privacy rather than hookups. "Radio-free" areas prohibit amplified music — a good sign for light sleepers. Walk-in sites (a short carry from the car) tend to be quieter and more private because they're slightly less convenient. If privacy matters, look at campground maps for sites bordered by trees rather than open fields.

Check the rules that would ruin your trip

Every campground has rules; the question is whether they conflict with your plans. Common trip-killers:

  • Quiet hours and generator rules — usually enforced more strictly than people expect.
  • Pet policies — some parks restrict dogs on beaches or trails; some prohibit pets entirely in certain loops.
  • Firewood rules — many parks prohibit bringing firewood from outside the area (to slow the spread of tree pests). Buy it where you camp.
  • Alcohol and visitor policies — enforcement varies, but surprise rules are the worst kind.

Use reviews the smart way

Camper reviews are useful, but read them for patterns, not opinions. One person's "dead quiet" is another's "boring." Ignore star ratings and look for repeated specifics: "train noise," "sites are close together," "great showers," "mosquitoes were brutal in June." Also check when the review was written — a campground in October is a different experience than the same one in July.

Season and timing change everything

The same site can be five different trips depending on the date. Shoulder seasons (late spring, early fall) mean fewer crowds and easier bookings but also cooler nights and sometimes closed facilities — check whether comfort stations and water are on for your dates. Peak summer weekends book out far in advance at popular parks, so plan ahead or aim for weekdays. And watch the weather history for the area: low-lying sites near rivers are lovely until a wet week turns them into a swamp.

Safety basics before you book

Picking a campground is also a safety decision. Check the area's typical wildlife — in bear country you'll need to plan for proper food storage (locked in your vehicle or a provided locker, never in the tent). Note the distance to the nearest hospital or urgent care, especially when camping with kids or anyone with medical conditions. And check fire conditions: during dry spells, fire bans are common and enforced. A trip planned around campfires needs a backup plan if the ban sign is up.

The short version: match the campground to the trip, read the fine print on facilities and rules, and trust repeated review patterns over ratings. Do that and you'll guess wrong a lot less often.
