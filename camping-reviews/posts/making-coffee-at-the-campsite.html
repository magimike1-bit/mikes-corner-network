Making coffee at the campsite: methods compared on grind, time and cleanup

Food · 8 min read

The gear is rarely what ruins campsite coffee. Water poured at a rolling boil, a grind that does not match the brewer, and grounds left sitting in the finished coffee do more damage than a modest pot ever will. REI makes the same point from the other direction: the secret to great coffee at camp is knowing how to make great coffee, with recipe, grind size and water as the three variables that matter. Fix those three, in morning order, and the method choice becomes a question of group size and cleanup, not rescue work.

Start with the ratio that anchors every method below. According to the Specialty Coffee Association, cited by REI, the ideal brewed coffee ratio is 1 part coffee to 16 parts water, adjusted to taste. That is 20 grams of coffee to 320 grams of water, or 30 grams to 480 grams. Without a scale, REI gives the field measure of 2 level tablespoons of whole bean coffee for every 6 ounces of water. Pre portion each batch at home into small reusable containers or sealable bags, and use a marked water bottle to keep the water side consistent.

Heat the water first, and let it come off the boil

Water temperature is the first morning decision because every method waits on it. REI puts the ideal brew temperature at 205 degrees Fahrenheit at the start of brewing, with the water not dipping below 195 degrees Fahrenheit during the process. Boiling water scorches grounds and pushes the cup toward bitterness, while water that is too cool under extracts and tastes sour. The campsite technique needs no thermometer: bring the water to a boil, remove it from the heat for approximately 30 seconds, and it should cool to about 205 degrees Fahrenheit. At higher elevation water boils at a lower temperature, which is why REI suggests a cold brew concentrate made ahead as the summit alternative.

Grind follows temperature. Coarser grounds need a longer brew because water takes longer to soak through each larger particle, and finer grounds extract faster because more surface area is exposed. A burr grinder gives the most even result. Grinding in advance sacrifices a little freshness but saves weight and bulk, so match the grind to the brewer at home and pack it sealed.

Read the cleanup column as a real cost, not a footnote. Aerial Resupply Coffee ranks the same methods by cleanup in the same order the table shows: the AeroPress puck pops out, a pour over filter lifts out, and a French press or percolator leaves wet grounds to deal with at a cold picnic table. Cowboy coffee, the zero gear fallback, sits at the messy end as well, with grounds loose in the pot. The method a group will happily clean is often the method that survives a three day trip.

Percolator timing deserves its own warning because the brewer keeps extracting while it sits on the heat. A percolator cycles boiling water through the grounds over and over, which is why it turns bitter fast. Use a coarse grind, keep it at a gentle perk instead of a rolling boil, and pull it off the heat after 6 to 8 minutes, as soon as the coffee in the glass knob looks right.

Brew the morning cup, step by step

The procedure below uses a French press, the group method in the table. The water, ratio and timing steps transfer to the other methods with their own grind and brew times from the table.

  1. Measure before the water boils. Add coarse ground coffee to the press at 1 part coffee to 16 parts water, for example 30 grams of coffee for 480 grams of water, and shake the brewer gently until the grounds are level. Success looks like this: the dose was measured at home or by tablespoon count, not guessed from the bag.
  2. Heat, rest, then pour. Bring the water to a boil, take it off the heat for approximately 30 seconds, then pour it in, saturating all the grounds. Stir once gently if needed so every ground is wet.
  3. Hold the grounds under water for 4 to 5 minutes. Rest the lid on the brewer and depress the plunger just until it sits under the water level, so all the coffee stays submerged. Set a timer rather than estimating.
  4. Press slowly and pour promptly. After 4 to 5 minutes, press the plunger gently to the bottom. If it resists, do not force it. REI notes that resistance is generally caused by too fine a grind, so back the plunger up a little and continue. Pour the coffee out rather than leaving it sitting on the grounds.
  5. Check the cup before you repeat it. A balanced cup tastes sweet with a pleasant aftertaste. Bitterness with a long lingering aftertaste points to over extraction, and a sour taste with a short, drying finish points to under extraction. Change one variable next time, grind, ratio or water temperature, and note which one.

What success looks like: a full mug at the right strength, a press that is empty rather than still brewing, and a grounds plan ready before the next task of the morning starts.

Clean up as part of the brew, not after it

REI closes its camp coffee guide with Leave No Trace, and the rules are specific. Store coffee beans, grounds and even instant coffee packages securely, in a bear canister, locker or other secure location as the campsite recommends, because they can attract wildlife. Pack out used filters and coffee grounds. Grounds should not be scattered or buried in the wilderness, because they are foreign to the local ecosystem, can attract wildlife, and can cause illness if ingested. Disposable filters need packing out as well, and a reusable metal or cloth filter reduces what you carry out at the cost of water to clean it.

Leftover brewed coffee gets the wastewater treatment. If the campsite has a wastewater drain, use it. Otherwise dilute the coffee considerably and dispose of it at least 100 feet away from campsites and shelters, and 200 feet from any water source. When in doubt, pour it into a sealable receptacle and deal with it at home. At an Ontario Parks site, that approach also fits the park rule already covered on this site: wash dishes on site in a basin, never at the water taps, and take grey water to a sanitation station or vault toilet.

Questions campers ask about coffee outside

What ratio should a beginner start with? Start at 1 part coffee to 16 parts water for a press, pour over or percolator, and 1 to 8 for an AeroPress or a cold brew concentrate, then adjust to taste. The AeroPress ratio looks stronger because the brew is a smaller, concentrated cup that can be diluted.

How long does each method take once the water is hot? An AeroPress takes 2 to 3 minutes, a pour over 3 to 5 minutes, a French press 4 to 5 minutes, and a percolator 10 to 12 minutes, per the REI cheat sheet. Cold brew is the outlier at 12 to 24 hours, which is why it is made before the trip or the night before.

Can coffee be made with no brewer at all? Yes, as cowboy coffee. Boil water in any pot, let it sit 30 seconds off the heat, stir in about 2 tablespoons of coarse ground coffee per 8 ounces of water, steep 4 minutes, add a small splash of cold water to help the grounds sink, and pour slowly, leaving the last inch in the pot where the grounds settle.
