Tent buying basics: choosing one without overthinking it

Gear · 5 min read

Tent shopping paralyzes people because there are hundreds of options and every review swears a different spec is the one that matters. For car camping — where you're not carrying the tent on your back — the decision is much simpler than the internet makes it look. Four things matter. Everything else is garnish.

1. Capacity: buy bigger than the label says

Tent "person" ratings are based on sleeping shoulder-to-shoulder with no gear inside. A 4-person tent fits four sleeping pads and almost nothing else. The working rule: subtract one from the rated capacity for comfortable camping with gear. Two campers? Look at 3- or 4-person tents. A family of four? Six-person tents are the sweet spot. The extra space costs little for car camping and transforms rainy afternoons from miserable to tolerable.

2. Season rating: 3-season is right for almost everyone

Tents are rated by season: 3-season (spring, summer, fall) or 4-season (built for snow loads and harsh winter wind). Unless you're specifically winter camping, buy a 3-season tent. Four-season tents are heavier, less ventilated, and overkill for fair-weather camping — you'll be hot and clammy in July. Within 3-season tents, ventilation matters: look for mesh panels and vents that reduce condensation, because a tent that rains on you from the inside is a special kind of disappointment.

3. Setup: the best tent is the one you'll actually pitch

Here's the honest truth about tent design: freestanding dome tents are the default recommendation for beginners for good reason. They go up fast, they're forgiving of uneven ground, and you can move them around before staking. Set the tent up at home or in the yard before your first trip — every experienced camper has a story about learning their new tent in the dark, in the rain, at 10 p.m. Larger cabin-style tents give you standing room and feel like a small apartment, but they take longer to pitch and catch more wind. For car camping that's a fair trade if comfort matters more than speed.

4. Weather protection: the rainfly and the floor

Two features do most of the weatherproofing work:

  • A full-coverage rainfly that reaches close to the ground, not a skimpy cap that leaves mesh walls exposed. In a real storm, partial rainflies let wind-driven rain straight in.
  • A bathtub floor — the floor fabric wraps up the sides a few inches, so water running under the tent doesn't wick inside. Seam sealing matters too; some tents come pre-sealed, others need a weekend of DIY with seam sealer.

Add a ground tarp underneath (folded so it doesn't stick out past the tent walls — exposed tarp collects rain and funnels it under you) and you're set for most weather car campers actually encounter.

What doesn't matter much for car camping

  • Packed weight. Critical for backpacking, nearly irrelevant when the car carries it. Don't pay a premium to shave grams.
  • Ultralight materials. Thinner fabrics save weight at the cost of durability and price. For car camping, slightly heavier and tougher is better.
  • Brand prestige. Fit, features, and condition matter more than the logo. A well-kept used tent from a reputable maker beats a flimsy new one.

New vs. used

Tents are one of the better used-gear buys, since a lightly used tent can cost half of new. Inspect before buying: check zippers (the most common failure point), look for UV damage or delamination on the rainfly coating (flaky, sticky, or peeling coating means it's done), and ask to see it set up — missing poles or a torn floor are deal-breakers unless the price reflects them. If buying used isn't your thing, entry-level tents from established outdoor brands are genuinely fine for car camping; you don't need the expedition model.

After you buy: the boring maintenance that makes it last

Three habits double a tent's life: never pack it wet (if you must, set it up to dry within a day of getting home), store it loosely in a cool dry place rather than compressed in its stuff sack for months, and keep it clean — a quick shake-out and occasional sponge-down beats ground-in grime. Re-waterproof the rainfly when water stops beading on it. That's it. No rituals, no special products.

Pick a size up from what you need, 3-season, full rainfly, and practice the setup at home. Everything beyond that is preference — and preference is earned on trips, not in the store.
