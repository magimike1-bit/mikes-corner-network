Thunderstorm and lightning safety at the campground: where to shelter, and the 30-minute clock

Camp safety · 9 min read

Safety note: this guide summarises published guidance from the National Weather Service, Ontario Parks and the National Park Service for general information. It is not a substitute for park staff instructions or emergency services — in an emergency, call 911.

A tent keeps rain off. It does not ground a strike

Start with the fact that decides everything else: a tent is rain shelter, not lightning shelter. The National Park Service states it plainly — tents do not protect you from lightning. The National Weather Service goes further: there is no 100 percent safe shelter outside, and the only places of safety from lightning are inside a vehicle or a substantial, enclosed structure. That single distinction reorganises the whole problem. The question at a campground is never “how do I make the tent safer?” It is “where is the real shelter, how long does it take to reach, and when do I leave?” Those three questions can all be answered on arrival, in daylight, before any cloud builds — and answering them then is the entire strategy, because once thunder is audible the National Park Service is blunt about the timing: if you can hear thunder, you are already at risk of being struck, so do not wait.

The season makes this a planning item, not an edge case. Ontario Parks notes that thunderstorms are common in all parts of Ontario from late April to early October — essentially the whole camping season. The first control is the forecast, checked the right way: Ontario Parks stresses checking the forecast for the area local to the park, not your hometown forecast, and the Weather Service's first safety bullet is simply to check the forecast before the trip. If thunderstorms are imminent, the Weather Service's advice is to postpone the outdoor activity — and Ontario Parks applies the same logic one step further for the backcountry: stay home if the forecast carries weather warnings for strong winds, floods or thunderstorms. A storm that arrives despite a clean forecast is the scenario the rest of this guide covers.

The campground shelter audit

Here is the audit the tip lists never build: every structure a camper actually walks past, ranked by what the three authorities say about it. Run this audit on arrival — Ontario Parks explicitly recommends making a lightning safety plan for your group when you arrive at the campground and identifying a safe place to wait out a storm.

Two audit traps deserve emphasis. The first is the dry trap: a picnic shelter keeps a group dry and feels like a building, which is exactly why people choose it — but dry is not the test, grounded and enclosed is. The second is position within the site: Ontario Parks warns against sheltering near the tallest object in the area or near objects that conduct an electrical charge, naming tall trees, posts, fences and other equipment, and the Weather Service's list of tall objects to stay away from adds towers, telephone poles and power lines. A vehicle parked tight against a lone tall tree or a fence line is a worse vehicle than the same vehicle in the open part of the lot.

The 30-minute clock, run as a procedure

Every source states the 30-minute wait; almost none turns it into a procedure with a start trigger, a reset rule and a distance check. Here it is, in order:

  1. On arrival, measure the shelter run. Walk from your site to the shelter your audit picked and time it. The National Park Service's planning point is exactly this: know how long it will take to get to safe shelter and how far away safety is. That number is your departure deadline — thunder does not wait for the walk.
  2. Act on the first thunder, not the first rain. The Weather Service: when thunder roars, you are in danger, so move to shelter. The National Park Service: do not wait — move towards shelter immediately.
  3. En route, keep moving. If the group is caught in the open on the way, the National Park Service says to keep moving towards safe shelter and not stop. Get off elevated ground on the way — the Weather Service lists hills, mountain ridges and peaks as places to leave immediately.
  4. Start the clock at the last thunder, and restart it every time. Stay in shelter for 30 minutes after hearing the last clap of thunder (Weather Service); Ontario Parks phrases the hold as maintaining precautions for 30 minutes after the last flash of lightning or rumble of thunder. Each new rumble is a new “last” — the clock resets, it does not average.
  5. Use flash-to-bang as the distance check while you wait. Ontario Parks: count the seconds between the flash of lightning and the rumble of thunder, and divide by three to get the distance in kilometres. A count that is shrinking means the storm is approaching, whatever the rain is doing.
  6. Do not let easing rain end the wait early. The National Park Service warns that you are still at risk after a storm has passed and not to go back outside just because the rain has slowed or stopped. Rain is weather; thunder is the signal.
  7. Inside a building, finish the job properly. The Weather Service advises not taking a bath or shower during a thunderstorm — plumbing is part of the building systems a strike can reach, which is also why the enclosed building protects in the first place.

Success check: the wait ends only when a full 30 minutes have passed with no thunder and no lightning flash, the flash-to-bang count is stable or growing, and the group returns together rather than drifting back in ones and twos as the rain eases. If any new rumble lands during the walk back, the procedure restarts at step 2.

Pitch-time choices that lower the risk before any storm

Shelter is the main control, but site choice decides how exposed the group is while getting there. The Weather Service's outdoor list is consistent: stay away from isolated trees and other tall objects, never shelter under an isolated tree, and never use a cliff or a rocky overhang as shelter. The National Park Service adds the terrain list — avoid open fields, hills, boulder fields, rocky outcrops and ridge tops, and never lie flat on the ground. Translated to a frontcountry pitch decision: a site in the open that leaves the tent the tallest thing around, a site hard against a single tall tree, and a site on a rise are all worse starting positions than a lower site among a general stand of trees, because they lengthen the exposed walk and raise the group's profile during it. None of this makes a tent safe — it makes the trip to the vehicle or building shorter and lower, which is the part that is actually in your control.

If there is no shelter: frontcountry, backcountry and water are different problems

The decision framework changes with the setting, and blending the three is where most tip lists mislead:

SettingFirst moveFallback if shelter cannot be reached
Frontcountry campgroundHard-topped vehicle or substantial enclosed building, on first thunderKeep moving towards shelter; stay away from tall objects, fences and power lines
Backcountry, no building or vehicleLowest ground available, away from ridges and open high groundWait deep in a thick group of trees; if there are no trees or only solitary trees, the lowest-lying area, crouched with head covered; avoid large-trunk trees when strikes are close and open areas more than 100 m wide
On or in the waterGet to land and find shelter immediatelyStay on land away from the water's edge; water is a conductor

The backcountry row needs its limits stated honestly. Ontario Parks' grouped-trees and crouch guidance is a fallback for when no real shelter exists — it lowers exposure; it does not make a position safe, and the Weather Service's rule that no place outside is 100 percent safe still governs. Two group rules apply in that fallback: the National Park Service advises spreading the group out, so that at most one person is likely to be hurt and the others can apply first aid, and staying within a lower group of trees if in a forest rather than beside the tallest trunks. Ontario Parks also treats communication as part of backcountry storm preparation: carry a satellite messenger or beacon device, because cell service may be limited — and in any emergency, call 911 where service allows.

Finally, the storm is not over when the lightning risk clears. Ontario Parks warns that water levels may be higher after a storm, that creeks and rivers can be subject to flash flooding, and that flooded streams or rivers must never be crossed because of strong undercurrents — if flash flooding occurs, get to higher ground. A site that was safe at pitch time can be cut off by the walk back; check the crossings before assuming the trip resumes as planned.

FAQ

Is a tent with metal poles more dangerous? The authorities do not rank tents by pole material — the National Park Service's position is that tents do not protect you from lightning, full stop. The decision is shelter versus no shelter, not which tent.

Is the campground washroom building a good shelter? Only the substantial, enclosed kind counts: the Weather Service's safe category is a substantial, enclosed structure. A small shed-like outbuilding is in the Weather Service's do-not-use list, and Ontario Parks specifically warns that a vault privy has no method to ground a strike — when in doubt, the hard-topped vehicle is the answer that every source agrees on.

The rain stopped and the sky is brightening. Can the kids go back to the site? Not on the rain's say-so. The rule from both the Weather Service and Ontario Parks is 30 minutes after the last thunder or flash, and the National Park Service explicitly warns against going back outside just because the rain slowed or stopped.

How far away is the storm? Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder and divide by three — that is the distance in kilometres, per Ontario Parks. A falling count means it is coming closer.

What if someone is struck? Call 911 immediately — Ontario Parks' emergency instruction — and use the satellite messenger or beacon carried for the backcountry where there is no cell service. The group's spacing rule exists partly for this moment: unhurt members are the ones able to help.
