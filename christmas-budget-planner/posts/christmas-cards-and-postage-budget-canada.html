Christmas cards and postage: budgeting both together in Canada

Seasonal guide · 9 min read

Christmas cards have a quiet way of blowing a small hole in a holiday budget. The cards themselves get a line in the plan; the postage often does not. Then forty envelopes need stamps, a handful are going to the United States, two are going overseas, and three beautiful thick cards turn out to cost more to mail than the plain ones. None of that is a disaster on its own — but it is exactly the kind of unbudgeted extra that makes December feel more expensive than the plan said it would. This guide fixes that by costing cards and postage as one line, using Canada Post's official current rates, checked on 25 October 2026.

The official rates that decide your total

Canada Post prices a card by three things: where it is going, how much it weighs, and whether it fits the standard size. For a standard-size card — a letter envelope, postcard or standard card, up to 30 grams, which Canada Post describes as up to about five sheets of paper — the current rates are:

Destination (standard size, up to 30 g)Postage per card
Within Canada, stamp from a booklet, coil or pane$1.24
Within Canada, single stamp$1.44
To the United States$1.75
To other international destinations$3.65

Those domestic prices took effect on 13 January 2025, and there was no January 2026 stamp increase — the consumer price guide updated on 23 March 2026 still lists the same Lettermail prices. Canada Post also notes that its prices do not include taxes and are subject to change without notice, so re-check the stamp-prices page in the week you actually mail. One more rule worth knowing: PERMANENT stamps — the ones marked with a letter P instead of a dollar value — are always accepted at the current domestic postage rate, even if rates have increased since the stamp was bought. A booklet left over from last year still covers a standard domestic card this year.

Booklet versus single stamps: the saving at 20, 40 and 60 cards

Buying stamps one at a time is the most expensive way to mail the same cards. A dated price check makes the difference concrete: Canada Post's own store listed the Christmas (2025) booklet of 12 PERMANENT stamps at $14.88 on 25 October 2026 — exactly twelve times the $1.24 booklet rate. Eight dollars on a forty-card run will not rescue a holiday budget by itself, but it is real money found by changing nothing except how the stamps are bought — and it roughly covers the postage on six more domestic cards.

A mixed-list worked example: domestic, U.S. and international

Most real lists are not purely domestic. Here is a worked example — the arithmetic is exact, and the list shape is an example to copy with your own numbers, not a claim about what any household sends. Suppose the list is forty cards, all standard size and under 30 grams: thirty staying in Canada, five going to the United States, and five going to other countries.

GroupCardsRatePostage
Within Canada (booklet stamps)30$1.24$37.20

Total postage40—$64.20

Notice what the mix does: the ten cards leaving Canada are a quarter of the list but $27.00 of the postage — about 42 per cent of it. That is why a single per-card guess fails. The honest budget line is a formula, not a guess: (number of cards × what you actually pay per card) + postage worked out group by group. If your cards cost $1.50 each, for example, forty cards are $60.00, and the full line for the example list above is $60.00 + $64.20 = $124.20, before tax where it applies. Put that whole number in the holiday plan, not just the $60.

The two traps: heavy cards and oversize envelopes

Two features quietly move a card into a higher price. The first is weight. A standard card over 30 grams and up to 50 grams — up to about nine sheets of paper — costs $1.75 within Canada instead of $1.24, $2.61 to the United States instead of $1.75, and $5.21 internationally instead of $3.65. Photo inserts, a folded family letter and a thick envelope can push a card over that 30-gram line without looking any different.

The second trap is size. Standard mail must stay within 245 mm × 156 mm × 5 mm and 50 grams; anything larger, thicker or heavier moves into the non-standard and oversize category. An oversize card up to 100 grams costs $2.61 within Canada — more than double the booklet rate for a standard card. Square, rigid, heavily embellished or very thick cards are the usual suspects. Neither trap is a reason to avoid a card you love; both are reasons to know its real price before forty of them are sealed.

The weigh, measure and budget procedure

Run this once, before the envelopes are stuffed, and the postage line stops being a surprise:

  1. Split the list by destination. Make three piles on paper: within Canada, United States, other international. Count each pile.
  2. Assemble one finished card exactly as it will be mailed — card, insert, photo and envelope together.
  3. Weigh that finished card on a kitchen scale. Under 30 grams is the standard band; 30 to 50 grams uses the higher standard rates above.
  4. Measure it. Check length, width and thickness against the standard limit of 245 mm × 156 mm × 5 mm. If it exceeds any of them, price it as oversize: $2.61 within Canada up to 100 grams.
  5. Do the group math. Multiply each pile's count by its rate from the tables above and add the groups together.
  6. Add the cards themselves. Cards × your real per-card cost, plus the postage total, is the budget line. Write that number into the holiday plan.
  7. Buy booklets, not singles, for the domestic pile, and buy them before the mailing week so a sold-out counter display never forces the $1.44 single-stamp price.
  8. Mail to the delivery standard, not the deadline. Canada Post's Lettermail delivery standards are two business days locally, three within a province and four nationally — standards, not guarantees — so mailing a few days earlier than feels necessary is the cheap insurance.

Trim the list without trimming the thought

If the total still stings, trim with intention rather than guilt. Hand- deliver the local pile — those cards cost no postage at all. Move distant acquaintances who would genuinely be just as glad to hear from you to a thoughtful message or a New Year card mailed in a quieter week, when the gesture stands out more anyway. Keep the mailed list for the people for whom a physical card landing in the box is the point: grandparents, faraway family, the friend who keeps last year's card on the fridge. A shorter list sent warmly beats a long list sent resentfully, and the budget line shrinks in exact proportion to the piles you counted in step one.

What success looks like

Success is checkable: every envelope is stamped at the correct rate for its destination, weight and size; the amount you spent on cards plus postage matches the single line you wrote in the holiday plan, within a couple of dollars; and no card comes back marked insufficient postage because a thick insert quietly crossed 30 grams. If all three are true, the card tradition survived the budget intact — which was the whole point.

Frequently asked questions

Do last year's PERMANENT stamps still work? Yes. Canada Post says PERMANENT stamps are always accepted at the current domestic postage rate, even after a rate increase.

Is a postcard cheaper to mail? Canada Post counts postcards as standard-size mail, so a standard postcard up to 30 grams uses the same rates as a standard card — the saving is on the card itself, not the stamp.

What if a card is just over 30 grams? It moves to the 30-to-50-gram band: $1.75 in Canada, $2.61 to the U.S. and $5.21 to other countries, for standard-size mail.
