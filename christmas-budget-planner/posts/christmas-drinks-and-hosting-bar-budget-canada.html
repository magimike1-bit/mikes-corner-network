A Christmas Drinks and Hosting Bar Budget for Canadian Hosts

Seasonal guide · Published 31 October 2026 · Last updated 31 October 2026

The usual way to stock a Christmas bar is to stand in the liquor store, guess generously, and find out what the evening cost sometime in January. The guess has a second problem, quieter than the money: a bar bought by the anxious bottle run has no quantity logic in it at all — nothing that says how much is enough, who is pouring, or when pouring stops. A bar budget built per drink fixes both at once. The unit is the standard drink, the prices below are checked, and the plan for twelve guests at the end of this page totals $113.65 — including the non-alcoholic line that most bar budgets leave at zero.

The trap: the calculator's number is a ceiling, not a target

Party calculators start from pace. One Canadian retailer's planner works on one drink per guest per hour, plus an extra round for the first hour — guests × (hours + 1) — which turns a four-hour party for twelve into 60 drinks, split by its default of 40 percent wine, 40 percent beer and 20 percent spirits. As a stocking ceiling for a long party, that arithmetic is coherent. As a serving plan, it collides with Canada's own guidance: the Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (CCSA) advises that anyone who drinks should not exceed 2 standard drinks on any day. Sixty drinks available for twelve guests is not sixty drinks served — the gap between those two sentences is exactly where a host's budget and a host's responsibilities meet. Stock for availability, pour to the guidance, and let unopened bottles be the cheapest possible outcome: they keep.

What a drink actually costs

A standard drink, in the CCSA's definitions, is 341 ml of beer at 5 percent alcohol, 142 ml (5 oz) of wine at 12 percent, or 43 ml (1.5 oz) of spirits at 40 percent — each contains the same amount of alcohol. Pour yields come from PEI Liquor's Responsible Host Guide: a 750 ml bottle of wine pours five 5-ounce glasses, and a 750 ml bottle of spirits gives approximately 17 standard drinks. Set those yields against Ontario prices at the LCBO, checked 31 October 2026, and the bar prices itself per drink:

Two lessons sit in that table. First, the expensive-looking bottle is the cheap drink: the vodka at three times the wine's shelf price delivers a standard drink for less, because it holds seventeen of them. Second, bottle choice moves the per-drink cost by dimes, while bottle count moves the total by tens of dollars — which is why the quantity plan, not the brand, is where a bar budget is won. These are Ontario prices; every province sets its own liquor prices, so readers elsewhere should run the same division with their provincial store's shelf price. Beer is costed the same way — one 341 ml bottle is one serving, a case of 24 is 24 servings — priced from your store's case, since no dated case price is quoted here.

The plan for twelve: quantities first, then dollars

The worked example is a labelled scenario, not a recommendation: twelve adult guests at a Christmas dinner party, of whom ten drink alcohol and two do not — substitute your own guest list. The format decision comes first, because it sets the shape of the bill:

For the wine-led plan: six bottles of wine give 30 glasses, and one bottle of spirits gives roughly 17 drinks — availability of about 47 alcoholic drinks for ten drinking guests, poured measured and one at a time under the hosting rules in the next section but one. At the checked prices, six bottles of the $9.95 red cost $59.70 and the vodka $29.95: an alcohol subtotal of $89.65, or $7.47 per guest across all twelve. A toast, if the evening wants one, comes out of the wine count — the retailer's rule of one sparkling bottle per six guests describes quantity, and folding it into the six bottles keeps the total honest.

The line everyone leaves at $0

PEI Liquor's host guide tells hosts to plan on providing plenty of water and non-alcoholic beverages, and the retailer's planner lists ice, mixers and zero-proof options for drivers and non-drinkers. Both treat the line as essential. Almost no budget gives it a number. Give it one the same way the food budget gets one: as a named share, bought on the grocery trip, not as whatever change is left at the liquor store. In the worked total below it is a labelled assumption of $24 — soda and tonic (the spirits bottle alone calls for roughly three bottles of mix, per the host guide), sparkling water, a zero-proof option for the two non-drinking guests, and ice. Responsible hosting is also a budget rule

The hosting practices in PEI Liquor's Responsible Host Guide and the CCSA guidance read, on inspection, like a quantity-control system — which is exactly what a budget needs:

  • One measured server. The host guide advises measuring and serving drinks yourself, or appointing a trustworthy bartender, rather than letting guests pour their own — and using a measure, since guessing leads to excessive consumption. Measured pours are also the per-drink math above, actually happening.
  • One drink at a time, paced. CCSA's tips: drink slowly, eat before and while drinking, and have one non-alcoholic drink for every alcoholic one. Food is part of the bar plan, not a separate event — the host guide's holiday tips say to serve plenty of food alongside.
  • A stop time. The same holiday hosting tips say to stop serving alcoholic beverages at least one hour before the party ends, and switch to non-alcoholic drinks — a rule that caps both the evening's risk and its bottle count.
  • No drinking and driving, ever. The host guide warns that a host may be held legally responsible for the actions of guests, and is blunt: do not serve guests to the point of intoxication, and never let them drink and drive. Plan rides — designated drivers, transit, taxis — as part of the evening, and keep the non-alcoholic line generous enough that the drivers' glasses are never empty either.
  • Adults only. Never serve alcohol to anyone under the legal drinking age in your province.

None of this is a scolding appended to a shopping list. It is the mechanism that makes the $113.65 a ceiling instead of a floor: a measured server, a pace, and a stop time are what stand between the availability you stocked and the consumption you budgeted for.

When the bar is not worth stocking

Skip the full stock-up when the guest list is small and known: four adults who each drink a glass or two need two bottles, not a format. Skip the second and third spirits in almost every case — one spirit at roughly seventeen drinks covers the cocktail demand of a twelve-person dinner, and the unopened extras are money resting on a shelf. And if the budget is tight, cut formats before cutting the non-alcoholic line or the food: a wine-only bar with a generous zero-proof selection is a complete evening, while a full bar with nothing for the drivers is not. The decision, like the total, belongs in October — written down, priced per drink, and finished before the store lights do the deciding.
