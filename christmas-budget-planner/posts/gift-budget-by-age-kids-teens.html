A gift budget by age for kids and teens

Seasonal guide · 10 min read

Search "how much should I spend on each kid" and the internet answers with tables: so much for ages 0–3, so much more for teens, spend with confidence. The tables are not lying, exactly — they are just answering a different question. They describe what gifts cost. They say nothing about what your household can spend, across all the children on your list, in a season that also includes food, travel and everything else. A per-child amount pulled from a stranger's table can be unaffordable with four kids and needlessly stingy with one. This guide builds the number the other way around: from your own holiday total, divided honestly, then adjusted by age inside written bands — with a fairness framework for siblings and an all-in check so the amount you planned is the amount you actually spend.

One published rule of thumb is worth naming, to show how it should and should not be used. A Canadian gift-spending guide suggests $75 to $150 per child works for most middle-income Canadian families, with roughly $50 per younger child and $200 to $300 for teens in some families. Treat numbers like those as a sanity check at the very end — "is my derived number in a normal-looking neighbourhood?" — never as the starting point. The starting point is your own arithmetic.

Step one: the per-child base comes from your own numbers

The derivation has three moves, and none of them is optional:

  1. Set the holiday total first. One number for the whole season — gifts, food, travel, wrapping, everything. If no total exists, build one before any per-child math; the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada's free Budget Planner builds a customized budget in three steps and can be downloaded as a spreadsheet, and a plain list on paper does the same job.
  2. Carve out the gifts envelope. Decide what share of the total is gifts for children — your own children, plus nieces, nephews and any other kids you buy for. Write the envelope down as a dollar figure, not a percentage you will "roughly" honour.
  3. Divide by the number of children. That quotient is the per-child base. It is the same for every child at this stage, on purpose: fairness starts from equality, and age adjustments come next, in the open, with limits.

Example, with deliberately round numbers: a $900 holiday total, a $360 gifts envelope for children, and three children on the list gives a base of $120 per child. A household with the same $360 envelope and six children gets a base of $60 — which is exactly the information the age tables hide. Neither number is right or wrong in the abstract; each is simply what that family's season can carry, stated early enough to plan around.

Step two: adjust by age, inside written bands

Age adjustments are real. A toddler's delight costs little and a teenager's world — hobbies, technology, clothing that fits a growth spurt — genuinely costs more. The mistake is letting the adjustment happen gift by gift, in stores, until the youngest child's total is a third of the oldest's and nobody decided that. Instead, set bands around the base before shopping: a written multiplier per age group, applied once.

Age groupSuggested band around the baseBase $120 becomesWhy
0–2 years0.4× – 0.6×$48 – $72Needs are small, joy is cheap, and relatives reliably add gifts at this age
3–5 years0.7× – 0.9×$84 – $108Play is inexpensive; one good toy outlasts five small ones
6–9 years0.9× – 1.1×$108 – $132The reference group — sets the base in most households

These bands are this guide's framework — planning multipliers, not research findings — and they come with a non-negotiable rule: after adjusting, re-add the column. If three children's adjusted amounts total more than the envelope, the bands come down together, proportionally, until the total fits. The envelope is the decision; the bands are only how it gets shared. Adjusting one child's band upward without re-checking the total is how a $360 envelope becomes $430 of spending with every single purchase "within budget."

The fairness framework: equal dollars, equal piles, or one pooled gift

Siblings do not compare receipts; they compare experiences. Three fairness systems cover almost every family, and each is the right answer somewhere:

SystemHow it worksBest forWatch out for
Equal dollarsEvery child gets the same total spendOlder kids and teens, who can see and value amounts; households giving money or gift cardsYounger children experience one big item as "more" than three small ones of equal cost
Equal pilesEvery child gets a similar number of gifts to openChildren under about eight, for whom the unwrapping itself is the eventEqual piles at unequal prices need filler gifts — cap the filler at small, genuinely wanted items
Pooled giftTwo or more children's amounts combine into one shared, larger giftA big-ticket item the kids will genuinely share — a game system, a trampoline, a family experiencePooling must be agreed before the season, and each pooled child still gets one small personal gift to open

The decision rule is simple: match the system to what the children will actually compare. Under-eights compare piles. Teenagers compare value. Mixed-age households often run equal piles for the young ones and equal dollars for the teens, with the age bands above absorbing the difference. What does not work is switching systems mid-season because one child's list turned out expensive — that is not a fairness decision, it is the budget dissolving. Pick the system in October, tell any other gift-buying adults (grandparents especially) which one is in force, and let it hold.

The all-in check before anything is bought

A per-child ceiling only works if it is compared against the true cost of each gift: shelf price, plus sales tax at your province's rate, plus shipping if no free-shipping threshold is met, plus wrapping. A $130 item can quietly become a $150 spend — over a $144 band ceiling — without a single dishonest step. Run the check on paper for any gift within $20 of its ceiling: if the all-in total breaks the band, the options are a smaller item, a pooled contribution, or an explicit, written transfer from another child's unused band room — never a shrug.

One more check belongs here, because wishlist prices are marketing too. The Competition Bureau explains that a claimed regular price is only valid if it passes a volume test — more than half of sales at that price or higher within a reasonable period — or a time test, meaning the item was genuinely offered at that price for a substantial period; a "was" price that fails both can violate the Competition Act. When a wishlist item shows a dramatic markdown, a two-minute look at its price history is worth more than the percentage on the banner.

Worked example: three kids, one envelope

Put the whole method together. Holiday total $900; gifts envelope for children $360; three children aged 4, 9 and 15. Base: $360 ÷ 3 = $120. Bands: the 4-year-old at 0.8× is $96, the 9-year-old at 1.0× is $120, the 15-year-old at 1.2× is $144. Re-added: $96 + $120 + $144 = $360 — the envelope holds exactly. Fairness system: equal piles for the 4-year-old (four small gifts within $96), equal dollars for the teenager ($144, stated openly, part of it as money toward something being saved for), the 9-year-old in between with $120 across three gifts. All-in check on the teenager's main item: a $125 item plus tax and no shipping lands near the ceiling but inside it; if it had crossed, the fix would have been $15 moved — in writing — from the 4-year-old's unspent band room, keeping the envelope at $360.

When a wishlist item breaks the band

Sooner or later a child asks for the thing that costs double their band. Three honest routes exist. Pool it: the item becomes the pooled gift, with the other children's agreement where they are old enough to give it. Split it: grandparents or another adult contribute a stated amount toward it, by arrangement, not by hint. Bridge it: part Christmas, part the child's own savings or a birthday contribution — a route teenagers often prefer, because it treats them as partners rather than recipients. The route that is not on the list is quietly breaking the envelope and repairing the story later. Children learn what budgets are by watching one hold.

FAQ

Should each child get exactly the same amount? The base starts equal for every child; age bands then adjust it within written limits, and the adjusted total must still equal the envelope. Equality of total spend across a toddler and a teenager is less fair in practice than a modest, explained band difference.

Do nieces, nephews and friends' kids go in the same envelope? Yes — every child you buy for divides the same envelope. A common structure is full bands for your own children and a smaller fixed amount, set in October, for the wider circle.

Is cash for a teenager a cop-out? No. For the 13–17 band, money toward a named goal respects both the budget and the teenager. Count it at face value inside the band, and pair it with one small personal item to open.

This is general information about personal budgeting, not financial advice. Everyone's situation is different. The age bands and worked examples in this guide are planning frameworks with round numbers, not researched spending averages — your envelope, your bands and your total are the ones that matter.
