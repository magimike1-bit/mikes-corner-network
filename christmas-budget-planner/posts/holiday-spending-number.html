Setting a holiday number you can stick to

Step 1 · 7 min read

Every holiday plan starts with one decision: a single number. Not a wish, not a hope — one total amount you're genuinely comfortable spending this season. Everything else in the plan flows from it. And here's the important part: there is no "right" number. A hundred, a thousand, ten thousand — the only number that matters is the one that fits your life without hurting your January.

Start with last year, honestly

Before picking a new number, take five minutes to estimate what you actually spent last season. Check card statements, scan your banking app, or just add up what you remember: gifts, groceries for holiday meals, travel, wrapping, cards, decorations, those "small" treats that added up. Most people are surprised — and that's useful information, not a reason to feel bad. You can't steer a budget you haven't seen.

If you don't have records, guess and round up. An honest high guess beats a wishful low one.

Count the whole holiday, not just gifts

The number one reason holiday budgets fall apart is that they only cover gifts. But December spending is much bigger than presents. Make a quick list of every category that applies to you:

  • Gifts (everyone on your list)
  • Holiday food and drinks — meals, baking, hosting supplies
  • Travel — gas, flights, hotels, parking
  • Decorations, wrapping paper, cards, postage
  • Work parties, kid parties, school or club gifts
  • Charitable giving you plan
  • Events and entertainment — movies, shows, outings
  • A small buffer for surprises (they always happen)

Your holiday number is the total across all of these, not per category. When people blow past their budget, it's usually because four of these categories were never in it.

Pick the number from what you have

Now the gentle but honest part. Look at what you can actually afford — money you can spend without borrowing, without skipping bills, without touching savings meant for something else. Work backwards:

  1. What savings have you set aside for the holidays (if any)?
  2. What can you set aside between now and December from regular pay?
  3. Add those two. That's your honest ceiling.

If that ceiling is smaller than last year's spending, that is completely fine. It just means this year's plan needs a shape that fits — fewer categories, smaller gifts, homemade food, or a simpler celebration. A simpler season you enjoy beats an expensive one you spend three months regretting.

Split the number across people and categories

Take your total and divide it. Give each person on your list a rough amount, and give each category (food, travel, extras) its own line. It doesn't have to be exact — ranges work fine ("about $40–50 for Maya"). The point is that the pieces add up to the total, so there's no phantom money.

One useful trick: assign your closest people their amounts first, then divide what's left among the rest. It forces the real priorities to the front.

Write it down somewhere you'll see it

A number that lives only in your head will quietly grow every time something cute catches your eye in a store. Write it on a note in your phone, a piece of paper in your wallet, or a simple spreadsheet. When you're tempted, checking the number takes ten seconds — and ten seconds is often enough for the impulse to pass.

What if you can't afford what you want?

Sometimes the honest number is small, and that can sting — especially if you're used to giving big. A few reminders that tend to help:

  • People remember attention, not price tags. Thoughtful almost always beats expensive.
  • Homemade and secondhand gifts have dignity. A loaf of banana bread or a carefully chosen thrifted find is a real gift.
  • Kids adapt fast. They take their cues from you — calm and happy beats stressed and overextended.
  • It's one year. Next year can look different. This year's job is just to enjoy the season without hurting yourself.

Revisit, don't regret

Check your number once a week through December. If you're under in one category, you can move that money to another — that's not failure, that's budgeting working. If you're heading over, catch it early: that weekly glance is the whole reason this method works.

Your holiday number isn't a cage. It's a map. And once it's set, every other step — saving ahead, shopping smart, staying out of debt — gets much easier.
