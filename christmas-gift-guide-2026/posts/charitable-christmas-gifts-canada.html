Charitable Christmas Gifts in Canada: Donations in Someone's Name, Done Well

Gift ideas · Charitable giving · Canada · ~7 min read

Yes, you can give a donation in someone else's name for Christmas — and no, the tax receipt cannot go in their name. That second part is the trap in this gift, and it catches people every December. The receipt must be issued to the true donor: the person who actually made the gift. Sort that expectation out before anyone opens an e-card, and the rest of this gift is straightforward, generous, and genuinely simple to do well.

The trap first: whose name is on the receipt?

CanadaHelps states the rule plainly: you can dedicate a donation in honour or in memory of someone, but the tax receipt must be issued in the name of the true donor, following the Canada Revenue Agency guideline. The CRA's own guidance agrees — a charity cannot issue a receipt in a name other than the name of the true donor.

Read that as a household conversation, not fine print. If you donate in a parent's name expecting them to claim the credit, they cannot. If they donate in yours, you cannot. The honour belongs to the person named in the dedication. The receipt belongs to the person who paid.

Neither fact makes the other smaller. Trouble only starts when the two get mixed up — usually in January, when receipts are gathered and someone asks a question nobody raised in December.

So say it out loud when you give: the donation is in their honour, and the receipt comes to you. Most recipients who wanted this gift will not care. The ones who would have cared deserved to hear it first.

When this gift delights, and when it does not

Engage and Change, a charity that accepts donations in someone else's name, puts the suitability test in one line: a donation gift suits someone who asked for no gifts, or who cares about the cause. If they would rather have a present, get them the present.

That test holds up well under pressure. Apply it honestly, recipient by recipient:

What you know about the recipientCallWhy
They asked for no giftsDonateThe gift respects the request instead of working around it
They care about a specific causeDonate, to that causeThe fit is the thought — the cause shows you were listening
They were hoping for a particular presentBuy the presentA donation answers a wish they never made
You are unsure, and the occasion matters to themAsk, or choose a presentGuessing wrong turns generosity into a statement about you

One more check sits underneath the table. Pick the cause by the recipient, not by you. A donation to your favourite charity, in their name, is a gift to your own priorities with their name attached. A donation to the cause they talk about, volunteer for, or quietly support is the opposite: evidence that their priorities set the direction.

What a set amount actually does: one priced example

Abstract generosity is hard to picture, so anchor on one concrete example documented by the charity itself. At Engage and Change, $50 helps build a winter survival kit with a sleeping bag and a backpack for someone sleeping outside in the GTA.

That sentence does useful work in a card. It tells the recipient what left the account and what it became — a kit, with named contents, for a named situation. When a charity describes a set amount this specifically, use its wording. When it does not, do not invent an equivalent. Give the amount, name the charity, and leave the impact in the charity's own terms.

The mechanics: dedication, e-card, and timing

A donation becomes a gift in the presentation layer, and the platforms document exactly how that layer works. On CanadaHelps, before completing the donation you can add a dedication, leave a message for the charity, and send a free e-card to the person of your choice.

Timing is the detail gift guides miss. At Engage and Change, the e-card goes out when the donation is complete — so give on the day you want the card to arrive. A donation completed on December 18 is not a Christmas-morning surprise held in reserve. It is a card that landed on December 18.

Done as a procedure, the gift runs like this:

  1. Choose the cause by the recipient. Run the suitability table first. If the answer is a present, stop here and buy the present.
  2. Verify before you pay. Confirm the organisation is a registered charity, and confirm you understand the receipt rule: a registered charity may only issue official receipts for donations that legally qualify as gifts, and the receipt will name the true donor — you.
  3. Complete the donation on the day the card should arrive. Add the dedication in honour or in memory, in the recipient's name, before completing the donation.
  4. Send the free e-card as part of the same completion. Write the message so it names the cause and, where the charity states one, what the amount does.
  5. Keep your own receipt. It arrives in your name because you are the true donor. File it with your records, not with the gift.

Success looks like this: the right person gets a card on the right day, naming a cause they care about, with no January surprise about a receipt that was never going to carry their name.

In memory, handled gently

The same dedication mechanics support donations in memory of someone, and Christmas is when that version surfaces most — a gift in memory of a parent, given to a sibling, or in memory of someone the recipient lost this year. The mechanics do not change. The judgement does.

A memorial donation is a tender gift, not a default one. Give it where the loss is shared and the cause connects to the person remembered. Where you are unsure how it will land, the suitability test still governs: a present that asks nothing emotional of the recipient is the safer generosity.

A short pre-send checklist

  • Cause chosen by the recipient's interests, not the giver's.
  • Registered-charity status confirmed, and the true-donor receipt rule understood by the giver.
  • Dedication wording added before the donation is completed.
  • Donation timed for the day the e-card should arrive, because the card sends on completion.
  • No promise made to anyone about a tax credit in their name.
