Gifts for the Hard-to-Shop-For

Gift ideas · Strategy · ~7 min read

Every family has one: the person who answers "what do you want?" with "nothing," buys everything they want the moment they want it, or has taste so specific that guessing feels like defusing a bomb. Hard-to-shop-for people aren't ungrateful — they're just immune to generic gifts. The fix isn't a better product list. It's a better strategy, matched to the personality type.

Type 1: The Minimalist ("I don't need anything")

The minimalist genuinely means it: objects are a burden to them. Buying them a decorative object is an act of hostility disguised as generosity. What works:

  • Consumables they actually use — their favorite coffee, good olive oil, quality candles. Things that disappear through use are the minimalist's love language.
  • Experiences — a dinner out together, tickets, a class. (See our experience gifts guide.)
  • Upgrades to something they already own — the nicer version of a daily-use item that's wearing out. This respects their "no new categories of stuff" rule.
  • A donation in their name — only for the true believer, and only to a cause they'd pick themselves. When in doubt, ask.

Type 2: The Hobbyist (knows more than you about their hobby)

The trap with hobbyists is buying hobby gear: you'll pick the wrong thing, the cheap version, or something they already own. Instead, gift around the hobby:

  • Consumables for the hobby — quality ingredients for the baker, good yarn for the knitter, fresh strings for the guitarist. Even experts run out of supplies.
  • Hobby-adjacent comfort — the great chair for the reader, the insulated mug for the early-morning runner, the apron for the cook.
  • Learning — a masterclass, a workshop, a beautiful reference book. Experts love deepening.
  • Ask their enabler. Every hobbyist has a friend or partner who knows their wishlist. One text message — "what would they actually want?" — beats ten hours of research.

Type 3: The Has-Everything (buys it the day it comes out)

You cannot out-shop this person. Don't try. Your advantages are thoughtfulness, rarity, and personalization — things money can't shortcut:

  • The vintage or rare version — the first edition, the discontinued flavor, the import they can't easily get. (Thriftmas skills apply — see our Thriftmas guide.)
  • Personalized and custom — engraved, monogrammed, commissioned. A custom illustration of their pet beats any gadget.
  • Access — the hard-to-get reservation, the backstage tour, the lesson with the local expert. Things that aren't for sale to everyone.
  • Your time and skill — the home-cooked feast, the built bookshelf, the photo book you actually finished. For someone who can buy anything, handmade is the only scarce resource.

Type 4: The "Don't Get Me Anything" (means it, mostly)

This person is usually saying one of two things: "I hate the obligation cycle" or "I hate waste." Respect the spirit while still showing up:

  • Keep it small and consumable. A great bottle of something, excellent chocolate, a small plant. Low obligation, high pleasure.
  • Make it about togetherness. "I'm cooking Christmas dinner" or "movie night, my pick, snacks included" is a gift that creates zero clutter and zero guilt.
  • Write the letter. A genuine, specific letter about what they mean to you routinely outranks every physical gift in post-Christmas surveys of remembered joy — and it costs a stamp.
The nuclear option: propose a gift-free Christmas for the adults, with gifts only for kids. Many "don't get me anything" people light up at this suggestion. The best gift for some people is permission to skip the whole exchange.

Type 5: The Picky (strong taste, stronger opinions)

The picky person isn't hard to shop for — they're easy to shop for badly. The strategy is reconnaissance:

  • Keep a year-round notes file. When they mention liking something in March, write it down. Christmas shopping becomes retrieval, not guessing.
  • Gift cards are not surrender — a gift card to their specific favorite niche store ("I know you love that place") reads as attentive, not lazy. Generic cards read as lazy.
  • Ask directly. Picky people usually love being asked — it means you'll get it right. "Send me three things you'd be happy with" is a perfectly good text to send.

The universal fallback

When all else fails, remember the three gifts that work on nearly every personality type: something consumable and excellent, something that's an experience you share, and something handmade or personal. None of them require knowing their size, their specs, or their wishlist. All of them say you were paying attention — which is the actual gift.
