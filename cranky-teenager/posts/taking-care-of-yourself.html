Taking care of yourself as the parent

For you · 5 min read

Nobody asks how you're doing. Everyone asks about the teenager — the grades, the attitude, the latest drama. But you're the one absorbing the eye rolls, de-escalating the blowups, lying awake wondering if you handled it right. Parenting a cranky teen is genuinely exhausting, and pretending it isn't doesn't make you stronger. It just makes you tired.

This one is for you. Not for your teen. For you.

You can't pour from an empty cup

You've heard the saying, and it's true in a very practical way. A depleted parent is a reactive parent: shorter fuse, less patience, more yelling, more regret. A rested parent is the calm, steady presence their teenager actually needs. Taking care of yourself isn't selfish, and it isn't a luxury. It's a parenting strategy — maybe the most effective one on this whole site.

Think of it like the oxygen mask on an airplane: you're no good to your kid if you're gasping for air yourself.

The basics are not optional

When parenting gets hard, self-care is usually the first thing to go. But the basics matter more now, not less:

  • Sleep. Your teen isn't the only one running on empty. Chronic tiredness makes everything feel worse — the attitude, the worry, the hopelessness. Protecting your sleep is protecting your parenting.
  • Food and movement. Not a fitness program — just the basics. Eat real meals. Take a walk. Your nervous system regulates better when your body isn't running on caffeine and adrenaline.
  • Downtime that is actually yours. Ten minutes with coffee before anyone wakes up. A show you watch alone. A drive with the radio on. Small, regular pockets of your-own-time add up more than a rare big break.

Keep your friendships alive

One of the loneliest parts of parenting a difficult teen is feeling like no one else gets it. Other parents post the highlight reel; you're living the blooper reel. That isolation makes everything harder.

You don't need a big social life. You need one or two people you can be honest with — someone you can text "today was brutal" without needing to explain or perform. A friend who has teenagers of their own is gold: they've seen the same movie. If you don't have that person, a parenting group, online or in person, can fill the gap. The point is simple: don't parent through the hard years alone.

Let go of the perfect-parent fantasy

A lot of parental exhaustion comes from guilt: I should have handled that better. A good parent would know what to do. Other families don't have this problem. (They do, by the way. They're just not posting about it.)

Here's the truth: there is no perfect way to parent a teenager, because teenagers are moving targets. You'll yell sometimes. You'll pick the wrong battle. You'll say the wrong thing. That's not failure — that's parenting a real human through a genuinely hard stage. What matters is the overall pattern: showing up, repairing when you mess up, and loving them through it.

Give yourself the same grace you'd give a friend. If your best friend told you they'd lost their temper with their teen, you'd say "of course you did, that sounds brutal" — not "wow, what a terrible parent." Turn that voice inward.

Keep something that's just yours

When your whole identity becomes "parent of a difficult teenager," every slammed door feels like a personal verdict. Having something in your life that has nothing to do with your kids — a hobby, a project, a friendship, a workout, a garden, anything — gives you an identity that doesn't rise and fall with your teen's mood.

This isn't about escaping your family. It's about being a whole person who parents, rather than a parent with nothing left. Whole people are calmer, funnier, more patient, and more interesting to be around — including to their teenagers.

Know when you need backup

There's a difference between the normal exhaustion of parenting a teen and feeling like you're genuinely not okay. If you're constantly overwhelmed, dreading every day, crying regularly, or feeling hopeless about your family — that's not just tiredness. That's a sign to talk to someone: a friend, a counselor, your doctor. Getting support for yourself models exactly what you'd want your teen to do when they're struggling.

A note to end on

Parenting a cranky teenager is one of the hardest jobs there is, and it's mostly thankless — at least in the moment. Your teen probably won't thank you. Not now. But the steady, imperfect, showing-up-anyway love you're giving them is shaping who they'll become, even when it looks like nothing is getting through.

Take care of the person doing that job. That's you.
