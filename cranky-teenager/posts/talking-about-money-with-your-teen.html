Talking about money with a teen who thinks you're being unfair

Money & fairness · 8 min read

“Unfair” sounds like an accusation about your character. It usually is not. Underneath most teen fairness complaints sits a simpler problem: your teen can see one number — the thing they want, not funded — and cannot see any of the numbers around it. They are comparing their request against a friend's funded request, or against a sibling's funded request, with no view of the total either request came out of. That is not a values problem and it will not be fixed by a speech about gratitude. It is a missing-information problem, and missing information has a practical fix: turn the argument into a budgeting conversation, with categories on the table and a rule agreed before the next request arrives.

Show the categories, not the whole ledger

The middle path between “none of your business” and handing over the full household accounts is categories. A teen does not need the mortgage figure to understand the shape of a family budget; they need to see that money arrives in a finite total and leaves in named buckets, and that their bucket is one real bucket among several — not the residue after everyone else's, and not an unlimited line that a mean parent keeps switching off.

Keep the tone of this conversation deliberately flat. Guidance on teens and money is consistent on the delivery: discuss finances matter-of-factly, without getting emotional, and do not use money as control, punishment, or reward. A categories conversation held in a level voice teaches budgeting. The same conversation held as a rebuttal — receipts produced mid-argument to win — teaches only that money talks happen when someone is in trouble.

Let them itemise their own needs

Here is the move that changes the dynamic. Instead of ruling on requests one at a time as they arrive — the system that guarantees every no feels personal — ask the teen to itemise what they actually need: each item, its purpose, and its cost, written down. Then review that list together against the overall family budget. The request stops being a verdict on your generosity and becomes an entry in a list that has to fit inside a total, exactly like every other entry in the household.

Some families run a version of this as the standing system rather than an emergency measure. The teen receives a regular allowance and buys what they need themselves — personal items included — rather than each need becoming a separate purchase request to a parent. When a genuine need comes up between allowances, the teen flags it and the amount is added to the next allowance. The mechanics matter less than the effect: the teen is practising the real skill, planning their own money against their own needs, inside a system where the rules are known in advance.

Inside that system, two small tools do a surprising amount of work. The first is the spend, save, give split — a simple framework for dividing allowance into money for now, money put aside, and money for others. A suggested starting point for the saving slice is 10 per cent a month: small enough to actually happen, large enough to become a visible pile. The second tool is the quality-versus-disposable comparison, and it teaches itself once a teen is spending their own money: three $10 sweaters that wash badly, against one $30 sweater that lasts. Put as a lecture, it is a lecture. Discovered at the till with their own allowance, it is an education.

Agree the rule before the next ask

Most money fights are really rule fights: the request arrives, no rule exists, so the answer has to be invented on the spot — and an invented answer always looks like favouritism or mood. Agree the working rules while nobody wants anything:

  1. Base the allowance on your budget, not on other families. The amount should follow the family budget and which expenses the teen is expected to cover from it — not what other parents give, a figure your teen will always be able to source selectively.
  2. Name what the allowance covers. Needs the teen buys themselves, wants, or a stated mix — the fairness arguments breed in the gap where coverage was never specified.
  3. Decide the overspend rule now. If a teen spends all their money, the agreed backstop is narrow: advance only what can be earned under the arrangement you have agreed. An unlimited rescue teaches that the budget is theatre; no rescue at all, sprung as a surprise, teaches that the system is a trap. Agreed in advance, the middle rule is simply the rule.
  4. Set the review date. Amounts that made sense at one age stop making sense at another. A scheduled review gives “it's not enough” a legitimate place to go that is not tonight's argument.

What success looks like: the next request gets answered by pointing at the list and the rule — “where does it sit in your itemised needs?” — instead of by a fresh judgment about the teen, the item, or you.

When siblings get different amounts

This is where fairness complaints get their sharpest edge, and where “same” and “fair” quietly part company. Different ages legitimately need different amounts: the expenses a teen covers grow as they do, so an allowance pegged to the budget and to what each teen actually covers will produce different numbers for different siblings — correctly. The rule that keeps this defensible is consistency of expectations: the same system, the same logic, the same review rhythm for each teen, even though the figures differ. A younger sibling can be shown the ladder they will climb; an older one can be shown that their higher amount arrived with higher expected coverage. Fair, in a family budget, means the same rule applied to different situations — and saying that sentence out loud, early, is worth more than any single funding decision.
