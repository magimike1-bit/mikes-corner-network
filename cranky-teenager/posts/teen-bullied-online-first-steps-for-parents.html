When your teen is bullied online: first steps for parents

Safety & support · 9 min read

The phone goes face down at dinner. It happens again the next night, and the night after that, and somewhere in the same week your teen starts finding reasons to skip the activity they used to leave early for. Public Safety Canada lists the pattern plainly among the warning signs of cyberbullying: a teen who suddenly avoids their devices or spends far more time on them, grows secretive about online activity, avoids school and social situations, falls behind in schoolwork, or seems sad, frustrated or withdrawn after messages, with trouble sleeping or less interest in eating at the heavier end. You may be looking at exactly that pattern, or your teen may have simply told you. Either way, the first 48 hours have an order to them, and the order matters more than speed.

The first conversation: listen before you fix

Everything else on this page works better if this part goes well. The Government of Canada guidance for parents starts here: a child is more likely to open up and accept help if you make it clear you are on their side and know what to do. Teens may be reluctant to talk because they feel uncomfortable, ashamed or afraid, and many are quietly certain the phone will be confiscated as the first step, which is one reason problems surface late.

So the first conversation has three jobs. Listen calmly and let the story arrive whole. Reassure them that they are not alone, that they are going to be okay, and that you are there to help. And learn the extent of what has happened, because it may be a small incident that has already been handled or something considerably more serious, and the right response differs. Two things to keep out of this conversation: blame, including blame for how they handled it before telling you, and criticism for not coming to you earlier. Canada's guidance is explicit that no child deserves to be bullied, even if something they said or did preceded it, and that criticising a teen for coming late makes the next disclosure less likely, not more.

The first 48 hours, in order

  1. Settle the immediate question first: is your teen safe and okay tonight? Ask directly how they are doing, and read the heavier warning signs seriously. If your teen is showing signs of depression, isolation or anxiety, loss of interest in eating or sleeping, or any signs of self-harm, Canada.ca advises not hesitating to seek support from a mental health professional. The support box at the end of this article lists services your teen can reach directly, tonight.
  2. Agree together that nothing gets answered. Your teen should not respond to or forward any of the bullying messages, and should not retaliate. The reason is practical, not just principled: bullies crave attention, and replying gives them more power. Kids Help Phone's sequence for young people starts the same way: stop, do not be mean back, take a breath.
  3. Preserve the evidence before anything gets blocked or deleted. This is the step families most often do in the wrong order. Details in the next section.
  4. Then block. Once the evidence is saved, have your teen block the person on social media and as a contact for email, text and phone. Canada.ca calls blocking one of the most effective early ways to stop cyberbullying, and most platforms provide ways to block users from seeing a page or sending messages. If the contact keeps finding new routes, changing an email address or phone number is also on the table.
  5. Report through the right channels, in the right order. Platform first for the content itself, school next if classmates are involved, police immediately if the behaviour crosses the lines set out below. The following section helps you sort which door is which.
  6. Write down what was reported, to whom, and when. A short dated log, kept with the evidence, turns a stressful blur into a record you can hand to a school or a platform if the behaviour continues.

What success looks like: by the end of the second day, your teen has told the story once, fully, to an adult who believed them; the messages are saved, dated and stored outside the phone; the accounts are blocked; at least one report has been filed through the right channel; and your teen knows two support services they can contact without going through you.

Preserve the evidence before anything gets deleted

Evidence is what turns "someone is being awful to my kid" into something a platform, a school or a police officer can act on, and it is fragile. Posts get edited, accounts get renamed, group chats scroll on, and a teen's first instinct is often to delete the whole thread so they never have to see it again. That instinct deserves sympathy and a gentle redirect: capture first, delete after the reports are filed, if at all.

Concretely: save and take screen captures of the offending posts, messages, images and videos; record the dates and times of all incidents; note the usernames and the platform each item appeared on; and save and print the screenshots, or store copies somewhere other than the teen's phone, so the record survives a lost or replaced device. Kids Help Phone's guidance for young people puts the same point in four words: stop, save, tell, block. Save sits before tell and block for a reason.

Which door: platform, school, or police

Not every incident needs every door, and choosing well keeps the response proportionate. Use this sort:

What is happeningFirst doorWhy
Cruel posts, messages or a fake account targeting your teen on an app or siteThe platform or service providerSites and providers have policies and reporting channels for abusive content and can warn, suspend or remove accounts
The people involved are classmates, or it is spilling into the school dayThe school: teacher, vice-principal or principalCyberbullying often happens between classmates, and face-to-face bullying can be happening at the same time; ask what steps the school and board take
Threats of physical harm or violenceLocal policeNamed by Canada.ca as a police matter; do not hesitate
Sexual or intimate images of someone under 18 being sent or sharedLocal police, plus NeedHelpNow.ca for removal helpNamed by Canada.ca as a police matter; the Canadian Centre for Child Protection helps remove sexual images and videos at NeedHelpNow.ca
Stalking or persistent harassing contact that has your teen fearing for their safety, or someone using another person's identity or accountsLocal policeBoth are named by Canada.ca as behaviours to report to law enforcement

One framing from PREVNet, Canada's bullying research network, is worth holding onto while you make these calls: bullying is a relationship problem that requires relationship solutions, and adults should be constructive and avoid using their power aggressively, because modelling bullying makes it harder for children to develop healthy relationships. That is the academic version of a practical rule. You are escalating to systems whose job is to stop the behaviour and repair the situation, not running a counterattack of your own.

What not to do in the first week

Canada.ca lists the traps directly, and they are worth naming because most come from love. Do not ignore it and hope it goes away; recognising and responding appropriately is the job. Do not tell your teen to retaliate, which hands the bully attention and teaches that hurtful behaviour is an acceptable reply. And do not contact the other child's parent yourself unless you already know them well; the guidance is to let schools or other authorities help everyone reach a solution that works for all the children involved. Add one more from hard experience across many families: do not take the phone away as your opening move. The phone is where the evidence lives, where the supportive friends are, and where your teen's sense of control sits. Blocking, reporting and support protect your teen. Confiscation mostly protects the bully's privacy.

Support your teen can reach directly

Part of helping is making sure your teen has doors that are not you, because some nights a teen will talk to a trained stranger more easily than to a parent in the next room. These details are taken from the services' own listings as published by Kids Help Phone, Public Safety Canada and CAMH:

  • Kids Help Phone (Canada): free, 24/7, confidential counselling, information and support for young people. Call 1-800-668-6868, text 686868, or use live chat at KidsHelpPhone.ca.
  • 9-8-8 Suicide Crisis Helpline (Canada): call or text 9-8-8, any time, for trained responders offering support to anyone thinking of suicide or worried about someone they know, in English and French. More at 988.ca.
  • NeedHelpNow.ca: help from the Canadian Centre for Child Protection with removing sexual images and videos from the internet.
  • Cybertip.ca: Canada's tipline for reporting online sexual exploitation of children, listed among the caregiver resources by Public Safety Canada.

, Grace Okafor, Family & Parenting Writer
