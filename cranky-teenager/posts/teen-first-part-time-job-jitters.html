First part-time job jitters: supporting your teen without taking over

Work & independence · 9 min read

The uniform is laid out like a costume for a play your teenager did not audition for. The group chat has opinions. Someone's older sibling has horror stories about the manager. And your teen — who wanted this job, applied for this job, was pleased for a full day about getting this job — is now quietly catastrophising in the doorway about doing the till wrong, being slow, being watched, being the new person who knows nothing. This is the normal weather of a first job, and it presents parents with a familiar trap in a new costume: help too little and you feel absent; help too much and the first adult milestone of your teen's life quietly becomes yours. This guide walks the stages in order — application, interview, the night before, the first shifts, the first mistake — with the helpful move and the takeover move named at each one.

New-job nerves are not a problem to eliminate

Start with the framing the guidance for anxious teens insists on: nervousness before a first job is natural even when the job is a good fit, and it is a different thing from an anxiety disorder. A manageable challenge — real nerves, real stakes, support available — is exactly the size of difficulty that builds confidence. The parent's job is not to make the nerves go away before the first shift. It is to keep them company-sized: big enough to take seriously, small enough to walk in with. Teens starting work are anxious about recognisable things — an unfamiliar place, being evaluated by someone with actual authority, more demands on their time — and each of those has a rehearsal, a routine, or a sentence that shrinks it. What does not shrink any of them is a parent absorbing the task: filling in the application, rehearsing the teen into a script they cannot sustain, or phoning the workplace. Every absorbed task tells the teen the fear was accurate.

Underneath the table sits a rhythm borrowed from workplace-safety guidance for parents: check-ins at natural milestones — before they start, the first day, around thirty days, around sixty — rather than a continuous low-level interrogation. The same guidance makes a point parents rarely hear: much of the early work is managing your own nerves. Be observant before they start — notice the excitement and the concerns your teen drops in passing, without turning dinner into an interview. If the workplace is a shop or café, visit beforehand as an ordinary customer; if your teen is genuinely fine with it, a brief introduction to the manager lets you picture where they will be. Visualising the place settles the parent, and a settled parent is the single most calming piece of equipment a nervous teen owns.

The night before the first shift: a short routine

  1. Lay out the physical world. Uniform or outfit, shoes, bag, water bottle, the route and its timing, the alarm. Most night-before dread is logistics wearing a disguise; remove every removable unknown and the remaining fear is the honest kind.
  2. Rehearse the three sentences, not the whole job. How to greet the trainer, how to ask where things are, and the most valuable sentence in any first job: "I haven't done this before — can you show me?" Guidance written for teens themselves lands on the same skills: practise how to ask for help, write down questions as they occur, and use a breath or a grounding pause before the shift rather than white-knuckling through it.
  3. Name the professional basics once. Youth work guidance reduces them to a short list: show a positive attitude and take an interest in people; be proactive — spend breaks in the shared spaces rather than disappearing into a phone; be kind and be a team member; keep it professional, which mostly means friendly without oversharing. One pass, no lecture.
  4. Say the mistake sentence out loud. "You will mess something up in the first week, and that is the job working as designed." The teen-facing version is blunt: you cannot perfect a job in one shift, everyone in the building was once the new person, and supervisors are mostly watching how a mistake gets handled — admitted, owned, asked about — not whether one happens.
  5. Close the evening early and normally. No ceremony, no vigil. A normal bedtime is a message in itself: this household believes tomorrow is survivable, because it is.

What success looks like: the teen goes to bed able to say what time they leave, what they are wearing, who they report to, and what they will say when they do not know something. The nerves will still be there in the morning — smaller, specific, and carrying a plan.

The first bad shift: a debrief, not an interrogation

Somewhere in the first fortnight comes the bad shift: a rude customer, a public correction from a supervisor, a task that went sideways in front of a queue. Your teen will retell it in the car or not at all, and the debrief can go two ways. The interrogation version — twenty questions, rising parental alarm, an offer to call the manager — teaches the teen that reporting a bad shift triggers a parental incident, and the reports stop. The debrief version follows the rhythm the check-in guidance recommends: listen actively, attend to the details, and let the story arrive whole before any response. Virtual support earns its keep here too — the encouraging text sent before a shift, or timed to be found on a break, lands precisely because it asks for nothing back.

When advice is genuinely wanted, aim it at the teen's next move, never at the workplace's management. "What will you do if that customer type appears again?" is coaching. Drafting what the manager should have said is fiction. And protect the standing rule that mistakes get normalised in this house — adults included, out loud, with their own recent failures named plainly and without self-flagellation. A teen who watches a parent spill coffee and say "not my best work; messes can be cleaned" is learning the exact emotional move a first job demands weekly.

Jitters or a wrong job: the watch list

Support is not the same as insistence. National polling of parents of working teens found nearly half reporting that their teen had run into job-related problems — hours stretching beyond what was agreed, and unsafe situations among them — which is why the guidance pairs encouragement with a watching brief. Watch, calmly, for: hours creeping past the schedule the employer agreed to respect, especially where school nights are concerned; any request to skip safety steps or operate equipment the teen is not trained or permitted to use; sleep, schoolwork, or mood sliding in a way that tracks the rota rather than ordinary tiredness; and a teen who dreads every single shift weeks in, after the newness should have worn into routine. Parents should also simply know that teen employment carries legal limits — on total hours, on permitted work times, on hazardous tasks — which vary by place and are worth a ten-minute look for your own area.

The response ladder matters as much as the list. First rung: coach the teen to raise it — a scheduling conflict and a safety worry are both conversations a teen can be rehearsed for, and having them is itself a first-job skill. Second rung: help the teen weigh a different job; the polling guidance is explicit that if problems persist, encouraging a move to a job that supports the teen's health and safety is the right call, and a first job that ends by the teen's own assessment is experience, not failure. The rung where a parent contacts the employer directly is reserved for safety and legality — and by then it is not overstepping, it is the job.

FAQ

Your teen wants to quit after two shifts. Now what? Separate the verdict from the weather. Two shifts is all jitters and no data; agree on a fair trial — through the training period, roughly the thirty-day check-in — with the watch list above as the agreed early-exit conditions. If a watch-list item fires, the trial ends early with your full backing, and everyone already knows why.

Should a younger teen start with something informal? Often, yes. Polling guidance notes that for 14- and 15-year-olds, whose formal options are limited in many places, informal work — babysitting, lawn care, helping neighbours — builds confidence and experience at a scale where the jitters are small and the wins arrive fast. Parents can help by making introductions, outlining what a good job looks like, and talking through the challenges that might come up.

The job is fine but the pre-shift dread never fully leaves. Worry? A manageable challenge that matches the teen's capacity, with support available, is the growth-producing kind — dread that shrinks as competence grows is the process working. Dread that is intense, escalating, or starts swallowing school and sleep is a different signal: that is the watch-list conversation, and if it persists, a conversation with a qualified professional, not a bigger pep talk.
