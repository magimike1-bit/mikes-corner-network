When your teen refuses to go to school

School · 10 min read

This article is general information about a common parenting problem, not medical or mental-health advice. School refusal can be a sign of anxiety or another condition that deserves professional assessment — the plan below includes the points at which professionals should take over.

It starts as a stomach ache on a Sunday night, then a morning where getting out of bed is suddenly impossible, then a week where every day begins with a negotiation you lose. The instinct is to treat refusal as a discipline problem and push harder. But refusal is a symptom with several different causes, and the right first move for each cause is different — pushing harder is actively wrong for some of them. Sort the cause first. Then work the plan.

Refusal is not truancy

The distinction matters because the responses are opposite. As the New South Wales Department of Education's parent guidance puts it, school refusal is different from truancy because it stems from the teen's anxiety about school — worry about schoolwork, interacting with other students, dealing with teachers, sports, or being away from family. The Child Mind Institute's description of anxiety-driven refusal is recognisable: a teen who is extremely nervous about school, tries to stay home whenever possible, leaves school early or calls home from school, often feels sick or visits the school nurse with physical complaints, and becomes agitated just thinking or talking about school.

Most of what follows is about refusal. If what you are looking at is truancy, the discipline tools on the rest of this site are the relevant ones — and applying them to a refusing, anxious teen is the single most common way families make refusal worse, since punishing school refusal rarely works and often deepens the anxiety driving it.

Sort the cause before you pick the fix

You cannot diagnose your teen, and this table does not try to. It sorts the questions worth asking in the first days, because each likely driver has a different first move:

Likely driverClues to listen forFirst move
Social anxietyDread centres on people: presentations, group work, the cafeteria, a specific class's social weatherTell the school counsellor what you are hearing; ask about check-ins and a break space the teen can use
Academic overloadRefusal spikes around tests, presentations, or a subject where they have fallen behind; missing work is part of the storyContact the teacher: a plan for the backlog and extensions can remove the specific wall they are facing
Peer problems or bullyingSudden onset, a friendship rupture, reluctance that names — or carefully avoids naming — specific people or placesRaise it with the school as a safety issue for the school to address, not a resilience lesson for your teen
Physical illnessComplaints that persist through weekends and holidays, visible symptoms, a recent illness that never quite clearedFamily doctor first, before any attendance plan — physical causes get ruled in or out medically
Exhaustion and sleep collapseRefusal is worst early in the week, mornings are the whole battle, weekends reverse the clock entirelyTreat the sleep schedule as the primary problem and work on it in parallel with attendance

The conversation that feeds this table has rules: open-ended, at a low-pressure moment, with listening that does not instantly convert into fixing. A teen may not volunteer what is really going on, especially if they expect judgment or dismissal — and a professional assessment can also uncover things a parent conversation cannot, including a learning difficulty quietly making one subject unbearable.

The first two weeks: a procedure

  1. Day one: tell the school, do not just call in sick. Contact the school — teacher, counsellor, or the wellbeing/attendance contact — and say plainly that your teen is refusing and you are working on it. If the first person is not helpful, ask them to refer you to someone who can be. Learn the school's attendance policy at the same time, so you know the formal picture while you work.
  2. Days one to three: run the cause sort. Have the conversation, use the table, and write down what you learn. Patterns beat single mornings: note which days, which classes, and which complaints repeat.
  3. Agree a support structure at school. Ask for what makes school predictable and survivable: a named staff contact, advance notice of substitute teachers or schedule changes, lesson plans shared so a missed day is not a mystery, a counsellor check-in arrangement, and permission to be excused from a specific high-anxiety activity while the plan beds in. Knowing there is a person and a place to go when it gets bad is itself a relief to an anxious teen.
  4. Set the home-day protocol (below) for any day missed. School hours stay school-shaped: up, dressed, schoolwork provided by the school, screens parked. Boring, structured, and explicitly not a punishment.
  5. Build the return gradually if full days are not possible. Do not expect an all-or-nothing return. Start with partial days, one class, or a low-pressure activity, and increase time at school step by step — the gradual-exposure model used in anxiety treatment. Praise the brave behaviour specifically; acknowledge effort, not just outcomes.
  6. End of week two: review against the threshold. If refusal is continuing despite the school partnership and the gradual plan — or physical complaints are part of the picture — book the family doctor. If there is no physical cause, a doctor can refer on to a mental-health professional, and the school can formalise a transition plan with shorter days or fewer subjects while treatment starts.

What success looks like: not a teen who suddenly loves school. Success in week two is movement: more time in the building than week one, mornings that are hard but survivable, and a named adult at school your teen will actually go to. Judge the plan on direction, not on the absence of bad mornings.

The home-day protocol: boring, not punitive

A refused day that becomes a holiday teaches that refusal works; a refused day run as a punishment teaches an anxious teen that home is also unsafe. The middle path is structure. The expectation for a day home is schoolwork, and the standard advice is to remove the competing distractions — television, computer, headphones, games — during school hours until the teen is returning daily. Keep the day's shape: normal wake time, work in a common area, breaks on a school-like rhythm, and the usual evening routine and bedtime. Say the reason out loud, once: "Home during school hours is for working or recovering. It is not a day off, and it is not a punishment either." Then stop selling it. The protocol works by being uninteresting, not by being argued about.

When to escalate

Act sooner rather than later on any of these: refusal that continues past the two-week mark despite a genuine school partnership; physical symptoms that persist or worsen; withdrawal spreading beyond school — dropping friends, activities, and family life as well; or distress that includes talk of hopelessness or self-harm, which is urgent and goes straight to professional and crisis support, not to a wait-and-see plan. The reason for urgency is structural: avoidance brings short-term relief and long-term cost — academic setbacks, social withdrawal, deepening anxiety — and the longer refusal continues, the harder it is to reverse. Early help is not an overreaction; it is the cheap version of this problem.

FAQ

Should I physically make them go? For an anxious refuser, force escalates the anxiety and the family conflict without touching the cause. The evidence-based direction is the opposite: understand the driver, partner with the school, and return gradually with support. If you are at the point of considering force, you are past the point where professional guidance should be involved.

What about switching to online or alternative schooling? It is a real option — education departments list flexible and alternative arrangements, and for some teens it is the right long-term answer. Treat it as a decision to make with the school and, where anxiety is involved, a professional, rather than a surrender negotiated on a bad morning: for an anxious teen, removing school entirely can also remove the gradual exposure that resolves the anxiety.

Will missing two weeks ruin their year? Falling behind is a real cost of refusal, and missed work becomes its own source of anxiety — which is why the plan includes asking teachers about extensions and a backlog plan early. Two supported weeks with a school partnership is recoverable; two unsupported months is a different problem, and preventing that is the point of moving in week one.
