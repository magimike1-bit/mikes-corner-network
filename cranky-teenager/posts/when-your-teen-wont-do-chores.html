When your teen won't do chores

Home life · 9 min read

The dishwasher is still full, the bathroom has not been touched, and the response to asking is a sigh you could hear from the driveway. The usual escalation — bigger consequences, louder reminders — treats every refusal as defiance. But "won't do chores" is at least four different problems wearing the same eye-roll, and three of them are system problems, not attitude problems. Punishing a system problem produces a teen who is punished and a dishwasher that is still full.

Four different refusals wear the same eye-roll

Run this sort before you reach for a consequence:

Refusal typeWhat it looks likeThe actual fix
Unclear standardDoes a version of the chore; you reject it; argument about whether it "counts"A written done-standard (next section) — the fight is about the spec, not the work
Surprise timingExplodes when asked mid-game, mid-show, mid-anything; would have done it laterRequests made in advance with a deadline window, never ambush assignments

The expert guidance lines up with this sort. Kids often resist chores when they do not fully understand what "done" looks like, and resistance is a known signal that a task has been set too hard — the recommended response is to break it into smaller steps before assuming defiance. Surprise demands are their own trap: requests dropped on a teen mid-leisure reliably produce resistance that an identical request made ahead of time would not. Only after those three are handled does refusal mean what it looks like.

Write the done-standard down

"Clean your room" fails because it is a vibe, not a specification. The fix that counsellors recommend is concrete instructions: not "clean your room" but "dirty clothes in the hamper, bed made, books on the shelf." Take that one step further and write the standard once, where everyone can see it, so inspection stops being your opinion versus theirs:

Chore"Done" meansWindow
DishwasherEmptied, clean dishes in cupboards, dirty items from the sink loaded, machine ready to runAny time before dinner
Bathroom (weekly)Mirror wiped, sink and toilet cleaned, floor swept, towel hung, supplies restockedAny 30 minutes before Sunday evening
Own laundryWashed, dried, and put away — a clean pile on the bed is step three of four, not doneOne full cycle per week, day of their choosing
Trash and recyclingAll bins emptied to the outside bin, new liners in, bins back in placeEvening before collection day

Write your household's version together, and let the teen argue about the standard at the writing stage — that argument is cheap. What the table buys you is that later inspections become verification against an agreed spec: either the mirror is wiped or it is not, and neither of you has to perform outrage about it.

The setup procedure: choice, windows, one reminder

  1. Hold one family meeting and assign from a list. Put the household's recurring chores on the table and let your teen choose among them where you can. Input on which chores and when is what moves a chore from your demand to their commitment — and frame the whole list as shared household responsibility, not as punishment inventory.
  2. Attach the done-standard and the window. Every chore leaves the meeting with a written standard and a deadline window (from the table above), never a minute. Within the window, how and when is theirs — micromanaging the approach and nagging as the deadline approaches both reliably backfire.
  3. Set the consequence now, in when-then form. Known ahead of time, logical, and proportionate: "When the dishwasher is unloaded, then the car keys / WiFi / ride to practice." Natural consequences do the heavier teaching where they exist — laundry not washed is laundry not available to wear.
  4. Remind once, maximum. One reminder inside the window; after that, the agreed consequence happens without a lecture. Constant reminders train a teen to wait for reminder number five, and anger makes the whole system worse.
  5. Follow through every time. Consequences enforced only sometimes teach exactly one lesson: that waiting you out works. Consistency is the unglamorous mechanism under every version of this system that succeeds.

What success looks like: two or three weeks in, chores happen inside their windows without being mentioned, inspections are a glance against the written standard, and chore talk at home is mostly logistics instead of conflict. If you are still arguing, check which of the four refusal types has crept back in before raising consequences.

Pay or not: sort the task, not the teen

The allowance argument generates more heat than the chores do, partly because experts genuinely disagree. The case for paying ties money to value: a teen who can earn by solving household problems learns to look for them, and jobs beyond the basics get priced — small amounts, checked against the going rate for that kind of work. The case against is that paying for ordinary contribution turns family membership into a job, and every task acquires a price negotiation. There is no single evidence-based rule requiring allowance to be tied to chores or kept separate — which means the honest answer is a sorting test, applied task by task:

  • Membership test: does this task exist because the teen lives here and uses this home — their dishes, their laundry, their bathroom, shared spaces they occupy? Then it is unpaid membership work, the same category as the unpaid work every adult in the house does.
  • Hire-out test: is it occasional, beyond the household's routine, and something you might plausibly pay someone else to do — washing the car, clearing the garage, mowing, babysitting younger siblings? Then it is a legitimately paid extra job.
  • Decide before the negotiation starts. The line only works if it is drawn in advance: taking your own dishes to the kitchen is a normal responsibility; organising the garage is paid work. A boundary invented mid-argument is not a boundary.

When refusal is a signal, not a strategy

One pattern deserves a different response entirely: persistent refusal that is a change from previous behaviour. A teen who used to pitch in and has stopped doing everything — chores, and usually more besides — may be carrying stress, anxiety, or something heavier than a chore dispute. The right first move there is curiosity, not a bigger consequence: "This is new — what's going on?" And if you are worried about your teen's emotional wellbeing in general, a pediatrician or family counsellor is the appropriate next step. Chores are a small enough thing that a sudden total shutdown over them is information, and it is worth reading it as information before reading it as defiance.

FAQ

Should chores ever be used as a punishment? Keep the categories separate. Chores assigned as penalties teach that housework is what bad behaviour earns, which poisons the membership framing the whole system rests on. Consequences for a missed chore should be logical and connected — privileges that wait for the chore, or the natural result of the undone task — not extra chores piled on.

How many chores is reasonable for a teenager? There is no sourced magic number, and any chart claiming one is inventing it. The workable test is the window test: the total load fits inside the week alongside school, homework, and sleep without daily conflict, and each task has a standard and a window. If the load needs daily enforcement to survive, the load — or the system — is the problem.

What if my teen does the chore badly on purpose? That is what the written standard is for. Work that does not meet the agreed spec is not done, and the when-then consequence simply has not been unlocked yet — stated flatly, once, without a speech. Strategic incompetence only pays when redoing the argument is more exhausting for you than redoing the chore is for them; the standard reverses that math.
