Five-ingredient dinners from the cupboard

Weeknight meals · 6 min read

Five ingredients sounds like a constraint, but in pantry cooking it's closer to a relief. You don't need a long list — you need a formula. Every dinner below follows the same skeleton: a bulk base, a protein, a sauce or seasoning, and something to wake it up. Salt, pepper, oil, and water don't count toward the five. Neither does anything fresh you happen to have.

Formula 1: Grain + protein + sauce, in a bowl

This is the shape of a thousand good dinners. Cook rice (or any grain), warm canned black beans with a pinch of cumin and a spoonful of salsa, and pile them together. Top with hot sauce. That's four ingredients plus pantry basics. Swap in chickpeas with curry paste and coconut milk over rice. Swap in tuna mixed with olive oil and vinegar over pasta. The structure never changes: starch, protein, flavor. It works because each part does a different job, and nobody asks more of the ingredients than they can give.

Formula 2: The skillet hash

Small-cut starches crisped in a pan with something savory. The classic version: dice canned potatoes (or leftover rice, or cubed stale bread), fry until golden in oil, and stir in canned corned beef or black beans with a chopped onion if you have one. Season assertively. The trick to hash is patience — spread it in the pan, let it sit undisturbed until it browns, then flip. Constant stirring steams it instead of crisping it. A hash is also the fastest way to make a single sad can of something feel like dinner: fry it, crisp it, season it, and suddenly it's a dish with a name.

Formula 3: Pasta + canned tomato sauce

Pasta is five-ingredient cooking's best friend because it's nearly a meal by itself. Boil short pasta, then simmer a can of diced tomatoes with olive oil, garlic (fresh or powdered), and dried oregano while it cooks. Toss together with the starchy pasta water — half a cup will make the sauce cling. Optional upgrades that keep you at or under five: a can of tuna, a spoonful of peanut butter whisked into the sauce for body, or red pepper flakes. The pasta water tip matters more than any extra ingredient: it turns thin canned-tomato liquid into an actual sauce.

Formula 4: Soup from a pot of anything

Broth or water plus two or three shelf-stable things, simmered. Dried red lentils, a can of diced tomatoes, and curry paste in broth is a complete soup in about thirty minutes. White beans, canned tomatoes, and a splash of olive oil with dried herbs make a rustic one. The full method is in our soups from the shelf guide — the short version is that soup forgives everything: wrong proportions, odd combinations, and tired ingredients all melt into something comforting once they simmer together.

Formula 5: The sheet-pan or one-dish bake

If you have an oven, canned ingredients can roast too. Drain canned chickpeas, toss with oil and spices, and roast at high heat until crisp; pile over rice with a dollop of yogurt or salsa. Canned potatoes quartered and roasted with oil, salt, and paprika beat their reputation completely. Bakes work on the same principle: canned beans, frozen or canned vegetables, and tomato sauce in a dish with a starchy topping (breadcrumbs from stale bread, crushed crackers) baked until bubbling.

Making five ingredients taste like more

With so few parts, each one has to pull its weight. Three habits make the difference. First, season in layers: salt the water, salt the base, then adjust at the end. Second, finish with acidity — a squeeze of vinegar or lemon, or even a spoonful of pickle brine, brightens canned-heavy food more than any extra spice. Third, give something texture: crisped, toasted, or crunchy. Mush is the enemy of pantry cooking, and one crispy element per dish defeats it.
