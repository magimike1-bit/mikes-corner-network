Jarred salsa cupboard meals: rice skillet, soup pot and the liquid rule

Pantry staples · 8 min read

What is a jar of salsa, in dinner terms? Not a dip. A standard jar is 16 ounces (454 g) of tomatoes, peppers, onions and seasoning that have already been chopped, salted and cooked together, which makes it the closest thing the cupboard has to a sauce that needs no building. The two dinners here are a rice skillet of about 30 minutes and a soup of about 15 minutes, both low difficulty, both built entirely from shelf stable cans and jars, and both serving 4 to 6 people. What decides whether they work is not the salsa itself. It is when the jar meets the rice, because the checked recipes disagree about that completely, and each choice gives a different dinner.

The liquid rule: three documented ways to use one jar

Salsa is part sauce and part water. Rice, meanwhile, needs a measured amount of liquid and a set time covered. Put those two facts together carelessly and the skillet is either soupy or scorched. The three strategies below are all documented, all workable, and not interchangeable.

The picker is simple. Want separate grains and a skillet that eats like a casserole top? Cook plain and stir in. Want the fewest decisions and a softer, unified pot? Cook in the salsa, and respect the undisturbed 15 minutes, because lifting the lid is how that version loses its steam. Want dinner for 6 from one jar? Soup, where the jar is stretched by broth instead of being asked to carry a pan of rice alone.

One more choice sits inside all three: thickness. A thick, chunky salsa is the documented preference for the soup, where chunky style is stated to work best, and chunk is what keeps a cook in the salsa skillet from going watery. A thin, runny jar belongs in the soup pot, where extra liquid is welcome.

Dinner 1: the cupboard rice skillet

This skillet follows the first strategy, the cook plain and stir in method, with one cupboard swap: the fresh chicken in the documented version becomes a can of chunk chicken, drained. Canned chicken is a documented swap in the soup below, and it suits this method for the same reason. It is already cooked, so it only needs the final covered rest to heat through, exactly when the salsa and cheese go in.

Ingredients: 1 cup (about 200 g) uncooked long grain white rice, 1 and 3/4 cups (425 mL) chicken broth from a carton, cube or powder, 3/4 cup (175 mL) jarred salsa, 1 can of chunk chicken, drained, 1 cup (about 115 g) shredded cheese, and taco seasoning or a mix of chili powder and garlic powder from the spice shelf. Long grain rice is the right grain here for a documented reason: it cooks up drier and less sticky than short grain, which is better suited to sushi or risotto.

  1. Pour the broth into a deep skillet or Dutch oven, stir in the rice and the seasoning, and bring it to a simmer.
  2. Cover, turn the heat to low, and cook for 18 to 20 minutes, until the rice is tender and the liquid is absorbed. Do not stir.
  3. Fluff the rice once with a fork. Stir in the salsa, the drained canned chicken and half the cheese.
  4. Scatter the remaining cheese over the top, cover again, and rest off the heat for about 10 minutes, until the cheese has melted and the chicken is hot through.
  5. Serve straight from the skillet, with extra salsa at the table for anyone who wants the fresher, uncooked taste on top.

What success looks like: grains that are separate enough to fluff, cheese melted in a continuous layer, and no pool of liquid at the edge of the pan. A salsa verde version follows the second strategy instead: the same rice toasted briefly, then cooked in salsa verde and water or broth, covered, for about 15 minutes, with canned chicken and beans stirred in for the rest.

Dinner 2: black bean and salsa soup, all cans

The soup is the fastest full dinner the jar can make, and its formula is documented at 6 servings: 1 jar (16 ounces, 454 g) salsa, chunky style working best, 1 can (15 and 1/2 ounces, about 440 g) black beans, drained and rinsed, 43 ounces (about 1.25 L) chicken broth, 2/3 cup (about 120 g) instant brown or white rice, 1 cup corn kernels, drained if canned, and 2 and 1/2 cups cooked chicken, for which the recipe itself notes that canned chunk chicken can be used.

  1. Tip the broth and the whole jar of salsa into a large saucepan and bring it to a simmer.
  2. Add the drained beans, the drained corn, the instant rice and the canned chicken, in its chunks.
  3. Simmer for 10 minutes, until the instant rice is tender and the chicken is hot through.
  4. Taste before salting. The jar and the broth have both brought salt, and the soup may need none.
  5. If the pot looks thicker than you want, add more broth or more salsa, which is the documented fix, a splash at a time.
  6. Serve with tortilla chips crumbled over the top if the cupboard holds them.

What success looks like: a soup that is brothy rather than stew thick, beans and corn suspended in every spoonful, and a tomato and pepper flavour that tastes like it simmered for an hour rather than 10 minutes. That last part is the jar doing its work: the cooking already happened at the factory.

The opened jar clock and leftovers

An unopened jar is a true cupboard staple, keeping about one year from purchase in the pantry, per the USDA figures reported by Chowhound. The day it opens, it changes categories. Store bought salsa keeps for one month refrigerated after opening, at 40°F (4°C) or lower, per the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service. A half jar left from the skillet is therefore not a someday ingredient. It is a use this month ingredient, and the soup above, which takes a whole jar, is the cleanest way to finish a second jar rather than strand a third of one.

Leftovers follow the general chart instead. Health Canada puts soups at 2 to 3 days in the refrigerator and 4 months in the freezer, and cooked meat and vegetable dishes at 3 to 4 days refrigerated, with the refrigerator itself at 4°C (40°F) or lower and the danger zone between 4°C and 60°C (140°F). The skillet source allows up to 4 days for its leftovers in an airtight container, which matches the dish end of that chart. The soup freezes as it is. The skillet freezes acceptably and reheats better with a spoonful of water and a covered pan, because rice dries as it waits.
