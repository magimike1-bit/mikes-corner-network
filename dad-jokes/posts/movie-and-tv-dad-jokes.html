Movie and TV dad jokes

Movies & TV · 8 min read

Cinema recognised the dad joke long before the internet named it. WatchMojo's countdown of the twenty most cringeworthy movie dad jokes is essentially a museum of the form: the deadpan exchange in Airplane! built around "Don't call me Shirley," the lesser-of-two-weevils pun in Master and Commander, Timon's groaners in The Lion King, and entries drawn from Jurassic Park and Iron Man 2 besides. What the museum never does is hand the exhibits back. The jokes sit behind glass, ranked and clipped, when their natural habitat is a family movie night — told in the ad break, performed over the credits, and groaned at by exactly the audience the films had. This collection repatriates the form: original movie and TV jokes, sorted by the two traditions the films themselves use.

Tradition one: the scripted pun, delivered deadpan

The Master and Commander entry is the purest specimen in the countdown's catalogue: Captain Aubrey engineers an entire choice between two weevils so that the "lesser of two weevils" pun lands whichever one is picked, and the countdown treats the long setup not as a flaw but as a cornerstone of dad humour. That is tradition one — the joke is a machine built in advance, and the teller's job is a straight face. These twelve are built to the same specification: the setup does the work, the punchline is a single bent word or image, and the delivery should be as flat as the teller can manage:

Why did the film director bring a ladder to the set?
The script called for high drama.

What does a movie camera eat for lunch?
A reel sandwich.

Why did the popcorn go to the movies alone?
It was tired of being taken for a kernel of truth.

What is a film star's favourite kind of tea?
Celebri-tea — served in a mug shot.

Why did the sequel get promoted?
It followed the original's lead — and improved on the take.

What do you call a movie about a runaway tractor?
A thriller with a gripping plot.

Why did the TV remote refuse to work?
It was tired of being pushed around by everyone on the couch.

What is a documentary's favourite party trick?
Being factual at parties — it never acts.

Why did the credits roll so slowly?
They wanted their names to get a long moment.

What do you call a film with no plot twists?
A straight-to-DVD personality.

Why did the stunt double get a raise?
The lead actor refused to fall for the same joke twice.

What is a horror movie's favourite season?
Fall — everything is dying to appear in it.

Tradition two: the anti-joke, where the delivery is the joke

The countdown's Iron Man 2 entry documents the opposite failure mode with unusual precision: a character whose cadence announces a joke so confidently that the audience only notices afterwards that the line itself never delivered one. Played deliberately, that failure is tradition two — the anti-joke, whose humour lives entirely in the gap between the promised punchline and the flat fact that arrives instead. It is the hardest style to write and the easiest to ruin, because the teller must commit completely: pause, lean in, and then state something crushingly ordinary. These eight are built flat on purpose:

Why did the weatherman bring an umbrella to the studio?
He forecast a chance of reign over the whole broadcast.

What is a quiz show's favourite dessert?
Trivia trifle — layers of questions in every bite.

Why did the news anchor bring a ladder?
The headlines were over everyone's heads.

What do you call a sitcom with no audience?
A laugh track's worst nightmare.

Why did the soap opera character bring a map?
Every episode, they were completely lost.

What is a cartoon's favourite exercise?
Draw-cio — it stretches the imagination daily.

Why did the detective show get cancelled?
It could not case the audience's attention.

What do you call a TV that tells jokes?
A real stand-up screen.

Read that set again and notice the construction: every punchline is technically an answer and emotionally a shrug. Told with a grin, they die. Told like a newsreader, they are the funniest jokes in this collection — which is exactly the lesson the film catalogue teaches by accident.

Movie-night mechanics: performing the collection

A collection told all at once is a list; a collection dealt across a film is an event. This tested procedure runs the evening:

  1. Before the film, deal each viewer three joke cards — written out, face down — mixing both traditions without labelling them.
  2. Jokes may only be played in the gaps the film provides: the pre-show trailers, a paused snack run, and the credits. The film itself is a no-joke zone; competing with the movie is how tellers lose the room.
  3. The teller reads only the setup aloud, then the room votes — pun or anti-joke — before the punchline is revealed. Guessing the tradition correctly scores the guesser a point; fooling the room scores the teller two.
  4. Credits rule: the last joke of the night must be played as the credits roll, and it must be an anti-joke. Ending on a shrug is the house style.

Success looks like this: the room starts arguing about whether a joke "counts" as an anti-joke — the argument means both traditions have been understood well enough to be worth disputing, and the collection has become a game about jokes rather than a queue of them.

The framework exists because the two traditions fail differently. A pun told to the wrong room merely flops; an anti-joke told to the wrong room gets "fixed" by a helpful listener explaining what the punchline should have been — the only heckle that can actually kill the form. Match the tradition to the room and both survive the evening.

A note on stealing from the screen, honestly

One habit separates a good movie-night teller from a bore: credit the tradition, not the line. The famous specimens — Shirley, the weevils — belong to their films and work best as references, the shared vocabulary the room uses to judge the new material. The jokes in this collection are the new material. A teller who opens with the weevils story as a standard ("long setup, tiny pun, total commitment") and then deals the cards has taught the room how to listen — which, as every entry in the film catalogue proves, is most of what a dad joke ever needed.

FAQ

Why do films contain so many dad jokes? The countdown's catalogue suggests the practical answer: a dad joke is the cheapest reliable laugh in a script — one line, no setup footage, and a groan that plays in every demographic the studio is selling to.

What is the longest acceptable setup? The weevils standard: as long as it needs, provided the punchline was engineered before the setup began. Length is not the sin in dad humour; a long road to a small pun is the genre's favourite shape.

Can these be told outside movie night? The scripted puns travel anywhere. The anti-jokes need a seated, attentive room and a teller the audience trusts — which is to say, they need exactly what a movie night provides.
