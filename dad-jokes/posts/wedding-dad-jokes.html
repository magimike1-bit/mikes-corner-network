Wedding dad jokes for the speech, the card, and the MC

Occasions · 7 min read

A wedding joke is told in the riskiest room in ordinary life: two families, most of them strangers to each other, plus colleagues, grandparents, and at least one person filming. The big wedding-joke collections online ignore the risk entirely. They sort jokes by who holds the microphone — best man jokes in one pile, father-of-the-bride jokes in another — and never ask the question that actually decides whether a line lands or curdles: who is the joke about? A joke about the speaker is safe almost anywhere. A joke about the wedding itself is nearly as safe. A joke about either partner, or about anyone's past, is a gamble taken in front of witnesses. This page sorts by destination and grades by target, because those are the two decisions a nervous speaker actually has to make.

The sincere part: a wedding joke is not really there to be funny. It is there to lower the room's shoulders for the thirty seconds before the sincere sentence arrives. The groan is the door; the kind sentence walks through it.

The shared canon: wedding lines every collection carries

Every wedding list draws from the same small well. LaughDaddy's wedding-speech collection carries the centre of it: marriage as a deck of cards — in the beginning all you need is two hearts and a diamond, later you are looking for a club and a spade — and the older chestnut that love is blind but marriage is a real eye-opener. BridesmaidForHire's speech-jokes list adds the emotional fake-out, "the cake is in tiers, and so am I," and labels it exactly what it is: a classic dad joke, safe for all audiences. These lines survive because their target is marriage in the abstract. Nobody in the room is the punchline.

The canon has a shadow side, and the collections print that too, without warnings. NaturallyFunny's best-man page opens with an anecdote about a speech that was going well until the speaker referred to the groom's ex as "the one that got away" — followed, the page itself notes, by awkward silence. PunAndJokes' father-of-the-bride list includes the line a father supposedly says when giving his daughter away: "Take her; I've been raising her for free." Both lines aim at a real person connected to the couple, and both are presented as ordinary list entries. The sections below are built on the opposite rule: the closer a joke's target sits to the couple, the more carefully it has to be chosen — and some targets are simply retired.

For the speech: jokes that aim at the speaker

A speech joke has one structural advantage: the speaker is standing right there, available as a target, and self-directed jokes cannot offend anyone else in the room. LaughDaddy's delivery advice is worth following here — pause after the joke and let the room react, know the audience before choosing how cheeky to be, and rehearse the toast out loud more than once. These lines are written to open or punctuate a speech, and every one of them lands on the person holding the glass.

  1. Speech ask to be kept short? It said it had been raised to be brief.
  2. Best man bring a ladder to the reception? He wanted his toast to reach new heights.
  3. Father of the bride practise his speech in the garden? He wanted his remarks to grow on people.
  4. Microphone get nervous before the toast? It had a lot of feedback to deal with.
  5. Speaker thank the seating plan? It had put him next to his best material.
  6. Toast put on its best clothes? It wanted to be well-dressed for the occasion.
  7. Speechwriter bring a pencil to the reception? He wanted to draw a few laughs from the room.
  8. Speaker bring a ladder to the venue? He heard the bar for toasts had been set high.

For the card: lines that work read silently

A card is read at a kitchen table, possibly weeks later, with no voice, no pause, and no room to warm up. That rules out every line that needs timing — and rules in short wordplay aimed at the wedding's objects: rings, vows, cake, and the day itself. Nothing in a card should aim at either partner, because a written joke cannot smile to show it means well.

  1. Wedding ring never get nervous? It had been going in circles for years and loved it.
  2. Cake feel proud at the reception? It finally got to be the centre of attention in tiers.
  3. Vows ask for a pen? They wanted everything in their own words.
  4. Bouquet enjoy the ceremony? It was thrilled to be picked.
  5. Guest book feel important? Everyone wanted a word with it.
  6. Wedding car wear ribbons? It wanted to tie everything together.
  7. First dance step on no toes? It had been practising its best moves.
  8. Confetti make a quiet exit? It preferred to fall gracefully.

For the MC and the gaps: short filler that offends no one

Every wedding has dead air: photos running long, a buffet changeover, a DJ cable being hunted down. Whoever holds the microphone in those minutes needs lines that are short, need no setup, and target the situation itself — the one target guaranteed to be shared by the whole room.

  1. Buffet line move so slowly? It was savouring the moment.
  2. Photo session run long? It wanted every angle covered — twice.
  3. Dance floor stay empty at first? It was waiting for a brave opener.
  4. DJ's cable go missing? It was tired of being taken for granted.
  5. Sun come out for the photos? It had been waiting to shine.
  6. Evening guests arrive hungry? They had saved room for the occasion.

Choosing a wedding joke: a small safety framework

  1. Name the target before anything else. Speaker, wedding, situation, couple, or a specific person. The first three are safe in almost any room. The couple as a pair is safe only when the joke is affectionate and generic. A specific person — and above all anyone from either partner's past — is out.
  2. Match the joke to the destination. Speech lines can lean on the speaker's delivery; card lines must survive silent reading; filler must work with no setup at all. A line that needs a pause belongs in a speech or nowhere.
  3. Place one joke early. LaughDaddy's structure is the reliable one: humour first to relax the room, then a personal anecdote, then well wishes to close. A joke in the first minute does more work than three jokes stacked at the end.
  4. Rehearse the pause, not just the line. The delivery advice in the collections is consistent on this: say the joke, stop, let the room react. A rushed second sentence buries the groan that makes the first one count.

What success looks like: the couple asks for the speech afterwards. Not the video — the words, because there was a line in it they want to keep.

FAQ

How many jokes should a wedding speech contain? Two or three, spread out. The collections' own structure points the same way: LaughDaddy builds its toast advice around humour balanced with a personal anecdote and well wishes, which leaves room for only a few jokes before the balance tips into a stand-up set the room did not ask for.

Are jokes about marriage itself safe? The abstract canon is — the deck of cards and the eye-opener line have survived because marriage in general is the target, not this marriage. The moment the same idea is aimed at the actual couple ("you two will be looking for a club and a spade"), it stops being canon and starts being a prediction. Keep it general or cut it.

What is the single riskiest joke topic at a wedding? Past relationships. NaturallyFunny's own opening anecdote is the evidence: a reference to the groom's ex produced awkward silence in the middle of a speech that had been going well. There is no version of that line with a safe target, which is why this page retires the whole category.
