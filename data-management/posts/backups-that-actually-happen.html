Backup habits that survive real life

Backups · 5 min read

Everyone knows they should back up. Most people don't — not because they disagree, but because the backup setup requires effort they never quite get around to. The fix is a routine with almost no ongoing effort: automatic, verified, and simple enough that it keeps running when life gets busy.

The simple rule: two kinds of copies, one offsite

You don't need enterprise infrastructure. You need copies that fail differently. If your laptop drive dies, the backup drive plugged into it probably isn't helpful — and a cloud-only copy doesn't help if you can't get online. A sensible setup for most people:

  • One local copy: an external drive with automatic backups (the built-in tools on your operating system handle this fine).
  • One remote copy: cloud storage or cloud backup, so a stolen laptop, a house fire, or a dead drive doesn't take everything.

Two copies in two different places protect you from almost every common disaster: hardware failure, theft, fire, and accidental deletion.

Automate it or it won't happen

Manual backups have a predictable lifecycle: you do them religiously for two weeks, then monthly, then "I really should get around to that." The only backup routine that survives real life is one that runs without you:

  • Turn on the automatic backup feature your operating system already includes, and point it at an external drive you leave plugged in (or plugged in regularly).
  • For cloud copies, use a sync service or a cloud backup plan that uploads on a schedule — nightly or weekly, your choice, but scheduled.
  • Set a calendar reminder for the first backup of each month to glance at the logs and confirm it's actually running. One minute, once a month.

Know what you're backing up

A backup that misses the important stuff is worse than no backup, because it gives you false confidence. Check that your setup covers:

  • Documents and work files: the obvious stuff — usually covered automatically.
  • Photos and videos: often the most irreplaceable data you own. Make sure they sync somewhere, not just on your phone.
  • Email and contacts: if you use a cloud email provider, these already live on their servers — but check what happens if the account is lost.
  • App data and settings: password managers, notes apps, financial software. Know which of these sync automatically and which need exporting.

Test your restores

A backup you've never restored from is a theory. Once or twice a year, pick a file — a document, a photo — and restore it from your backup. This takes five minutes and answers the only question that matters: does this actually work? People discover corrupted backup drives and expired cloud subscriptions this way, while there's still time to fix them.

Common ways backups fail

  • The external drive sits in the same drawer as the laptop. It survives a drive failure but not theft or fire. That's what the offsite copy is for.
  • Cloud sync is not backup. If you delete a file and the deletion syncs everywhere, it's gone everywhere. Real backup keeps versions and deleted files recoverable for a while — check that yours does.
  • The subscription lapsed. Cloud storage billed to an expired card quietly stops working. That monthly one-minute check catches it.
  • Only the old computer is backed up. After buying a new machine, re-set-up the backups immediately — new-device day one, not "later."

A minimum routine that works

If you do nothing else: enable automatic local backups to an external drive, sync your photos and key documents to a cloud service, and restore one test file twice a year. That's roughly an hour of setup and five minutes a month — and it covers nearly everything that goes wrong for ordinary households and small businesses.
