How to adjust kids' bedtimes for the time change

Families · 8 min read

Here's the fall back problem in one sentence: your toddler's body doesn't know the clock changed. A child who happily slept until 6:00 a.m. will, on the morning of Sunday, November 1, wake up at what their body insists is 6:00 — but the clock now says 5:00. And a child who is wide awake at 5:00 a.m. is not going back to sleep because a government proclamation says the hour is new. The good news: fall back is the fixable clock change, because you can prepare. Four evenings of small shifts, starting Wednesday, October 28, and the whole thing passes almost unnoticed.

Why the shift works

Kids' sleep runs on routine and light, not on the numbers on the clock. If you move bedtime and wake-up time 15 minutes later each day for four days before the change, your child's body gradually adopts a schedule one hour later. Then the clocks fall back an hour on Sunday morning — and the two shifts cancel out. Your child wakes at their usual clock time, rested, and bedtime on Sunday night lands where it always did. Nothing about the routine changes except the numbers, which is exactly how kids like it.

The direction matters: for fall back, shift later. (Spring forward is the opposite — earlier, and genuinely harder. One problem at a time.)

Age-by-age notes

  • Babies under about 6 months: their schedules are still forming, and most ride the change out with a day or two of fuzziness. You can use a gentler version — 10 minutes a day — or simply split the difference on Sunday (see below) and let naps absorb the rest.
  • Toddlers and preschoolers: the full four-day plan works best here, because this is the age group that will absolutely be standing beside your bed at 5:00 a.m. otherwise. Keep wake-up time just as consistent as bedtime; it's the morning anchor that does most of the work. A toddler clock that changes colour at wake-up time is genuinely useful this week if you have one.
  • School-age kids: often adjust in a day or two on their own. The four-day shift still helps — Monday morning, November 2, comes early enough as it is — but don't stress if you only manage the last two or three nights.
  • Teenagers: congratulations, fall back is the one change that works in their favour. An extra hour in the morning is rarely a problem. Leave them to it.

Light is the other lever

Timing sleep is only half the adjustment — light is the other half. In the four shift days, keep evenings dim in the last hour before bed (screens down or off, lamps over overheads), and get outside into daylight soon after the shifted wake-up time. After November 1, mornings are brighter by the clock, which works in your favour: open the curtains at breakfast and let the new schedule set itself. This is the same advice that anchors our tips for grown-ups, because parents' body clocks obey the same rules.

What about naps?

Naps move with everything else. Shift each nap's start and end by the same 15-minute steps as bedtime, so the whole day's rhythm slides together rather than bedtime moving alone and colliding with a nap that now ends too close to it. Two nap-specific cautions: first, protect the end of the last nap of the day — a late-running afternoon nap is the fastest way to wreck the bedtime you've carefully shifted, so wake your child from the last nap at the planned (shifted) time even if the nap started late. Second, if your child is on the edge of dropping a nap anyway, this is not the week to drop it. Keep the structure stable, shift the times, and make any bigger sleep changes the week after, when the clocks have settled.

The morning after: Sunday, November 1

Plan Sunday morning before it arrives. Get your child up at the usual clock time — the plan's payday — even if Saturday night ran long after trick-or-treating. Breakfast near a bright window, outdoor time in the morning if the weather allows, and the regular day's rhythm at the regular clock times from the very first day. If your child does surface an hour early (the best plans leak a little), treat it as quiet time rather than the start of the day: stay dim, stay boring, keep them in their room with books or quiet toys until the usual wake-up time if they'll tolerate it, and don't hand over breakfast and cartoons at 5:00 — that's how a one-off early morning becomes the new schedule. By Monday or Tuesday, most kids who did the shift — and plenty who only did half of it — are back on track.

Get the other caregivers on the plan

A bedtime shift only works if the whole day is shifted, and for many kids the day belongs partly to other people. Tell your daycare, preschool, or the grandparents doing pickup exactly what you're doing and why — most caregivers have survived many clock changes and will happily match a shifted nap or a slightly later lunch if they know it's deliberate rather than drift. The failure mode to avoid is a split schedule: home shifting bedtime later while daycare keeps naps at the old times can leave a child napping too close to the new bedtime, awake and furious at both ends of the evening. One short conversation — "we're moving everything 15 minutes a day until Sunday, here's the nap time we're aiming for" — keeps every adult rowing in the same direction. If your child has an early Monday commitment (a swim class, a long school-bus ride), mention the change to yourself as much as anyone: the first school morning after the change is not the morning to discover the routine runs ten minutes longer than the bus allows.

If you forgot and it's already Sunday

No plan survives contact with October. If November 1 arrives unplanned, use the split-the-difference approach: for the next three days, split the hour. Put your child down 30 minutes later than the clock-time usual (which feels like 30 minutes early to them), wake them 30 minutes later than the new clock time, then land on the normal schedule by Wednesday. Expect one or two early mornings and slightly ragged evenings; it resolves within the week. Protect your own sleep in the meantime — a parent who went to bed at their usual body-clock time handles a 5:30 a.m. wake-up much better than one who stayed up celebrating the "extra hour."

Halloween collision: the biggest shift night, Saturday, October 31, is Halloween. The plan still works — trick-or-treating naturally runs late, and a bedtime an hour later than usual barely registers as a change that night. Just keep the routine itself (bath, pyjamas, story) in the usual order, and let the sugar burn off before lights-out.

And if your province doesn't change clocks anymore

Families in BC, Alberta, Manitoba, the Northwest Territories, Saskatchewan, and Yukon can skip this entire guide — permanently. That's rather the point of the changes described in our province-by-province rundown. Send this page to a friend in Ontario instead; they'll need it.

A note on scope: this guide is general information about routines, not medical advice. If your child has a diagnosed sleep problem, snores heavily, or you're worried about their sleep for any reason, that's a conversation for your family doctor or pediatrician — not a parenting website, including this one.
