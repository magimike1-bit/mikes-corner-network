Pets and the time change: adjusting feeding and walk schedules

Household · 6 min read

Will your dog or cat actually notice a one hour change, and if so, what should you change first? Short answer: pets live by routine more than by the clock on the wall, so a sudden one hour jump in feeding and walks is the part that can unsettle them. Dogs and cats are generally adaptable to schedule changes. The kind way to use that adaptability is to move in small steps, not to test it all on one Sunday morning.

Shift to the new clock, or hold body time?

Sources disagree on the headline choice, so here is the decision made explicit. One approach gradually shifts mealtimes to the new clock time. Another, reported from other experts, keeps feeding at the same body time all year. Both can work. The deciding factor is your household: your own work and sleep are moving to the new clock, and pet dogs in particular adjust their sleep and wake cycles to their owners schedules. For most households that settle on the new time themselves, shifting the pet to the new clock is the coherent choice. Holding body time suits a household whose own routine barely moves.

Shift to the new clockMove feeding, walks and play by about 10 to 15 minutes a day toward the new timeMost households, whose own schedules move with the clock
Hold body timeKeep the pet routine at the same body time all yearHouseholds whose own routine stays effectively unchanged
Split meal bridgeFeed a small meal at the usual time and the rest at the new time during the transitionPets who demand breakfast loudly at the old time

That comparison table is the article in miniature. Pick one approach before the weekend, and do not mix all three at random.

How much should you move each day?

About 10 to 15 minutes a day is the range the sources converge on. Chewy guidance suggests gradually shifting feeding by about 15 minutes a day toward the new time. Spot guidance for dogs uses 10 to 15 minutes a day across feeding, walking and playtime. Dr Jeffrey Kelu, a circadian researcher at King’s College London quoted in national reporting, advises adjusting routines gradually about a week before the change, by 10 to 15 minutes. Starting a few days ahead is enough for a one hour move at that pace. Starting the morning of the change is the sudden version, and sudden shifts in feeding or walking can trigger anxiety or an upset stomach.

A four day version looks like this. Day one, breakfast and the morning walk move 15 minutes toward the new time. Day two, another 15 minutes, with evening feeding and the last walk moving too. Day three, another step. Day four, you are at the new clock time, and the pet has never faced a gap larger than a quarter hour.

What about the pet who demands food at the old time?

Use the split meal bridge Chewy describes. Feed a small meal at the usual time, and the rest at the new time. The pet is not ignored for an hour, and the main meal still lands where you want the routine to end up. As the daily shift progresses, the small early portion can shrink and the main portion takes over. It is a bridge, not a permanent second breakfast, unless you enjoy being trained.

For cats, an automatic feeder can help maintain consistency, as Spot notes. Set it to the shifted schedule rather than the old one, and change its timing on the same 10 to 15 minute a day plan. A feeder that still fires at the old body time will quietly undo the rest of your shifting.

Do dogs and cats differ here?

Partly. Dogs tie their day to walks as well as meals, and morning sunlight exposure on walks helps regulate a dog’s internal clock, so a morning walk at the new time does double duty. Dogs also follow their owners schedules closely, which cuts both ways: your own abrupt change becomes theirs. Cats are more feeder and territory driven, which is why consistency tools such as a timed feeder are mentioned most often for cats. In both cases the principle is identical. Small steps, all cues moving together, no single one hour jump.

What about medication timing?

The sources behind this article cover food, walks and play, and none of them addresses pet medication timing around the change. This guide will not improvise an answer for twice daily medication or any other prescribed schedule. Ask your veterinarian before the weekend, write down the answer, and follow that plan rather than the feeding plan. The feeding shift and the medication plan are separate decisions.

When is it more than the time change?

Watch for the stress signs Chewy lists: appetite loss, house accidents, excessive vocalizing and destructive behaviour. A day or two of mild unsettled behaviour during a gradual shift is one thing. Signs that persist, appetite loss that continues, or any sign that worries you is a reason to contact your veterinarian. Pets cannot tell you what changed, so a routine change is a prompt to observe, not a diagnosis to assume.
