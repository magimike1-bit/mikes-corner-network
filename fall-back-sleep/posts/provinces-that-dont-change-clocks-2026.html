Which provinces don't change clocks in 2026? BC, Alberta, Manitoba, NWT — and the holdouts

Provinces & territories · 8 min read

For most of living memory, the answer to "do we change the clocks?" was the same almost everywhere in Canada: yes, twice a year, whether we liked it or not. November 1, 2026 breaks that pattern. This year, four jurisdictions — British Columbia, Alberta, Manitoba, and the Northwest Territories — stopped changing clocks, joining Saskatchewan and Yukon, which had opted out years ago. If you've lost track of who does what, you're not alone. Here's the whole map, province by province.

The no-change club

British Columbia — permanent Pacific time

BC made the first move. The province announced in March 2026 that it would adopt permanent, year-round daylight saving time, and its final-ever clock change happened on March 8, 2026, when clocks sprang forward. BC's year-round standard is now officially called Pacific time (PCT), set at UTC−7 — the same offset the province used to have only in summer. On November 1, 2026, when clocks would previously have gone back, nothing happens: the transition simply completes. The province's stated reasons were fewer disruptions for families, more consistency for business, and an extra hour of evening light in winter. One local note: a handful of communities in BC's east and northeast have long followed Mountain-time arrangements rather than Pacific time — if that's you, check the provincial guidance for your area, because your rules follow the Mountain-time story below.

Alberta — permanent Alberta Time

Alberta followed in the spring: its legislature passed the change in May 2026, adopting permanent Alberta Time (ABT) — UTC−6, the offset previously known as Mountain Daylight Time. Beginning in November 2026, Alberta clocks no longer reset to Mountain Standard Time in the fall. So on November 1, Edmonton and Calgary stay exactly where they are. One consequence Albertans will notice: in winter, Alberta is now only one hour behind Toronto instead of two, and the same one hour behind Winnipeg — actually, read on, because Manitoba moved too.

Manitoba — permanent daylight time, by popular vote

Manitoba's decision is the freshest of the four. The province asked its residents in May 2026: nearly 72,000 votes came in, 92% supported ending the twice-yearly clock changes, and 58% preferred permanent daylight time over permanent standard time. On September 17, 2026, the government announced Manitoba would stay on daylight time permanently — UTC−5, year-round. Clocks will not turn back on November 1. The province's health analysis and the premier's announcement both singled out families: that one-hour shift disrupts kids' sleep and routines for days, and Manitobans voted to stop doing it.

Northwest Territories — Northwest Territories Time

The NWT made its move alongside Alberta, its largest neighbour and key economic partner. Its new permanent standard, Northwest Territories Time (UTC−6), came into force through regulations on August 21, 2026, so the territory shares Alberta's offset all year — and, as the territorial government points out, now matches Saskatchewan's clock too. The decision followed a 2022 survey in which more than 87% of respondents supported ending the seasonal changes. No clock change will occur in the NWT on November 1, 2026.

Saskatchewan and Yukon — the originals

Saskatchewan has kept Central Standard Time year-round since 1966 — most Saskatchewanians have never changed a clock in their lives. (The exception is Lloydminster, on the Alberta border, which follows Alberta's time — now permanent Alberta Time, so it has also stopped changing.) Yukon stopped in 2020 and keeps a single year-round time at UTC−7 — which, conveniently, is now the same offset as BC's Pacific time, so Whitehorse and Vancouver share a clock all year.

Who still falls back on November 1, 2026

The "holdouts" — though "still deciding" is fairer — are concentrated in the east:

  • Ontario still changes clocks across most of the province. The government has pointed to coordination with Quebec and New York State — trade, markets, and broadcasting — as the reason to move together rather than alone. Exception: on October 13, 2026, Ontario announced that communities in the northwest, west of the 90th meridian (Kenora, Atikokan, and area), will stay aligned with Manitoba — so that corner of Ontario does not fall back this year.
  • Quebec still falls back across most of the province. Long-standing exception: the Lower North Shore stays on Atlantic Standard Time year-round, which means it drifts from Montreal time in winter.
  • Atlantic Canada — New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland and Labrador — all still observe daylight time and fall back on November 1.
  • Most of Nunavut still observes the seasonal change as well.

What the new patchwork does to everyday life

  • Winter time gaps shift. Winnipeg reads the same time as Toronto all winter (they drift apart again when Ontario springs forward on March 14, 2027). Vancouver is two hours behind Toronto in winter instead of three — and one hour ahead of Seattle, which still falls back.
  • Devices may get it wrong. Phones and computers learn time rules from software updates. The NWT government warned that older devices and some Windows computers may need a manual update; the University of Alberta flagged calendar events shifting by an hour around the Alberta change. If a device disagrees with your wall clock on November 1, believe the updated one — and check our checklist.
  • Sleep disruption is now regional. If you're in a no-change province, congratulations: the whole point is that your family's sleep never gets yanked around again. If you still fall back, our kids' bedtime guide and week-after tips will smooth it out.

One more everyday effect worth planning around: anything scheduled across the new divide. A weekly call between Calgary and Toronto that sat at 10:00 and 12:00 all summer now sits at 10:00 and 11:00 for the winter — nothing "changed" on either end, but the gap did. Flights, trains, and broadcasts between changing and non-changing regions shift the same way, and not every booking system, calendar invite, or recurring meeting series handles a rule change mid-series correctly. If November brings a mysteriously early or late meeting, a shifted clock rule is the first suspect — before assuming someone made an error.

Will the rest of the country follow? Ontario's position — move together with Quebec and New York, or not yet — is the one to watch. For 2026, though, the map above is the answer, and for the first time in decades, "fall back" is only half a Canadian tradition.
