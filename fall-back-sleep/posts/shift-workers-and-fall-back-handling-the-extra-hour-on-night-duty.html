Shift workers and fall back: handling the extra hour on night duty

Work and sleep · 7 min read

13 hours. That is the length of a standard 12 hour night shift on the night the clocks go back, because the hour from 1:00 to 2:00 a.m. happens twice. Daylight saving time ends on Sunday, November 1, 2026, and for most workers that weekend means a longer sleep. For the person on night duty, falling back can mean more hours on the job instead. Planning for 13 hours, rather than discovering them at the second 1:30 a.m., is the whole job of this guide.

The bottom line first

Three moves carry most of the benefit, and all three start before the weekend. First, shift your sleep, meals, exercise and light later by about 15 to 20 minutes a day for roughly three days before the change. That gradual later drift is the advice the American Academy of Sleep Medicine gives for the fall transition, cited in workplace guidance from SAIF, and it matches the direction of the clock: fall back is a delay, so your routine needs to move later, not earlier.

Second, protect a short nap. A safe place for a 20 to 30 minute nap is among the workplace supports SAIF recommends for this week, and tailored shift work guidance includes a 15 to 20 minute nap before a shift. Third, keep your daytime sleep window consistent after the shift. Consistency is what lets the adjustment settle instead of restarting every day.

None of that removes the extra hour. It spreads the cost of that hour across several days, in small pieces your body can absorb.

Before the weekend: drift later in small steps

Use this tested procedure in the three days before Sunday, November 1. It is built from the gradual shift described in the sources above.

  1. Pick your usual wake time, meal times and main sleep time, and move each one 15 to 20 minutes later on the first day.
  2. Move them another 15 to 20 minutes later on the second day, keeping meals and exercise moving with sleep so the cues stay together.
  3. Move them once more on the third day if you need the full hour. By the weekend you will have covered most of the clock change before it happens.
  4. On the transition night itself, keep breaks and any nap to the plan you agreed with your workplace, rather than improvising when tiredness peaks.
  5. After the shift, go to your main sleep at the planned new clock time, and keep that window on the following days.

What success looks like: you arrive at the extra hour already living most of an hour later, your first daytime sleep after the shift happens at a planned time, and the following sleep starts at the same time again.

On the night: the repeated hour and the nap decision

The repeated hour is a staffing and alertness question before it is a sleep question. Whether the extra hour is scheduled a particular way, and how it is treated by your employer, varies by workplace. The packet behind this article found no Ontario employment standards source it could rely on for a general pay rule, so this guide makes no pay claim. Check with your employer before the weekend rather than asking for the first time during the shift. That single conversation settles who is working the second 1:00 to 2:00 a.m. hour, where breaks fall, and whether a nap room is available.

For the nap itself, use a simple decision framework. If your workplace provides a safe nap place and your break allows it, a short nap of about 20 to 30 minutes fits the support SAIF describes. If no safe place exists, do not substitute an unsafe one. Raise it before the weekend instead. SAIF also recommends that employers avoid back to back shifts in the week after the change, which is worth knowing when rosters for that week are posted. A roster that stacks the long transition night against another close shift works against every other step in this guide.

Light and caffeine: what the sources do and do not settle

Honesty matters here. The general results behind this article gave conflicting light advice for night workers: bright evening light in one, blocking morning light in another, morning light in a third, with no reconciliation. This guide will not pretend those schedules are the same. Light timing for a permanent night worker is individual enough that a general article should not prescribe it. What the sources agree on is the gradual later shift of the whole routine, light included, in the days before the change.

Caffeine timing on the transition night was absent from the top results altogether. Rather than invent a cut off time, the practical step is narrower: plan caffeine as part of the shift plan you discuss before the weekend, and protect the daytime sleep window that follows. If caffeine regularly keeps you from sleeping in the day, that pattern is a topic for a health professional, not for a longer night shift experiment.

Sleeping after: consistency does the heavy lifting

Tailored guidance for shift workers, reflecting expert consensus work and American Academy of Sleep Medicine practice parameters, stresses keeping the daytime sleep window consistent on work days and off days. The same guidance is candid about the limit: complete circadian re entrainment is only realistic with a consistent schedule across seven days. Most real rosters do not allow that, and this guide will not pretend otherwise.

So aim for the achievable version. Keep the main sleep block at a consistent clock time across the change. Let the pre weekend drift do the moving, once, instead of swinging the sleep window back and forth each day. Expect the first daytime sleep after the long night to feel like the anchor for the week that follows.

When to see a professional

Talk to a qualified health professional if sleepiness persists well beyond the adjustment week, if you fall asleep unintentionally during waking hours, or if tiredness is affecting safety at work. Talk with your supervisor sooner if the roster itself is the problem, for example repeated back to back shifts in the week after the change. A clock change explains a few rough days. It does not explain ongoing, unsafe exhaustion.
