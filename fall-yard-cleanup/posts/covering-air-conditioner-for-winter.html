Covering the air conditioner for winter: top only, or not at all

Winterizing · 6 min read

The instinct is understandable: the outdoor unit was expensive, winter is coming, so wrap it up tight. That wrap is the mistake. A full cover sealed around the cabinet traps moisture against the metal, and trapped condensation is what drives rust and corrosion over a winter. It also builds a sheltered, windproof cavity at exactly the season when birds, mice, chipmunks, and rats are looking for one, and rodents inside a unit chew wires, insulation, and connections. The cover question has a real answer, but it starts with a different question entirely.

Decision one: what system is actually in the yard?

Before choosing any cover, confirm which system the outdoor unit belongs to, because the two answers are opposites.

  • Heat pump: if the unit heats the house in winter, it runs all season and needs airflow to operate properly. It stays uncovered, and it does not get powered off. Covering a working heat pump is not a judgment call, it is simply wrong. In most cases heat pumps should remain uncovered through the winter, especially when operating in heating mode.
  • Cooling-only central air conditioner: this unit sits idle until spring, and it is the only kind the rest of this guide applies to. Even here, the default answer is no full cover. In many cases modern systems are designed to withstand winter as they sit, and the manufacturer may recommend against covering at all, so check the manual before buying anything. Some manufacturers do not recommend covers to the point that using one might affect the warranty.

Decision two: what is actually threatening the unit?

The disagreement in most advice comes from mixing up two different threats. Snow sitting on top of a unit is the threat people picture, and it is the weaker one: exterior units are built for harsh weather, with coils and fins constructed to resist corrosion and a housing that protects the internal parts from moisture and debris. The real threats come from above. Falling ice chunks and roof runoff can dent the top and reach the fan, and heavy leaf and twig drop from overhanging trees collects in and around the unit all fall. As temperatures rise and fall, meltwater that refreezes can also expand and damage the fan and other delicate parts. So the decision framework is short: nothing threatening from above means no cover at all. Debris or ice falling from above means protect the top only, and leave the sides open to the air.

The November procedure

  1. Confirm the system type. Use the decision above. Heat pump: stop here, the job is done. Check: the thermostat's heating mode is known to run through this unit, or known not to.
  2. Clear around the unit. Remove leaves, dirt, and buildup from inside the top grille area and around the base, and keep the space open so snow and meltwater do not pool against the cabinet. Cut back branches hanging over the unit. Check: the base sits clear and drainage runs away from it.
  3. Turn the power off. For a cooling-only unit that will not run in winter, shutting off power at the outdoor disconnect prevents it switching on unexpectedly during an unusual warm spell. Check: the disconnect is off and stays off until spring.
  4. Fit the top protection, only if decision two called for it. Use a breathable cover designed for outdoor units, fitted to the top with the sides open. Secure it without over-tightening and without bending fins or damaging the cabinet. Check: air moves freely around the sides, and nothing presses on the coil fins.
  5. Set the spring reminder now. The cover comes off before the system restarts, and power comes back on at the same visit. Check: the reminder exists somewhere it will actually be seen in April or May, not just intended.
  6. Check after major storms. After heavy wind or a storm, confirm the cover has not shifted and debris has not piled up around the base. Check: cover in place, base clear, no nesting material visible.

What success looks like

Success in January is a unit that is either bare and clear at the base, or wearing a top cover with open sides and a clear base, and nothing sealed, tarped, or smelling of mildew. The spring confirmation matters just as much: cover removed before the first start, power restored, and the annual service booked, since most homeowners benefit from professional service at least once a year, usually in spring before peak cooling season. A unit that starts the summer clean and dry has had the winter handled correctly, whichever of the two good options was chosen.

FAQ

Will snow on the unit damage it? Snow load by itself is what the cabinet is built for. The damage cases involve ice and debris falling onto the unit from a roof or tree, and meltwater refreezing around the fan, which is why the protection, when it is used at all, goes on the top.

Does a cover make the unit last longer? A clean, debris-free unit does run more efficiently in summer, and a top cover can help keep it that way. A full wrap works in the opposite direction, holding moisture against the components it was meant to protect.

What if the unit is already wrapped? Unwrap it. Clear out anything that has collected inside, let the cabinet dry, and replace the wrap with a top-only cover if the location genuinely needs one, or with nothing at all.
