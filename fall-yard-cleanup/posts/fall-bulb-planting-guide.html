Planting spring bulbs in fall: timing, depth, spacing, and critter protection

Bulbs · 9 min read

Spring bulbs are a fall job with a spring payoff, and almost every failure traces back to one of three numbers: planted too early, planted at the wrong depth, or planted where the squirrels can reach them. The good news is that all three are measurable. University extension guidance gives exact depths and spacings per species, a soil-temperature trigger for timing that works in any region, and a clear ranking of critter defenses. This guide pulls those numbers together so the bulbs go in once, correctly, instead of becoming an expensive squirrel buffet.

Why fall: roots first, cold second

Spring-flowering bulbs are planted in fall for two reasons. First, the still-warm soil lets them build a root system before winter, and bulbs with good roots going into winter bloom far better than bulbs planted late or held over. Second, these bulbs must be exposed to a sustained period of cold temperatures in order to bloom at all — winter is not a hazard to survive but a requirement to satisfy. That is also why bulbs left in the bag are a write-off: unplanted tulips and other spring bulbs shrivel over winter, and bulbs planted in spring usually grow poorly and often fail to bloom because they missed both the root-building window and the cold.

Start with the bulbs themselves. Choose firm, solid bulbs and reject anything shriveled, lightweight, discolored by mold, or soft to the touch. Size matters more than variety marketing: the bigger the bulb, the better the flower display, because the flower is already formed inside and a larger bulb carries more stored energy to push it out.

Timing: soil temperature and the frost count-back

Calendar months are a rough guide at best, because the right week in one region is a month off in another. Two checks travel anywhere. The first is the count-back: aim to plant about six to eight weeks before the first expected hard frost, so roots have time to develop before the ground freezes. The second is a soil thermometer: start planting once soil temperature drops to around 60°F (15°C) or cooler.

Planting too early is a real failure mode, not a theoretical one. Iowa State University Extension notes that planting in September, before soil temperatures have cooled, may cause some bulbs such as tulips to emerge in the fall — growth that wastes the bulb's stored energy heading into winter. October is the ideal planting month in Iowa for exactly this reason. Running late is more forgiving than running early: spring-flowering bulbs can be planted as late as December if the ground remains unfrozen, and when planting a mix, the smaller bulbs such as crocus, scilla, and snowdrop go in earlier while daffodils, hyacinths, and tulips can go in later.

The verified depth and spacing table

The rule of thumb — plant at a depth of two to three times the bulb's height or diameter — is a decent memory aid and a poor planting plan, because the published numbers conflict. One popular online table lists daffodils at 4 to 6 inches deep; the Iowa State University Extension table lists them at 6 to 8 inches. The extension table below is the one to plant by, and it comes with the detail most guides omit: depth is measured from the base of the bulb to the soil surface, so the hole is dug to the full listed depth.

Two adjustments refine the table. Soil type shifts depth: in heavy clay, plant a little shallower than the table; in light, sandy soil, plant at the deeper end — and no bulb should be planted less than 2 inches deep regardless. Arrangement shifts impact: large bulbs such as daffodils and tulips should go in clusters of five or more of one variety, while small bulbs like grape hyacinths and crocuses look best in drifts of at least 25. Single bulbs in a row read as accidents; clusters read as a display.

The planting procedure, step by step

  1. Check the timing triggers. Confirm soil is at or below about 60°F and roughly six to eight weeks remain before the ground typically freezes. If the soil is still warm, wait — early planting risks fall emergence.
  2. Pick a well-drained, sunny spot. Nearly all bulbs require well-drained soil; poorly drained or wet soil causes decline and rot. Full or part sun suits most bulbs, and early bloomers can go under high-branched deciduous trees because they flower before the tree leafs out.
  3. Sort and inspect the bulbs. Plant only firm, solid bulbs. Discard shriveled, moldy, or soft ones before they reach the soil.
  4. Dig to the table depth. For a cluster or drift, excavate the whole planting area to the depth for the largest bulb rather than digging individual holes. Measure the first holes until the depth is consistent.
  5. Feed only if feeding at all. Fertilizer at planting is not typically necessary; it matters more for the following year's bulb than for the coming spring's flower. If it is used, extension rates are an all-purpose 5-10-5 at 2 to 3 pounds per 100 square feet worked into the soil before planting, or bonemeal at 3 to 4 pounds per 100 square feet — about one tablespoon worked into the bottom of an individual hole.
  6. Set each bulb pointed end up. The flat base sits down, the point faces up. If a bulb's top genuinely cannot be told, plant it on its side — the bulb will find its way up and bloom normally.
  7. Backfill, firm, and water. Cover the bulbs, tamp the soil down firmly with your hands, and water the planting area to settle soil around the bulbs and start root growth. Firm, watered soil is also harder for squirrels to dig through. After that first watering, water again only if the fall turns dry — overwatering rots bulbs.
  8. Add critter protection where pressure is high. See the ranking below; in squirrel-heavy yards this step is not optional for tulips.
  9. Mulch and mark. Apply 2 to 3 inches of mulch such as shredded leaves or bark to hold soil temperature steady and conserve moisture, and mark the planting spots so spring digging does not slice through the beds.

Critter protection, ranked by what works

Tulips and crocuses are highly desirable to squirrels, chipmunks, voles, rabbits, and deer — animals dig and eat newly planted bulbs in fall and browse the foliage and buds in spring. The defenses rank clearly:

  • First choice: exclusion. Iowa State Extension is direct that the best way to stop animals eating bulbs is to exclude them. Roll hardware cloth or chicken wire across the soil surface after planting and pin it down with landscape staples or bricks. Remove it promptly in early spring, before the foliage begins to emerge. Correct planting depth plus firming and watering the soil adds a second layer of difficulty for digging squirrels.
  • Second choice: plant what they skip. Squirrels tend to leave daffodils, hyacinths, and alliums alone, while tulips and crocuses are their favorites. In a high-pressure yard, shifting the plan toward the avoided species — or interplanting them around the tulips — removes the attraction instead of fighting it.
  • Last choice: repellents. Scent and taste repellents can discourage browsing, but extension guidance rates them typically not very effective at preventing damage on their own, and they need reapplication, especially after rain. Treat them as a supplement to mesh, never a substitute.

What success looks like

Success is checkable at three points. At planting: every cluster sits at the table depth measured from the bulb's base, soil is firmed and watered in, and any mesh lies flat and pinned, not tented. Through fall: no green shoots appear above the soil before winter — fall emergence means the planting went in while the soil was still too warm. In spring: foliage pushes through evenly across each cluster at roughly the same time, with no dug-out holes, scattered bulb skins, or missing patches marking where the squirrels got in first.

The one number to remember. Depth is measured from the base of the bulb to the surface, and the big three — tulips, daffodils, hyacinths — all sit 6 to 8 inches down. A bulb planter or trowel marked in inches turns the table above from advice into a measurement.

Common mistakes

  • Planting by the calendar in warm soil. September planting in uncooled soil can push tulips into fall growth. The soil thermometer, not the month, opens the window.
  • Using one depth for every bulb. A crocus buried at tulip depth and a tulip planted at crocus depth both underperform. The species table exists because bulb size varies by a factor of two or more.
  • Planting in soil that stays wet. Drainage is the non-negotiable. Bulbs in soggy soil rot in the ground over winter, and no depth or fertilizer choice compensates.
  • Keeping the mesh on too long. Wire left in place into spring traps and distorts emerging foliage. It comes off as soon as foliage starts to emerge.
  • Holding bulbs over for next year. Unplanted spring bulbs shrivel and die over winter. A marginal planting in unfrozen December ground beats a bag in the garage every time.

FAQ

Is fertilizer at planting time required? No. Extension guidance treats it as optional — it supports the following year's bulb more than the coming flower. If used, keep to the rates in step 5 rather than piling product into each hole.

What if a bulb goes in upside down? Usually nothing fatal. A bulb that cannot be oriented can be planted on its side and will still grow and bloom; even fully inverted bulbs often right themselves at the cost of some stored energy. Depth matters more than perfect orientation.

Can bulbs go under trees? Yes, under high-branched deciduous trees especially. Early bloomers flower and gather light before the canopy fills in, then tolerate the light shade that follows.

How late is too late? The limit is the ground itself, not a date: bulbs can be planted as late as December where the ground remains unfrozen. Once the soil cannot be dug, the window has closed.
