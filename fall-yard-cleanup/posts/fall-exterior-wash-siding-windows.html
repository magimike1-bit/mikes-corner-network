The Fall Exterior Wash: Soft-Wash the Siding, Skip the Pressure Washer, Read the Seals

Siding & windows · 7 min read

Fall exterior cleaning has a public argument running through it. One side — the seasonal checklists and washing services — promotes a good pressure wash before winter. The other side — the siding manufacturers and the people who repair water damage — says that is close to the worst move on the calendar. Both sides agree the house should be cleaned before winter. They disagree about the weapon. This guide settles it with the sources on the table, then runs the wash as a procedure with a second job hidden inside it: the last inspection of the seals before the cold arrives.

The case for cleaning at all

Start with the agreement. A season of spring and summer leaves the exterior carrying dirt, pollen, algae, mildew, mould, and organic debris, and the fall-washing case is that removing it before winter protects the surfaces — growth left in place can harden over winter and grow harder to remove — and, just as importantly, reveals the house. Once the grime is gone, peeling paint, deteriorated caulk, and damaged siding become visible in time to act before the weather does. Windows carry the same double value: a final thorough clean before winter removes summer's contaminants and admits more natural light through the short days, and the act of cleaning is how cracks and weaknesses in window seals get found — the ones that become January drafts.

The method the manufacturers prescribe

LP SmartSide's and Nichiha's care guidance, as summarised in fall siding-maintenance guidance, is specific and unglamorous: mild household detergent and water, applied with a soft-bristle brush, sponge, or cloth, rinsed gently with a standard garden hose. Harsh chemicals and abrasive tools are out. High-pressure washers are out — explicitly, because they can damage siding surfaces and finishes. Nichiha's interval for its fiber cement siding is approximately every twelve months, which makes the fall wash the annual wash, not an extra one. The same guidance folds the wash into the moisture system around it: keep gutters functioning so water does not overflow onto or behind the siding, make sure downspouts carry water away from the house, and keep mulch, soil, and landscaping from piling against the siding at grade.

Why late-fall pressure washing is the risky version

The warning, carried by Men's Journal and The Spun from restoration practice, is mechanical. A pressure washer drives water with enough force to push it into small gaps and joints — up behind lap siding, past trim, and into window and door flashing. Porous materials like brick and mortar absorb that water, and trapped moisture can take a long time to evaporate. When temperatures drop below freezing, the water expands, breaking and loosening the surrounding material and accelerating rot. The quoted expert — John Blackburn of ARS Restoration Services — puts the Pacific Northwest version plainly: late in fall the siding often never fully dries before the cold, wet season, and water in the wall cavity can sit against sheathing and framing for weeks, which is where rot and mould are found months later. None of this says pressure washing is never defensible; it says the margin for it collapses as the drying days shorten, which is precisely when the fall-cleaning urge strikes.

The soft-wash procedure

  1. Pick the weather window first. A dry stretch with several days of sun in the forecast and temperatures safely above freezing, so surfaces dry properly. Check the gaps and seals before any water comes out — washing a house with failed caulk is irrigating the walls.
  2. Clear the water system. Gutters cleaned (the fall rule: before leaf drop finishes, and again after if needed), downspouts confirmed to discharge away from the house, mulch and soil pulled back from the siding line.
  3. Wash top-down, section by section. Mild detergent solution, soft brush or cloth, working downward; rinse gently with the garden hose, spraying downward — never up under the laps — and stay clear of windows, vents, and light fixtures with any direct stream.
  4. Clean the windows as an inspection. Wash inside the same circuit; at each window, look at the seal and the caulk line while the glass is clean. Cracks, gaps, and deteriorated caulk go on the fix list the same day.
  5. Walk the dry house. After drying, one slow circuit: peeling paint, stained or damp patches, siding pulling away at corners and rooflines — the problems the wash was partly for revealing.

What success looks like: siding clean without a single high-pressure pass, every window seal either sound or listed for caulk, gutters empty and discharging away, and the whole job finished with drying days to spare before the first freeze. If the forecast turns, the wash waits — a dirty house survives winter better than a wet one.

Where the ladder fits — and where it doesn't

The soft-wash method has one more advantage the checklists rarely credit: most of it happens from the ground. A garden hose, a soft brush on an extension handle, and a downward rinse reach the siding that actually carries the season's grime, and the window circuit's ground floor needs no elevation at all. The upper storeys are where judgement replaces enthusiasm. This site's standing rule for ladder work applies without exception: if reaching a section means an unstable footing, an overreach, or a wet ladder on a cold morning, that section belongs to a professional with the right equipment — or it waits, honestly logged, for a better day. A fall wash is a maintenance task measured in years of siding life; it is not improved by urgency, and it is the only task in this guide whose worst-case outcome is worse than skipping it entirely. Clean what can be cleaned standing on the ground, list what cannot, and let the list — not the ladder — do the climbing.

FAQ

So is any fall pressure washing acceptable? The sources draw the line at method and timing rather than issuing a ban: services promote it, manufacturers warn against high pressure on their products, and restoration experience says late and wet is the dangerous combination. A homeowner set on it should stay early, stay low-pressure, spray downward — and know the manufacturers' position before choosing.

How often does siding actually need this? About annually on the fiber-cement guidance — which is why folding the seal inspection into the same circuit costs nothing extra and buys the winter-draft list while there is still weather to fix it in.

What about walkways and driveways? Hard surfaces carry none of the siding risks and collect the slip hazards; the seasonal checklists put them in the same fall circuit. The caution in this guide is aimed at walls, trim, and flashing — the places water can be driven in and trapped.
