Fall fence, gate, and deck check before snow and frost heave

Yard structures · 6 min read

The common assumption is that winter damages fences and decks. More often, fall neglect does — winter just collects the bill. A loose post, a gate that already sags, water pooled at a post base: none of these start in January. They start as small, fixable problems in October, and frozen ground is what turns them into spring projects.

Mistake one: checking the post, not the water at its base

Most fence checks push on posts and stop there. The driver underneath the problem gets one look at most: standing water. Water that pools around a post base and then freezes exerts significant heaving force on that post. The post that leans a little more every spring is often a drainage problem wearing a fence costume.

So the walk starts low. At every post base, look for ground that holds water, soil piled so it directs runoff toward the post, and blocked drainage. The fix named in the guidance is grading: clear drainage channels and grade soil away from post bases, so water leaves instead of collecting where it can freeze.

Mistake two: finding the problem in March

Timing is the second error, and it has a hard edge. Complete the structural re-inspection in September and October: an issue found before the freeze can be corrected, while the same issue found in March waits for thaw. Frozen ground does not negotiate. Post work, grading, and footing repairs all need workable soil, which is why this single walk covers fence, gate, and deck together — there may only be a few workable weekends left.

Mistake three: leaving a loose gate to become a dropped gate

Gates fail gradually, then all at once. Walk the perimeter and tighten fasteners, and adjust sagging gate hinges in fall, because a loose gate entering winter will be a dropped gate by spring. Check the full swing: a gate that sags, drags, or does not latch is telling you its hinges or its post have already moved.

Finish the gate work with oil. Oil gate hinges and latches so they work smoothly during winter — a latch that barely catches in October is one that will not catch at all in February.

The one walk, in order

  1. Drainage at every post base. Look for standing water, blocked channels, and soil graded toward the post. Grade soil away from post bases where water collects. Success looks like no post sitting in a dip that will hold water.
  2. The push test on posts and panels. Check for loose or leaning posts, cracked or missing boards, and loose panels. Clear vegetation growing against the fence and anything holding the fence in contact with wet soil. Success looks like every post either firm, or marked for repair before freeze.
  3. Clear the fence line. Keep the area near the fence clear of debris, leaves, and dead plants, which trap moisture and rot wood. Success looks like a fence line you can see the base of, end to end.
  4. Swing every gate. Open and close each gate fully. Note sag, drag, a latch that misses, and rusted hinges. Tighten fasteners, adjust the hinges, then oil hinges and latches. Success looks like a gate that swings clear, latches on its own weight, and moves quietly.
  5. Walk the deck boards. Look for loose, cracked, or warped boards and soft areas underfoot. Success looks like suspect boards marked, not just noticed.
  6. Push the rails, check the stairs. Test railings, stairs, and balusters for looseness, and look for protruding nails, loose screws, and rusted fasteners. Note spots where water collects and any worn finish. Success looks like rails that do not move and a fastener list written down.

What success looks like

No post base sits in pooled water. Every post is firm or booked for repair while the ground is still workable. Gates swing, clear the ground, and latch. Deck boards, rails, and stairs show no unrecorded looseness, and the worn-finish and rusted-fastener notes exist on paper — ready to act on, in the order below.

One question the search results ask — how does frost heave damage fence posts? — now has its answer in the order of the walk: water collects at the base, freezes there, and exerts heaving force on the post. Remove the water in fall and the mechanism loses its fuel.
