The fall sump pump check: test it before the ground freezes

Basement · 8 min read · Published November 4, 2026

2 to 4 times a year. That is how often FloodSafe Saskatchewan says a sump pump should be inspected and tested, and the schedule names this exact window: once in the fall, before the ground freezes, and again early in the spring. Early November is that fall slot in most of Canada. The check below takes one bucket of water, one walk outside, and one unplugged cord, and it catches the failures that otherwise announce themselves during a January thaw.

The mistakes that flood basements in January

Start with what goes wrong, because each item on the list maps to a step in the check that follows.

  • The pump was never tested, only assumed. A pump sits unused for months at a time. A float cable tangled against the pit wall, a plug pulled to run another device and never put back, or a switch stuck fast all leave a pump that looks installed and does nothing.
  • The motor ran, so the job was called done. A running motor is not protection. Water has to leave the pit and show up outside. A failed check valve recycles the same water back into the pit, and a blocked discharge line sends it nowhere at all.
  • The discharge outlet was never checked outside. Leaves, dirt, and debris clog the pipe end, and a line that dumps water beside the foundation feeds the same water back toward the house.
  • The backup was trusted without being run. An outage test that never happens is a hope, not a backup. The battery system has to carry a bucket test on its own before it can be counted on during a storm.
  • A frozen line got the wrong fix. A blow torch on a frozen discharge pipe, or antifreeze poured into the pump, are both named as never do items in the provincial guidance. A portable heater is the listed thaw method.

Tools and materials

  • Five gallon pail, filled with water (more than one pail may be needed to trigger the pump)
  • Flashlight, for reading the pit and the float
  • Gloves, for clearing debris from the pit and the outdoor outlet
  • A second person or a phone, so the discharge outside can be watched while the pump runs

Difficulty: easy for the test itself. Time: about 20 minutes. Hire a pro line: a pump that has power but will not start goes to a licensed plumber, and any thaw job that feels uncertain goes to a professional contractor. Both lines come straight from the guidance cited below.

The fall check, step by step

  1. Clear the pit and read the setup. Lift the pit cover and clear debris from around the pump. Confirm the pump sits on a solid, steady surface, a flat brick is the example given, and not on gravel or silt that can be drawn in and damage the motor. Check the plug: the pump belongs directly in a GFCI protected outlet, never on an extension cord, and never left unplugged after the outlet was borrowed. Check: the pit is clear, the pump sits steady, and the area in front of the pit is not piled over with storage.
  2. Lift the float. Raise the float valve by hand. A humming noise means the pump has started. If nothing happens, check the circuit breaker. If the pump has power and still fails to start, stop here and call a licensed plumber. Check: the pump starts on the float alone, without help.
  3. Run the bucket test. Slowly pour the five gallon pail into the pit. The float should rise, the pump should start, and the water level should fall steadily until the float drops and the motor stops. Listen while it runs. Grinding or rattling is a warning sign, not background noise. Check: one full cycle, start to stop, with the water level visibly dropping.
  4. Verify the discharge outside. While the pump runs, go outside and watch water flow out of the discharge pipe. The outlet should be clear of dirt and debris and should direct water a minimum of two metres, about 6.5 feet, away from the foundation. Back at the pit, watch the water after shutoff: if it pours back in, the check valve is letting discharge recycle instead of leaving. Check: water seen outside, at distance from the wall, and no rush back into the pit at shutoff.
  5. Test the backup with the primary unplugged. Same day, same bucket. Unplug the main pump, repeat the bucket test, and confirm the battery system carries the load on its own. A high water alarm, where fitted, earns its place here too: it warns that the main pump has failed before the floor is wet. Check: the pit drains on battery power alone, and the primary is plugged back in afterward.
  6. Clean the backwater valve and reset the space. The provincial schedule pairs the fall pump test with cleaning out the backwater valve. Then put the cover back, and leave the pit accessible. A sump pit buried under stored boxes cannot be reached quickly on the one night it matters. Check: cover on, access clear, test date noted.

How long should a pump last? Reconciling the ranges

The published numbers disagree, and the disagreement is useful once it is sorted. FloodSafe Saskatchewan states that sump pumps reliably last between 7 and 10 years. A Niagara home inspection source gives a lower average, anywhere from 3 to 7 years, and then notes that pumps in busy pits typically last 7 to 10 years. Read together, the ranges describe duty, not contradiction: a pump in a dry pit that runs rarely is judged against the lower band, a pump in a wet pit against the upper one, and regular testing and maintenance is the factor the provincial guide credits for reaching the long end.

Backup power: what the numbers actually promise

A battery backup exists for one scenario: the storm that brings the water also cuts the power. The provincial figures put a fully charged backup battery at roughly 5 to 7 hours of continuous pumping, or 1 to 3 days of intermittent pumping, with run time varying by battery size and usage. That spread is the honest planning number. A backup is a bridge through an outage, measured in hours of hard running, not a second primary pump. It is also why step 5 above is a load test and not a glance at a charger light: the only figure that counts is the pit draining on battery alone.

One more symptom belongs in this section. If the pump runs but the water level does not drop, the usual causes are a failed check valve, a blocked discharge line, or a pump that has lost its prime. None of those fix themselves over winter, and the instruction attached to that symptom is to have the system serviced before the wet season rather than after it.

What success looks like

Success is observable, item by item. The float lifts and the pump starts. A five gallon pail drains in one steady cycle with no grinding or rattling. Water is seen leaving the discharge outside, at least two metres from the foundation, and it does not rush back into the pit at shutoff. The backup drains the pit with the primary unplugged, and the primary is plugged back into its GFCI outlet when the test ends. The backwater valve is cleaned, the cover is back on, and the pit is reachable without moving storage. A pump that passes all six points in November has earned the winter. A pump that fails any one of them has given fair warning, in the cheapest month to act on it.

, Tom Reilly, Home & DIY Writer
