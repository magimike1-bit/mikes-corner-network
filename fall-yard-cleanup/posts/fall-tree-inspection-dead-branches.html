Fall tree inspection: dead branches to deal with before winter storms

Trees & shrubs · 6 min read

Which dead branch in your yard actually matters this fall? Not necessarily the biggest one, and not necessarily the one on the biggest tree. The branch that matters most is the one hanging over something — a roof, a driveway, a walk, a play area. A dead limb over an empty corner of the back lawn and the same limb over the house are two different problems, and fall inspection works better when it starts with that difference.

Start with the target, then look at the tree

Work through the yard in target order. Begin with limbs over roofs, drives, walks, and play areas, then move to limbs over fences, sheds, and garden beds, and finish with trees that stand over open ground. That order decides the urgency before any cutting is discussed. A hazard found early in the list gets dealt with before winter storms; the same hazard at the end of the list may only need watching.

This is a ground-level job. No climbing, no ladder work in the canopy. The inspection uses eyes, a slow walk, and a second look from a different angle when a limb is hard to read against the sky.

Symptom one: deadwood in the canopy

Look up first, for dead, broken, or hanging limbs. A branch that stayed bare through the summer while the rest of the tree leafed out, that has peeling bark, or that sounds hollow when tapped is likely dead and should be removed. Dead or brittle branches belong on the inspection list wherever they sit — the target underneath them sets the order, not whether they count.

Hanging limbs deserve separate attention. A limb that has broken and lodged in the canopy is not resting safely; it has already failed once. Treat a hanging limb as a keep-people-away problem until it has been assessed, especially where it hangs over a route people use every day.

Symptom two: trouble in the trunk

From the canopy, move down to the trunk. The warning signs named in fall checklists are consistent: cracks in the trunk or major limbs, cavities, oozing or discoloured bark, hollow or soft areas, and insect bore holes. Any one of them changes the conversation from branch removal to the health of the whole tree.

A deep crack is the clearest example. A cracked trunk or major limb is a structural problem, not a pruning problem, and it sits on the professional side of the line drawn below.

Symptom three: lean and movement at the base

Finish the walk at the root flare and the ground around it. A leaning tree is on every inspection list, and a new lean matters more than an old, settled one. Visible root damage belongs in the same category: the base is what holds the tree up, and problems there are not solved by removing a branch or two from the top.

The ground-level inspection, step by step

  1. List the targets first. Walk the property and note what sits under each tree: roof, driveway, walk, play area, fence, or open ground. Success looks like a written order of trees to check, highest-target first.
  2. Look up. For each tree in order, scan the canopy for dead, broken, or hanging limbs, and note branches that stayed bare through summer or have peeling bark. Success looks like every suspect limb recorded with the target beneath it.
  3. Check the trunk. Circle the trunk looking for cracks, cavities, lean, oozing or discoloured bark, hollow or soft areas, and bore holes. Success looks like each sign noted, not explained away on the spot.
  4. Check the base. Look for visible root damage and any sign the tree has started to lean. Success looks like a base you have actually stood beside and examined, not one assessed from the porch.
  5. Sort every finding using the framework below. Nothing on the list stays unclassified. Success looks like each item marked cut, call, or monitor before you put the notebook away.

Cut from the ground, call an arborist, or monitor

  • Call a professional: large dead limbs near the home or power lines, a cracked or leaning tree, or visible root damage. Anything near utility lines belongs here without exception.
  • Keep people away and call for an assessment: a hanging limb, a deep crack, a new lean, or a hollow area — the shared rule across fall checklists.
  • Monitor: a dead branch over open ground, on a tree with a sound trunk and base and no other warning signs. Monitoring means it stays on the list for the next inspection, not that it is forgotten.

The boundary is plain: work that involves climbing, large limbs, lines, or a structurally suspect tree is professional work. Ground-level inspection is the homeowner's part, and it is the part that makes the professional's visit shorter and better aimed.

One question from the search results: how can you tell if a branch is dead?

Use the three signs together. The branch stayed bare through the summer, its bark is peeling, or it sounds hollow when tapped. One sign on its own is a reason to look closer; the combination is a reason to schedule its removal, in target order.

Timing: what fall is for, and what it is not for

Prune dead, cracked, or hanging limbs after leaf drop, when visibility improves and bare branches are easier to read. Limit fall cuts to safety and structure. Leave extensive shaping to late winter or early spring. Fall is the season to remove the hazards the inspection found — not to redesign the tree.

What success looks like

Every tree on the property has been walked, in target order. Each dead, cracked, or hanging limb is classified: removed, booked with a professional, or listed for monitoring with its target noted. Nothing hangs unexamined over a roof, driveway, walk, or play area, and nobody climbed anything to get there.

— Tom Reilly, Home & DIY Writer
