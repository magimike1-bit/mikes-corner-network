Sealing driveway cracks before the freeze: sort first, then fill

Driveway · 8 min read

Which crack are you looking at? That question decides everything about this job, because a hairline in concrete, a finger-width gap in asphalt, and a web of interconnected cracks that looks like alligator skin are three different problems wearing the same name. Two of them take an hour and a tube or bottle of filler. The third one eats filler and asks for more, because the failure is underneath. November is the last comfortable month to sort it out: water expands by roughly 9 percent as it turns to ice, and inside a confined crack that expansion pries the edges apart a little further with every freeze and thaw cycle of the winter.

Triage: read the crack before buying anything

Walk the driveway with a tape measure and sort every crack into one of three groups.

  • Group one, fill it. Isolated cracks with sound edges and no height difference between the two sides. On concrete, the minor, non-structural cracks are typically hairline fissures or cracks consistently less than 1/4 inch wide, with no sign of active growth or vertical displacement. These are the DIY cracks.
  • Group two, fill it with help. Wider or deeper single cracks, still with sound edges. These take filler too, but deep ones need backer rod first so the sealant sits at the right depth instead of sinking into a plug that fails early. The measurable rule from Sika Canada: sealant depth should be half the joint width, to a maximum depth of 13 mm (1/2 inch), and deeper cracks get backer rod before any sealant goes in.
  • Group three, do not fill it. Alligator cracking, a pattern of interconnected cracks resembling alligator skin, signals that the underlying structure of the pavement is failing. Sagging or deformation says the same about the base layers. On concrete, a crack that is actively widening over weeks or months, or one side heaved above the other, belongs to an engineer or foundation specialist, not a caulking gun. Filling group three is money spent decorating a structural problem.

Match the product to the surface

Surface and crackProduct classWhy
Asphalt, narrow to medium cracksCold-pour asphalt crack fillerThe homeowner format. It offers real protection against water, with one honest limitation: cold-pour material is less flexible than hot-pour sealant, cannot expand and contract with the pavement as well, and tends to fail sooner, cracking or peeling away over time
Asphalt, a driveway worth doing properlyHot-pour rubberized sealant, usually a contractor jobGreater flexibility across temperature swings and a longer service life, at the cost of heated application equipment
Concrete, horizontal cracks and jointsSelf-leveling polyurethane sealantIt flows into the crack and levels itself without tooling, forms a flexible joint, and stretches and compresses as the concrete moves through temperature swings. A rigid epoxy cannot accommodate that movement without cracking, which is why flexible sealant is the default for outdoor slabs

One product note for concrete, from Sika Canada's data for its SAKRETE self-leveling sealant: maximum joint width is 25 mm (1 inch), tack-free time is 4 hours, the sealant stays tacky for up to 24 hours, and it fully cures in 7 days, with foot and vehicle traffic allowed after 3 days. One 300 mL tube fills a channel roughly 6 mm wide by 13 mm deep by 3.6 m long, which is the number to plan quantities with. Whatever brand goes in the cart, the label on that tube is the specification that counts.

The temperature window, reconciled

The guidance conflicts because the products differ. One paving source puts crack sealing at pavement and air temperatures of at least 45°F. Another gives the ideal band as 40°F to 85°F, spring or fall, so the filler adheres and cures properly. Sika Canada specifies 4°C to 43°C for its concrete sealant. Read together, the floor under all three sits at about 4 to 7°C, and the deciding document is the label of the product in hand: if the forecast daytime high cannot clear the label's minimum with a dry surface, the job waits for spring rather than going down cold and peeling by February. Cold also matters for a second reason: at low temperatures filler can freeze before it bonds, and moisture or ice in the crack prevents adhesion outright.

The filling procedure

  1. Sort and measure. Assign every crack to group one, two, or three from the triage above and measure widths. Check: group three cracks are marked off the list, not filled on optimism.
  2. Clean the crack out. Remove all loose debris, dirt, grit, weeds, and organic growth with a screwdriver, wire brush, and blower. The crack must be clean and dry for the sealant to bond. Check: a fingertip run along the crack comes back dusty at worst, with no loose material and no dampness.
  3. Set the depth on group two cracks. Push backer rod into any crack deeper than half its width, so the finished sealant depth is half the width and no more than 13 mm. Check: the rod sits snug at a consistent depth along the crack.
  4. Fill from the bottom. On concrete, cut the tube nozzle to the bead width and squeeze the sealant into the bottom of the crack with a standard caulking gun, letting the self-leveling material settle. On asphalt, work the cold-pour filler into the crack in a steady bead, slightly overfilling to allow for settling. Check: the fill sits continuous, with no gaps, bubbles, or sections sunk below the surface.
  5. Respect the cure. Keep vehicles off the repair for the label's stated window; for the Sika product above, that is 3 days for traffic and 7 days to full cure. Check: no tire tracks, no filler picked up and tracked across the slab.

What success looks like

Success is checked in spring, not on the day of the job. The filled cracks should sit sealed and flexible after a winter of freeze and thaw, with edges still bonded and no peeling, no new parallel cracks opened beside the repairs, and no water ponding against the filled joints. Sealed cracks and joints block rainwater and melting snow from reaching the base beneath the slab, and they keep deicing salts out of the crack network, which is the entire point of doing this in November instead of admiring the damage in April.

When filling is the wrong spend

Three situations close this guide, because each one converts a cheap maintenance job into wasted money. Alligator cracking on asphalt usually requires removing the damaged asphalt and repairing or reinforcing the base before any sealing, so a driveway showing that pattern needs a paving assessment, not filler. A concrete crack that keeps widening, or a slab section that has heaved or sunk so the two sides sit at different heights, needs a specialist's diagnosis first. And a driveway at the end of its surface life, cracking everywhere at once, is a resurfacing conversation. Filling buys time on sound pavement. On failed pavement, it only buys filler.

, Tom Reilly, Home & DIY Writer
