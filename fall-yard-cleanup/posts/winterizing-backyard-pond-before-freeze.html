Winterizing a backyard pond before freeze-up in Canada: pumps, heaters, and fish

Winterizing · 9 min read

How deep is the pond — at its deepest point, measured, not guessed? Everything else in this job hangs off that one number. Depth decides whether fish can overwinter in the pond at all, and the Canadian sources do not agree on a single figure: one Waterloo, Ontario guide works in tiers starting at 75 cm, another source puts overwintering depth at around 120 cm, and a Newfoundland guide warns that a pond less than 3 feet deep will most likely freeze solid. The spread is real, and it tracks how much ice a region actually grows. Work through the tiers below with your own measurement before touching a pump or a heater.

Tier 1: 100 cm or deeper — fish stay, with a hole in the ice

At 100 cm (about 3 feet 3 inches) or more, a Waterloo-area guide says fish can hibernate outdoors, provided gas exchange is maintained. The deeper 120 cm figure from another Canadian source is the same advice with more margin — the extra depth is insurance for colder regions and thicker ice, not a contradiction of the 100 cm floor. The mechanism is consistent everywhere: ice forms on top, the water underneath does not freeze, and the fish wait out the winter in that bottom layer.

What kills fish in a deep pond is usually not the cold. More fish die from toxic gases trapped under the ice — produced by rotting vegetation — than die from freezing. That is why the open hole matters more than any heater specification: it lets toxic gases escape and oxygen enter, and a pond sealed over completely for more than a few days can suffocate its fish.

Tier 2: 75 to 100 cm — fish stay, but insulate

In the 75-to-100 cm band, the same Waterloo guide keeps the fish outdoors but adds protection: insulating material such as straw or styrofoam around and over the pond, with plywood over the top. The load-bearing detail is that the cover must not seal the pond completely — oxygen is still required, so the Tier 1 gas-exchange hole stays part of the plan. Insulation slows the freeze; it does not replace the opening.

Tier 3: under 75 cm — fish come indoors

Below 75 cm, the guidance is unambiguous: overwinter the fish indoors. The Newfoundland guide's warning points the same way — at less than 3 feet, most likely freeze solid; pond location, fish size, and fish numbers shift the odds, but not enough to bet fish on. Set up an aquarium or tank in a relatively cool spot, keep filtration and oxygenation running as needed, and feed moderately. When fish move in either direction, match the water temperatures by floating the transfer containers before release, so the change does not shock them. One Canadian retailer adds a standing caution for valuable or sentimental fish: if there is any doubt about safe overwintering in the pond, bring them in regardless of tier.

Keep the hole open: de-icer, aerator, or both

MethodHow it worksBest fit
Floating de-icer / pond heaterHeats a small area of surface so it stays ice-freeCold climates, where it must operate continually to hold the opening
Aerator / bubblerMoving water resists freezing and adds oxygen directlyModerate climates on its own; paired with a de-icer in colder ones
De-icer plus aeratorThe heater holds the hole; the bubbler moves water and oxygenDeep-pond Tier 1 setups in cold regions — the belt-and-suspenders combination

One placement rule is absolute: any pump or diffuser used for winter bubbling works near the surface, never on the pond bottom. The bottom layer's stable temperature is what the hibernating fish are depending on, and bubbling from the bottom stirs it away. In moderate climates, where ice forms thin and rarely, an aerating nozzle alone can keep the surface from freezing over, with an electric heater added where the cold deepens. If a hole does close over in a mild spell pattern, melt it open with a pot of hot water set on the ice. Never pound or hammer the ice open — the shock and vibration can kill the fish below it.

Pumps and filters: out, cleaned, stored by type

Unless a piece of equipment can genuinely run year-round, it comes out: pumps, filters, and fountain equipment all winter indoors. Ultraviolet lights come in before the first frost whether the pump stays in or not. Clean the pump thoroughly and run clean water through it for several minutes before storage. Drain any water lines that could crack when filled with ice.

Storage is where two flatly opposite instructions turn out to both be right — they are talking about different pumps. One source prescribes storing the removed pump in a bucket of distilled or purified water in a heated garage or basement, running it for a minute every few weeks. Another explains why: direct-drive pumps, which are not magnetic drive, need to stay in water so their seals stay moist, while magnetic-drive and asynchronous pumps can be stored in any dry place. Bucket for direct-drive, shelf for magnetic — check which type the pump is before choosing. One exception overrides the removal rule: if no heater is being used, a pump can stay in the pond to maintain the hole in the ice, as the hole-keeping device. And if a heater is used, that same source says the pump comes out first.

The waterfall decision

A running waterfall aerates and filters, and in a mild winter it can keep running. The failure mode is specific to prolonged cold: a waterfall can freeze into formations that do not return water to the pond, so the pump keeps pumping the pond's water out onto the ice and empties the pond. Expecting a prolonged cold stretch means shutting the waterfall circuit down — it is a judgement call made from the forecast, and the cost of guessing wrong is an empty pond in January.

Feeding: where the cutoff numbers actually agree

Three numbers circulate — stop feeding below 50°F, below 10°C, and below 8°C — and the first two are the same number: 50°F is 10°C. Two of the Canadian sources and a US retailer guide all land on that line: below it, fish are in or near torpor, metabolism has slowed considerably, and food they cannot digest only pollutes the water and feeds the gas problem under the ice. One Ontario guide feeds a little longer, through October while temperatures hold at 8°C, and resumes in spring only at 10°C and above. Treat 10°C as the hard stop and the 8°C figure as the outer edge, not the plan. Before the cutoff, the fall cleanout does the fish a favour nothing else can: remove leaves, decayed plant parts, and sludge; prune reed-type plants back to just above the water height (their stems aid gas exchange); lower hardy water plants below the freezing line, about 60 cm; fit a cover net so leaves land on it instead of in the water; and change about 30 per cent of the pond volume, conditioning the fresh water.

The procedure

  1. Measure the deepest point and pick the tier. Check: the number is measured with a marked pole or tape, and the tier from the sections above is written down.
  2. Clean out before freeze-up. Leaves, decayed plants, and sludge out; reeds pruned to just above the water; hardy plants lowered below about 60 cm; net fitted. Check: the bottom is visibly clearer and the net sits taut.
  3. Stop feeding at 10°C water temperature. Check: the decision is being made off a pond thermometer reading, not the air temperature on a weather app.
  4. Pull the equipment. Pump, filter, and UV units out; lines drained. Check: no hose or pipe section still holds water.
  5. Store the pump by its type. Direct-drive submerged in a bucket of water above freezing; magnetic-drive dry on a shelf. Check: the bucket setup has a note to run the pump a minute every few weeks.
  6. Install the hole-keeper. De-icer floating, aerator bubbling near the surface, or both. Check: from the bank, you can see open water where the device works and the bubbling is not coming from the bottom.
  7. Decide the waterfall from the forecast. Prolonged cold coming means off. Check: the pump circuit is either confirmed winter-safe or shut down — not left on a hope.

What success looks like

Success, checked on the coldest week of January, is unglamorous: a visible patch of open water that never fully closes, equipment stored dry or bucketed where it cannot freeze, and a pond whose bottom layer has not been stirred since November. The spring confirmation comes when water temperatures reach about 55°F (13°C): fish become active, filtration is re-established first, and only then does feeding resume. If fish appear near the surface gasping in spring, treat it as an oxygen emergency first and a feeding question later.
