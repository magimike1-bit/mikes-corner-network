Winterizing hoses, outdoor faucets, and sprinklers before the freeze

Winterizing · 9 min read

Every burst outdoor pipe tells the same story: water was left standing in a pipe, a hose, or a faucet body, the temperature dropped, and the water did what water does. The entire winterizing job is about denying it that chance — and the job changes depending on which kind of faucet is on the wall, because a standard hose bib and a frost-proof sillcock fail in different ways and are protected in different ways. This guide sorts the faucet types first, then gives one procedure that covers hoses, faucets, and sprinkler systems in a single pass.

What actually bursts, and why

Water expands by about nine percent when it freezes. In an open lake that expansion is harmless; in a closed pipe it has nowhere to go, so the ice pushes outward until the pipe wall gives. The deadline is not the first frost — a light frost can happen with air in the mid-30s and harms nothing. The deadline is the first hard freeze: a single night at 28°F or lower for several hours is enough to freeze standing water solid in an exposed pipe.

The nastiest version involves a hose left connected. Ice forms in the hose and the freeze travels back into the faucet and the pipe feeding it. The pipe then splits behind the wall, inside the house, where nobody sees it — the damage announces itself at the spring thaw, when the faucet is turned on and water pours into the wall cavity instead of out the spout. One hard freeze with a hose on the tap is enough to do it, and it applies to every faucet type, including the frost-proof kind.

Two faucet types, two different jobs

Before touching a valve, identify what is on the wall. The winterizing work is completely different:

A quick field check: a frost-proof sillcock has a noticeably longer body passing through the wall and often a larger, rounder handle and an anti-siphon cap on top. A standard bib is short, with the valve right at the wall face. When in doubt, do the full standard-faucet procedure — it harms nothing on a frost-proof unit, while skipping it on a standard bib is exactly how pipes split.

How a frost-proof sillcock works — and how a hose defeats it

The frost-proof design moves the shutoff point instead of insulating against the cold. A long stem runs from the outdoor handle through the wall — the barrel extends roughly 4 to 20 inches into the structure — and the actual valve seat sits at the inner end, inside the heated part of the house. Turn the handle off and the water stops there, in the warm zone. The water left in the barrel is no longer under pressure and drains out through the spout by gravity, leaving the cold section of pipe empty. No standing water, nothing to freeze. Most modern units add a vacuum breaker, which stops garden water from being siphoned back into the home's clean water supply.

Two conditions make the design work. The unit must be installed with a slight downward pitch toward the outside so the barrel actually drains, and the spout must be open to the air. A hose, splitter, timer, or shut-off nozzle left connected breaks the second condition: it traps water in the barrel, the barrel cannot drain, and the trapped water freezes and expands backward toward the valve seat — bursting the pipe inside the wall the design was built to protect. A steady drip is the other warning sign: a persistent drip from a frost-proof faucet points to a faulty internal seal or washer, which lets water keep flowing into the barrel all winter, where it can freeze and rupture even a correctly installed unit.

The winterizing procedure, step by step

  1. Disconnect every hose by the first frost. Hoses come off first, ahead of everything else: remove hoses, splitters, timers, and nozzles from every outdoor faucet, including frost-proof ones. This single step removes the main cause of burst faucets.
  2. Drain each hose completely. Lay the hose straight across the yard or driveway, remove any sprayer so water can flow freely, then lift one end and walk along it, working trapped water out of every low spot toward the open end.
  3. Store hoses under cover. Coil the drained hose and store it indoors — a garage, basement, or shed. If indoor storage is impossible, coil it off the ground in the most sheltered spot available.
  4. Close the interior shutoff for each standard faucet. Find the indoor valve that feeds the outdoor tap — usually in the basement, utility room, or crawl space on the same wall — and close it fully. Frost-proof sillcocks without a dedicated interior valve skip this and the next two steps.
  5. Open the outdoor faucet wide. Let all the water in the exposed section drain out. Then leave the faucet open for the rest of the cold season, so any small amount of water that remains or seeps in has room to expand instead of building pressure.
  6. Open the bleeder cap on the interior shutoff. Many shutoff valves have a small drain or bleeder cap on the side. Put a container underneath, open the cap, and let the water trapped between the shutoff and the outdoor faucet escape. Close the cap once dripping stops.
  7. Watch for drips. After draining, watch each outdoor faucet for a minute or two. A slow drip means water is still reaching the line — or, on a frost-proof unit, that its internal seal is failing — and a dripping line can build ice and freeze even when everything else was done correctly.
  8. Fit foam covers as a supplement. An insulating cover snug against the siding blocks wind and cold air at the connection point. It adds protection; it never replaces draining, and it is the main defense only on faucets that genuinely have no interior shutoff — in which case adding foam pipe insulation to the connected lines and planning a plumber-installed shutoff with a drain is the better long-term fix.

Sprinklers: drain or blow out, at the right pressure

An in-ground irrigation system holds far more water than a faucet, and which winterizing method applies depends on how the system was installed. Manual-drain systems have drain valves at the low points: shut the supply, open every drain valve, and open the test cocks on the backflow preventer. Automatic-drain systems empty themselves when pressure drops: shut the supply and run a zone briefly to release pressure. Most systems, though, need the blow-out method — compressed air pushing the water out zone by zone.

Pressure is where blow-outs go wrong, and published limits conflict, so use the conservative reconciliation. The manufacturer instruction for irrigation winterization caps air pressure at 50 psi and specifies a compressor delivering 10 to 25 cubic feet per minute (CFM) of air volume: high volume at low pressure clears lines, while a high-pressure, low-volume compressor is the wrong tool and can damage components. Some guides quote up to 80 psi for poly pipe; treat 50 psi as the working ceiling unless the system's own documentation says otherwise, and never blow against closed valves. Work one zone at a time, starting anywhere consistent, and run each zone only until the heads emit a fine mist instead of water — a couple of minutes per zone is typical. Wear eye protection throughout. If the available compressor cannot meet the volume specification, hire a contractor for this step rather than improvising.

Whichever method is used, finish at the backflow preventer. It sits above ground, holds water, and is the first component to freeze. Drain it, leave its test cocks open at a 45-degree angle, and wrap it if it is exposed.

What success looks like

Success is a set of physical checks, not a feeling. Every faucet: hose off, and on standard bibs the interior shutoff closed, the outdoor tap open, and the bleeder drained and closed again — with no drip appearing after a two-minute watch. Every hose: fully drained end to end and coiled under cover. The sprinkler system: controller off, supply closed, each zone blown or drained until only mist or nothing emerges, and the backflow preventer drained with its test cocks at 45 degrees.

Then run the spring check, because that is when hidden damage shows. The first time each outdoor faucet is used in spring, have a second person watch the inside of the wall behind it, or check the basement or crawl space below it, while the water runs. A pipe that split over winter often delivers water normally at the spout while leaking into the wall — a dry interior during that first run is the real confirmation that the winterizing worked.

Caught by a sudden freeze? Skip the full sequence and do the one step that matters most: disconnect the hoses now, open the outdoor faucets, and shut the irrigation controller and its supply valve. Then finish the full procedure on the next mild day.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving a hose on a frost-proof faucet. The most expensive mistake on this list. The sillcock cannot drain through a connected hose, so its freeze protection is exactly zero.
  • Closing the interior shutoff and stopping there. A closed shutoff with a closed outdoor faucet traps a full pipe section between them. The outdoor tap must be opened and the bleeder drained.
  • Closing the outdoor faucet after draining. Leave it open all winter. Residual water needs expansion room.
  • Blowing out sprinklers at hardware-store compressor pressure. Unregulated shop compressors can far exceed the 50 psi ceiling, and low air volume leaves water sitting in low spots. Volume clears lines; pressure breaks them.
  • Trusting a foam cover as the whole job. A cover buys a few degrees at the connection point. Draining is what prevents the burst.

FAQ

What if there is no interior shutoff valve? Disconnect all hoses, fit a foam cover snug and flush against the exterior wall, and wrap the connected lines with foam pipe insulation. Then treat adding an accessible shutoff and drain valve — a routine plumber job — as the permanent fix, because any water left in the line can still freeze in a deep cold snap.

Does a frost-proof sillcock need winterizing at all? Only one step, but it is mandatory: every hose and attachment comes off so the barrel can drain. It also needs to have been installed with a downward pitch; a unit that holds water when the hose is off was installed level or pitched the wrong way.

Why does the faucet drip after being winterized? On a standard bib, a drip means the interior shutoff is not sealing fully. On a frost-proof unit, a persistent drip points to a worn internal seal or washer. Either way the drip keeps feeding water into the exposed section and should be fixed, not ignored.

When exactly should all this happen? Hoses off by the first frost; faucets drained and sprinklers done before the first hard freeze — the first night forecast to sit at 28°F or lower for several hours. Work backward from that forecast, not from the calendar.
