Charades for family night, graded by whether the prompt can actually be acted

Party games · 8 min read

Charades is the oldest game on this site and the one most likely to collapse in the first ten minutes — not because the acting is bad, but because the prompts are. Most charades guides hand a family one giant word list and a two-sentence rules summary, then leave a seven-year-old to mime "camouflage" while the adults mime "Inception." The category tables that do exist, like the one at MPL Games, sort words into easy, medium, and hard columns — useful, but the sort is by vocabulary, not by whether a prompt can actually be acted by the person holding it. A word can be easy to know and nearly impossible to mime. This guide rebuilds charades for a mixed-age family table: prompts graded by actability, the standard signals taught before the first turn, and scoring that gives the other team exactly one steal instead of an argument.

The rules, stated completely once

The basic game, as MPL Games states it, is simple: one player acts out a word or phrase without speaking while the others guess within a time limit, and in team play the team that guesses the most words correctly wins. Party Games 4 Kids puts the standard frame at four or more players, ages five and up, with about twenty minutes per round. Everything else in this guide is the detail that keeps that simple game from stalling: what counts as a legal clue, how long a turn lasts, and what happens on a pass. Agree on all of it before the first card is drawn, because charades disputes are never really about the word — they are about a rule nobody stated.

  1. The actor draws a card and may look at it for a few seconds before the timer starts. Thinking time is not acting time.
  2. No speaking, no sounds, no mouthing the word, no spelling with fingers. Gestures, facial expressions, and pointing at objects in the room are legal.
  3. Only the actor's team guesses. A correct guess inside the limit scores one point. If time expires, the other team gets exactly one steal guess, worth one point.
  4. The actor may pass once per turn and draw a replacement card, at the cost of the clock continuing to run.
  5. Turns rotate through every player, including the youngest. Nobody sits out because a round looks hard; the prompt deck is graded so they do not have to.

Grade prompts by actability, not vocabulary

Here is the gap in the standard tables. MPL Games rates "meditate" as a medium action and "skydiving" as a hard one, which is right for vocabulary — but a child can mime skydiving in two seconds flat (arms out, falling) and no one of any age can mime "meditate" in a way that cannot also be "sleeping," "waiting," or "bored." Actability has its own logic: a prompt is actable when it has a big, distinctive body movement, a visible object to pretend to hold, or a famous silhouette. It is unactable when it is a state of mind, an abstract idea, or something whose mime looks identical to three other words.

PlayMime's themed list makes the same point from the other direction: its easy family categories are animals, actions like brushing teeth and riding a bike, and foods — all things with a body shape. Build your deck in three actability tiers. Tier one, for the youngest players: animals with a silhouette (penguin, kangaroo, elephant), two-handed actions (brushing teeth, baking cookies, flying a kite), and foods eaten in a distinctive way (pizza, ice cream, popcorn). Tier two, for school-age players: sports with equipment (archery, gymnastics, hockey), places acted by their activity (airport, museum, beach), and objects used in the hands (compass, backpack, telescope). Tier three, for teens and adults: multi-word phrases, movie titles the whole table knows, and the deliberately unfair tier — camouflage, hourglass, platypus — reserved for players who ask for it.

Teach the signals before the first turn

Half of every charades stall is the actor trying to communicate structure — how many words, which word, "you are close" — with no agreed language for it. Theresa's Reviews' elementary-school procedure states the classroom version of the rules cleanly: no talking or sounds, no lip movements hinting at the word, no spelling with fingers, no "sounds like" clues, and a visible timer of one to two minutes for younger children. What the classroom version has and family versions usually skip is a shared set of hand signals, agreed in advance:

  • Number of words: hold up that many fingers before starting.
  • Which word you are acting: hold up one finger for the first word, two for the second, then mime that word only.
  • "Sounds like": cup one hand around an ear — allowed only if your house rules permit sounds-like clues at all.
  • "You are close, keep going": roll both hands in a circle.
  • "Shorter" or "longer": hands close together or stretched apart, for when the guess is the right word in the wrong form.

Write the signals on one card and leave it on the table for the first two rounds. By round three nobody looks at it, which is exactly the point: the language has been learned.

Scoring and timing that end arguments

This comparison table sets out the three scoring models a family actually uses, with the argument each one prevents — and the one each one causes.

ModelHow it worksPreventsWatch out for
One point per word, one stealCorrect guess = 1; expired turn gives the other team a single steal guess for 1Endless guessing after the clockSteal guesses must be single — take the first one shouted
First to tenTeams race to ten points across rotating turnsGames that drift with no endingA runaway lead; add a mercy finish at a set round count
Whole-group, no teamsEveryone guesses; actor and correct guesser both scoreTeam imbalance with very young playersScorekeeping gets loose — appoint one scorekeeper

On timing: Theresa's Reviews uses one to two minutes for elementary players and the first team to ten points as the win condition, while PlayMime suggests keeping turns short at 60 to 90 seconds with one point per correct guess. For a family table, use 90 seconds as the default, drop to 60 for tier-three prompts, and give the youngest players their full two minutes without apology — the clock is a pacing tool, not a fairness tool.

A decision framework for the night itself

  1. Count the players and their ages honestly. Four or more with a spread of ages means teams, balanced by age rather than by family branch. Three players, or a wide age gap with a very young child, means the whole-group model.
  2. Grade the deck before anyone arrives. Deal each player prompts from their actability tier, not from one shared bowl. A single mixed bowl is where charades nights go to die.
  3. Set the ending before the start. First to ten, or a fixed number of rounds — either works, but "we will stop when it feels done" does not, because it never feels done to the team that is losing.

What success looks like: every player has acted at least twice, the youngest player has scored a point with their own mime rather than a pity rule, and the argument count for the evening is zero.

FAQ

Can a player who cannot read yet play? Yes, two ways: an adult whispers the prompt, as Party Games 4 Kids suggests for non-readers, or the deck for that player uses picture cards. Tier-one prompts — animals and two-handed actions — are chosen precisely so a whispered word is enough.

Are props allowed? Pointing at real objects in the room is legal under the standard rules; picking an object up and using it is where most house rules draw the line. Decide before the first turn and do not renegotiate mid-game.

What if a player draws a prompt they do not know? That is what the single pass is for: one redraw per turn, clock running. A player who does not know the word cannot mime it, and forcing the attempt teaches the table the wrong lesson about the game.
