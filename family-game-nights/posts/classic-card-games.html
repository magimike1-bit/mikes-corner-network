Card games every family should know

Family picks · 5 min read

A standard deck of cards is the greatest family game ever made. It costs almost nothing, fits in a drawer, works for two players or ten, and contains hundreds of games — most of which can be taught in a single sentence. If your family only ever learns what's on this page, you'll never again be stuck staring at each other on a rainy Sunday. Start with the easy ones, graduate to the strategic ones, and keep a deck in the kitchen drawer forever.

For the littlest players: Go Fish and War

Go Fish is the classic first card game. Deal everyone a handful of cards; take turns asking another player for a card you need to make a pair ("Do you have any sevens?"); if they don't, "Go fish" — draw from the deck. Pairs go face-up in front of you. Most pairs at the end wins. It teaches matching, memory ("I saw grandpa pick up a king"), and taking turns — and requires zero reading, so a five-year-old can play the real game, not a simplified version.

War is even simpler and gloriously pure luck: split the deck in half, both players flip a card, higher card takes both. Ties trigger a "war" — each side lays down extra cards and flips again. No decisions, no skill, no hurt feelings about losing. It's the game to teach a four-year-old so they feel included at the table while the older kids play something bigger.

Step up: Crazy Eights and Spoons

Crazy Eights is the grandparent of Uno: match the top card by suit or rank, eights are wild and let you change the suit, first to shed all their cards wins. It adds real decision-making — when to play the wild, when to hold it — while staying simple enough for a seven-year-old. Once kids outgrow Go Fish, this is the natural next game.

Spoons is the chaos option. Everyone passes cards around the circle trying to collect four of a kind; the first player to do it grabs a spoon from the middle — and then everyone dives for the spoons (one fewer spoon than players). It's fast, physical, and hysterically funny. One rule of experience: wooden spoons and polished tables don't mix; use plastic spoons, or play on the floor. Grandparents tend to be surprisingly ruthless at this game.

For older kids and adults: Rummy and Hearts

Rummy (the simple "Gin Rummy" style) teaches real card strategy: draw and discard to build matching sets and runs, and try to go out first. It's patient, thoughtful, and satisfying — a great game for a parent and a tween who've outgrown the loud games but still want table time together.

Hearts is the classic "avoid the bad cards" game: everyone plays one card per trick, and you don't want to take tricks containing hearts (or the queen of spades, which is the real villain). It introduces trick-taking, planning ahead, and the sweet agony of "shooting the moon" — deliberately taking all the bad cards so they count against everyone else. It's a family favorite precisely because it rewards being sneaky.

Solo and quiet: Solitaire

Klondike Solitaire — the one everyone knows from computers — is worth teaching as a real card game. It's the perfect rainy-afternoon activity for a kid, and it's secretly a math and planning teacher: sequencing, alternating colors, thinking two moves ahead. For a family twist, try racing solitaire: everyone plays their own solitaire deck simultaneously, and the first to finish wins. It sounds chaotic; it's actually delightful.

Keeping one deck fresh: twists and house rules

The genius of cards is that house rules keep old games new. Some family-tested twists:

  • Scoring series. Play best-of-five rounds and track wins on a notepad. A running tally turns a ten-minute card game into a season.
  • Rotate the dealer as teacher. Whoever deals explains one rule change they want to try ("in this round, queens are wild"). Kids love being in charge of the rules.
  • Two decks for big groups. Most of these games scale up if you shuffle two decks together — more players, same fun.

One deck, a table, and twenty minutes. That's all family game night has ever really needed.
