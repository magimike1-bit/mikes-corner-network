Escape-room games at home: Unlock!, EXIT, and Deckscape compared

Game night formats · 8 min read

A locked room in a box — and one purchase question first

Tabletop escape-room games compress a real escape room into a deck of cards and an hour: search scenes, combine objects, crack codes, beat the clock, all cooperatively. They have become a family-night staple because they produce the one thing competitive games cannot — a whole table solving as a single team, with a story's ending as the prize. But the boxes differ on an axis the best-of lists consistently bury, and it decides the value of the purchase before puzzle quality gets a vote: what is left of the game after you play it?

Unlock! — the Space Cowboys series that anchors the category — is built from cards and a companion app, and nothing in it is consumed. As one roundup puts it from experience, you can put the deck back in order and hand the game to another family: no writing on the cards, nothing ruined, nothing cut. EXIT: The Game, the other big series, takes the opposite design position: its puzzles run through a decoder wheel and physical components you are expected to mark, fold, and cut as solutions demand. Neither is a defect. They are two ownership models — a library book versus a theatre ticket — and a family buying for replay across siblings, cousins, and loans should know which one is in the cart.

The app question, and the tutorial that replaces the rulebook

Unlock!'s companion app is the category's dividing line, so be deliberate about it. The app provides the timer, checks the codes you enter, and dispenses clues when the table is stuck — in escape-room terms, it is the game master, and having one is why the box can teach itself. The box includes a ten-card tutorial that introduces the mechanics in play, with no rulebook reading required; the listing's phrase is that you learn in minutes and start solving. For a family night, that is a genuine structural advantage: the person who bought the game is not conscripted into a rules lecture while everyone's snacks go cold.

The counter-consideration is real, and it is preference, not quality: an app means a phone on the table, a battery, and a screen mediating the finale. EXIT and Deckscape answer with cardboard — a puzzle wheel, a self-contained deck. Groups that run game night as a screen-free ritual will feel the difference every session; groups that do not care will never notice it. Decide once, as a household policy, and the category sorts itself.

The loud-solver problem, and a hosting procedure that fixes it

Every format shares one failure mode, and the best review of Unlock! names it by praising the exception: a good design rewards teamwork 'without letting one loud player grab every clue.' Most boxes only partially solve this, so the host finishes the job. This procedure works with any of the three formats:

  1. Cards face up, centre table, always. Any card a player picks up goes back to the middle where all eyes can reach it. Information hoarding becomes physically impossible.
  2. The reader rotates. Whoever draws or reveals a card reads it aloud, in full, before anyone solves. Next reveal, next reader. Quiet players hold the pen of the story in turn.
  3. Propose-and-second for codes. Nobody enters a code or commits an answer alone: a solution needs a proposer and a seconder who can say why. This single rule converts the fastest solver from a soloist into a teacher, because their idea only counts once they have convinced one other person.
  4. Hints are a table vote. In app games the hint button is one tap away and one thumb can spend it. Agree that hints — and the score penalty they may carry — require a quick show of hands.
  5. Bank the win as a group. When the game ends, each player names the clue they cracked or the moment they unstuck the table. It takes ninety seconds and it is why the quiet ten-year-old asks to play again next week.

Success check: in the final third of the game, every player at the table has physically handled cards, read at least one aloud, and seconded or proposed at least one solution. If one player has done all three of those things for everyone, the procedure was skipped — run it next box and watch the same game play wider.

Which box first: the decision framework

Choose in this order. Ownership: if the game will rotate through siblings, cousins, or a lending shelf, a resettable format (Unlock!) is worth more per dollar no matter how good a one-and-done box is; if it is a single event — a birthday, a holiday night — EXIT's destructible puzzles are a legitimate spectacle and among the cheapest entries in the category. Youngest solver: under 10 means Unlock! Kids or wait; 10-11 means the Unlock! main line; 12+ unlocks Deckscape. Screen policy: app-as-game-master (Unlock!) versus cardboard-only (EXIT, Deckscape) is a household rule, not a review score. Group size: all three formats cover the family band, but a table of five or six should expect the hosting procedure above to matter more, not less — more solvers means more airtime to distribute, and cooperative games do not distribute it automatically.

FAQ

Can you replay an EXIT game? Not as designed — components are marked, folded, or cut during play. Treat it as a ticket: one group, one night, full spectacle. Unlock! is the resettable opposite and can be re-gifted after your group finishes it.

Do we need the app for Unlock!? The main line uses a companion app for clues, code checking, and the timer. Unlock! Kids requires no app, which is part of its design for younger players.

How hard are these for a first family night? The base Unlock! box is rated 10+ and teaches itself through a ten-card tutorial; its three scenarios vary in tone, so start with the gentlest. Expect to use hints on a first play — that is the system working, not the family failing.
