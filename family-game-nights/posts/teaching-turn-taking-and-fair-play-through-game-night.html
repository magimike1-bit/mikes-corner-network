Teaching Turn-Taking and Fair Play Through Game Night

Fair play & turn-taking · 6 min read

What actually teaches a child to wait for a turn — the rule explained at the start, or the forty small waits inside one game? The early-childhood guidance points to the waits. Board games are described as a natural way to practise turn-taking, rule-following, and respectful communication, and children learn to wait patiently, follow order, and respect rules through structured play. Game night is where that practice already lives: a real order to follow, real people to wait for, and a real result at the end. The trick is to run the evening like practice on purpose, instead of hoping the lesson absorbs itself between the snacks.

General information only — every child develops on their own timeline, and nothing here is developmental or medical advice.

A practice plan for one evening

Run the night in this order. Each step is small; the sequence is the point.

  1. Pick a game whose turns are measured in seconds. Simple race and matching games — the Candy Land class — and straightforward games like Connect 4 are the beginner trainers named in the turn-taking sources, because a turn ends almost as soon as it starts and the wait for the next one is short.
  2. Say the order out loud before the first turn. Who starts, which direction play moves, and what happens on a turn. Following order is one of the skills structured play is credited with teaching, and it teaches faster when the order is spoken, not assumed.
  3. Make the wait visible for the youngest players. A visual timer of 2–3 minutes helps preschoolers see how long a wait is. Set it beside a young child's place during other players' turns, and let it run out rather than narrating the seconds.
  4. Praise the wait, specifically, the moment it happens. Not "good job" in general — name the behaviour: "You waited while two whole turns went by." The guidance on turn-taking practice is explicit that specific praise for patient waiting is what reinforces it.
  5. Lengthen the turns as the skill holds. Keep initial turns very short and extend them gradually as skills improve: a matching game this month, a simple card game next, a longer game when the short waits stop being work.
  6. Close with the result, whatever it was. The winner is named, the losers are named kindly, and the game goes back in the box. No quiet re-deal to soften the ending — the ending is part of the practice.

What success looks like: a child who can say whose turn is next without being asked, waits through it without leaving the table, and hears the result — win or lose — while staying in the room for the box-up.

Choosing how much rule to enforce

The fair-play question parents actually argue about at the table is not whether rules matter; it is how much rule a given child can carry tonight. Use the child's hold on the wait — not their birthday alone — as the gauge:

Cooperative games belong in this ladder too. Board games, cooperative puzzles, and simple card games are all named as natural turn-taking practice, and a cooperative game lets a child practise the waiting and the order on a night when practising losing as well would be one lesson too many.

Words for the moment a child loses

The sources agree on the conduct — model phrases such as "thanks for playing with me" and "I'll try again next round" are the examples given — but a child who has just lost badly needs to hear the words from a person, in the moment, before they can say them back. Three lines cover most losing moments at a family table:

  • For the loss itself: "That one stings. You played the whole game and it went the other way at the end." Naming the sting without fixing it is what makes the model phrases believable later.
  • For the winner to say: "Thanks for playing with me." Said by the winner, to the loser, every time — it is much easier to lose to someone who says it.
  • For the loser's reply, offered not required: "I'll try again next round." If the child cannot say it yet, the parent says it on their behalf once, lightly, and lets the box-up carry the rest.

And the parent models the loss first. The first time an adult at the table loses a game they were winning, that is the teaching moment the whole plan exists for: lose at full volume of politeness — the thanks, the "next round," the hands helping pack away the game that just beat you. Children learn to wait patiently and respect rules through structured play, and the structure includes watching how the biggest person at the table handles the worst result of the night.

None of this needs a special purchase or a special night. It needs a short game, a spoken order, praise that names the wait, and a result that is allowed to stand. Run that loop weekly and the fairness stops being a lecture, because the table has practised it forty turns at a time.
