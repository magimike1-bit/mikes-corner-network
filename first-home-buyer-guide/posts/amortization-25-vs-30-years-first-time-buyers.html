Amortization 25 vs 30 years for first-time buyers

Mortgages · 7 min read

Since late 2024, a Canadian first-time buyer with less than 20% down can choose a 30-year amortization on an insured mortgage — five extra years that lower the monthly payment and raise the total interest. Most comparisons stop at those two facts, stated over the full life of the loan. But almost nobody keeps a first mortgage for its full life: terms renew every few years, homes get sold, loans get refinanced. Priced over the first term instead — and paired with the catch-up payment that can erase the difference — the 30-year choice looks less like a permanent cost and more like a starting point with an exit.

Who can actually choose 30 years, as of the December 2024 rules

Eligibility changed twice in 2024, and older guides still state the first version. An initial measure effective August 1, 2024 allowed 30-year insured amortizations only for first-time homebuyers purchasing newly built homes. The Department of Finance Canada then expanded the rules effective December 15, 2024: eligibility for 30-year insured amortizations extends to all first-time homebuyers and to all buyers of new builds, and the insured mortgage price cap rose at the same time from $1 million to $1.5 million. So a first-time buyer purchasing a resale home can now use 30 years — a point some top search results still get wrong by requiring a new build for everyone.

Two boundaries matter. First, this is about insured mortgages — less than 20% down. Buyers with 20% or more down hold uninsured mortgages, where 30-year amortizations were already available at most lenders long before 2024. Second, the longer insured amortization is not free: CMHC applies a premium surcharge of 20 basis points — 0.20 percentage points — to the mortgage loan insurance premium for borrowers opting for the 30-year amortization. Both facts belong in the same calculation, which is where most guides separate them.

One loan, two schedules: payment, first term, lifetime

Take a $500,000 mortgage at an illustrative 4.50% rate — a worked example, not a quote — computed with Canadian semi-annual compounding. Only the amortization changes.

Monthly payment$2,767$2,521$246 less a month
Interest paid in the first 5 years$105,024$106,766$1,742 more
Balance after 5 years$438,982$455,502$16,520 more owing
Total interest over the full amortization$330,209$407,588$77,379 more

The three differences tell three different stories. The monthly story is the one buyers feel: $246 a month of breathing room, which is also why the longer amortization can help a buyer qualify — the stress test and debt ratios run on the lower payment. The lifetime story is the headline number, $77,379 of extra interest — but it assumes the schedule is never changed for three decades, which is not how Canadian mortgages behave. The first-term story is the one that matches reality: over the first five years the extra interest is a comparatively modest $1,742, while the real cost shows up as a balance $16,520 higher at renewal. That balance gap, not the lifetime total, is the number a buyer will actually negotiate with at the first renewal, move, or refinance. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada's own summary frames the trade-off the same way: a longer amortization increases the interest paid but reduces the monthly payments, and the choice should fit the monthly budget.

The insurance surcharge, in dollars

On an insured purchase the premium itself changes with the amortization. Consider a $600,000 home bought with 10% down: the loan is $540,000, which sits in CMHC's 85.01–90% loan-to-value band, where the standard premium is 3.10% of the loan. Adding the 0.20-point surcharge for a 30-year amortization gives:

25 years30 years
Premium rate3.10%3.30% (3.10% + 0.20% surcharge)
Premium on a $540,000 loan$16,740$17,820
Extra cost of choosing 30 years$1,080, added to the loan like the rest of the premium

The premium is a one-time charge that may be added to the mortgage amount, so the $1,080 is financed rather than paid in cash at closing. One related cash cost does apply either way in Ontario: CMHC notes that premiums in Ontario, Quebec, and Saskatchewan are subject to provincial sales tax, and that tax cannot be added to the loan — it is due at closing, and it is calculated on the slightly larger premium too. None of this makes the surcharge a reason to avoid 30 years on its own. It makes it a line item: on this purchase, the option to lower the payment costs $1,080 of premium plus the first-term interest and balance differences above.

The catch-up plan: making 30 behave like 25

Here is the number comparison guides almost never compute. The entire difference between the two schedules on the example loan is $246 a month — the 25-year payment minus the 30-year payment. A buyer who takes the 30-year amortization for qualification or cash-flow reasons, then voluntarily pays the 25-year amount each month, is on the 25-year schedule in every way that matters: same payoff date, same interest, same balance at renewal. The longer amortization becomes a floor the lender requires, not a ceiling on what the household pays.

  • Check the prepayment privileges first. The extra $246 a month must fit inside the mortgage's prepayment allowances; payment-increase options and lump-sum privileges differ by lender and product, and exceeding them can trigger penalties. Confirm the specific privilege before relying on the plan.
  • Automate the difference, do not rely on intentions. Set the payment at the 25-year figure from the first month if the budget allows it, or schedule the top-up to start at a defined trigger — a raise, the end of a car loan — rather than "when things loosen up."
  • Use the renewal as the checkpoint. At the first renewal, compare the actual balance against the 25-year schedule's balance ($438,982 on the example). The gap, if any, is what remains to be closed — a concrete number, not the $77,379 lifetime figure.
  • Success check: if the balance at renewal matches the 25-year row of the table, the 30-year choice cost only the $1,080 premium surcharge on the insured example — the price of flexibility that ended up not being needed.

Run the logic in reverse and it is equally clear: a buyer who takes 30 years, spends the $246 difference permanently, and never tops up will arrive at renewal owing about $16,520 more on the example loan, and the lifetime interest figure stops being hypothetical. The schedule is not the decision. The payment behaviour is.

A decision framework: cash flow now vs equity speed

Choose 25 years when the higher payment fits comfortably inside the budget with a real reserve left after closing. It is the cheaper schedule in every column, builds equity faster, and needs no discipline to deliver its numbers. Choose 30 years when the lower payment is what makes the purchase qualify, or when the first years of ownership carry other heavy costs — childcare, a single income, a business being built — and there is a credible, dated plan to raise payments later. Treat 30 years as a warning sign when it is being used to reach a more expensive home rather than to make the same home affordable: stretching the amortization to raise the maximum price spends the flexibility and the equity speed at once, and leaves nothing in reserve for the renewal balance gap. In all three cases, decide with the first-term column in front of you. Five years is the horizon a first mortgage is actually lived on; the 30-year column is a default that behaviour can override.

FAQ

Does the property have to be a new build for a first-time buyer to get 30 years? No — not since December 15, 2024. The August 2024 version required a new build for first-time buyers; the December expansion covers all first-time homebuyers, resale homes included, and all buyers of new builds.

Is the 30-year option available with 20% down? Yes, but through the uninsured route, where 30-year amortizations were already standard at most lenders — the 2024 changes and the CMHC surcharge concern insured mortgages with less than 20% down.

Can the amortization be shortened later? Payments can be raised within the prepayment privileges, and the amortization can be reset at renewal. That is the mechanical basis of the catch-up plan above — the original schedule is a default, not a lock.
