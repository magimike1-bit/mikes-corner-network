Closing day funds: how the money actually moves, what to deliver to your lawyer, and the wire fraud check

Know the process · 7 min read

Your closing money never goes to the seller. Not the deposit, not the down payment balance, not one dollar of it. Every amount moves through lawyers' trust accounts, in certified funds you deliver days before closing, and the seller's lawyer releases the keys only after the transfer is registered. Once that path is clear, the deadlines stop looking arbitrary, because each one exists to keep a specific step in the sequence possible.

The money path, stop by stop

Ownright's explainer of the Ontario process states the rule behind the table: buyers and sellers never exchange money directly, and a lawyer's trust account is a regulated account governed and audited under Law Society of Ontario rules, with funds held until the legal steps are complete. Mortgage Squad's closing guide fills in your lawyer's workload on the day: receiving the advance, combining it with your funds, paying the seller's side, registering the transfer of title and the mortgage charge, and confirming you as the new owner.

What you deliver, and when: draft, wire, or certified cheque

Your one delivery is stop 2, and its specification has three parts. The amount is the balance of your down payment plus your closing costs. Mortgage Squad puts typical Canadian closing costs at 1.5 to 4 percent of the purchase price, on top of the down payment, and your lawyer's statement of adjustments turns that range into one exact figure for your file. The form is certified funds: a bank draft made payable to your lawyer's firm, in trust, is the standard method LegalClarity describes, with certified cheques and lawyer-arranged wires as the other accepted routes. Personal cheques are not accepted, because they take too long to clear.

The deadline is your lawyer's, and published guidance varies because files vary. Mortgage Squad says funds are usually delivered a day or two before closing. LegalClarity reports that most lawyers ask for the money at least two to three business days in advance, and notes the Law Society of Ontario's cautions about wires: sharing trust account details carries risk, and wired funds may need a clearance period before they can be disbursed. Treat the earliest date your own lawyer gives you as the deadline. A draft delivered three days early has never delayed a closing; a wire sent on the morning has.

The wire fraud check, as numbered steps

Real estate closings are a documented fraud target because the amounts are large, the deadline is fixed, and the instructions arrive by email. Savie Wander's Ontario closing guide states the defence in one line: verify wire instructions by phone using a number you look up independently, not from an email alone. Run it as a procedure.

  1. Receive the instructions, then stop. Do not act on any instruction that arrives or changes by email alone.
  2. Find your lawyer's phone number from a source you already trust, a signed retainer, the firm's listing, or a search you initiate. Never the number inside the email carrying the instructions.
  3. Call and confirm three things aloud: the account the funds go to, the exact payee name, and the exact amount. Savie Wander's checklist has the buyer confirming payee and amount with the lawyer the day before delivering funds.
  4. Send or deliver the funds exactly as confirmed, then call again to confirm receipt.
  5. Treat any later message changing the account, the payee, or the amount as hostile until a fresh phone call on the independently found number confirms it.

Success looks like this: the amount your lawyer confirms as received matches the statement of adjustments to the dollar, and no instruction was ever followed from email alone. Five minutes of telephone beats every other control on this list, because it is the one step a forged email cannot survive.

Closing day itself: registration, then keys

On the day, your job is mostly done, which is the point of delivering early. Your lawyer confirms the funds and documents are in place, registers the transfer, and the deal completes during business hours, often in the afternoon, because registration and the movement of funds set the pace. Keys are released once title is registered and funds have changed hands, not at a set hour. In Ontario agreements the possession time is often written as 6 pm on closing day, which is a deadline for the seller to be out, not a promise about when your key handover happens. Plan the moving truck for the day after, or hold the afternoon loosely. The buyers who suffer on closing day are the ones whose money is still travelling; deliver early and the day is quiet.

Decision framework: which delivery method fits your timetable

Bank draft is the default for a reason: it is payable to the firm in trust, it clears on the lawyer's schedule, and it leaves a paper trail you hold a copy of. Choose a wire only when your lawyer offers it, only with the phone verification procedure above, and only with the clearance period counted into your deadline. Choose a certified cheque where your lawyer accepts one and the amount was fixed early enough to certify it. In every case the method matters less than the two disciplines around it: funds in certified form, and funds in your lawyer's hands days before they are needed. Get those two right and the money path runs exactly as the table says, stop by stop, to the keys.
