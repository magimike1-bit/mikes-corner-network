Condo vs freehold for a first purchase in Canada: fees, rules, insurance, and resale

Choosing a home · 8 min read

"Condo or freehold?" is usually debated as a lifestyle question — amenities versus a yard, lock-and-leave versus control. For a first-time buyer it is more useful to treat it as a structural question: the two choices are different legal and financial objects. A freehold purchase is a home and the land under it, with every cost and decision landing on one owner. A condominium purchase, in CMHC's framing, is a unit plus a share of the common elements, inside a condominium corporation whose budget, rules, and repair decisions become partly yours — including some you would never have voted for. Neither structure is the "smart" one in general. Each is a bundle of specific obligations, and the right first purchase is the bundle whose obligations fit the buyer's budget, tolerance for surprises, and plans. This guide compares the bundles line by line, then gives the document-review procedure CMHC's own checklist implies, because on the condo side the most important part of the purchase is a corporation most buyers never investigate.

What you own, and who decides

Freehold: the buyer owns the dwelling and the land. Maintenance timing, renovation choices, and exterior changes are the owner's decisions, subject to municipal bylaws and permits rather than to a board. There is no monthly common fee — and no one else sharing the roof bill when it comes due.

Condominium: the buyer owns the unit and a share of the common elements — hallways, structure, grounds, amenities — with the corporation responsible for the common property and the owners collectively funding it. CMHC is blunt about the trade: condo living can mean less privacy, paying for amenities the owner rarely uses, having less say in deciding when repairs get done, and possible restrictions on things like parking, pets, or smoking. Those are not edge cases; they are the structure working as designed. The buyer's control over their own four walls is real but bounded by the declaration, bylaws, and rules — documents that exist before the purchase and bind after it.

The fee, line by line — and the freehold mirror image

The condo fee is the comparison's most misunderstood number, so place it carefully. The fee is a monthly charge from the condominium corporation, generally calculated according to the unit's share of the property, and it is not part of the mortgage payment — lender guidance is explicit that fees are paid separately to the corporation after possession. CMHC's own affordability question for a condo buyer lists the full monthly stack side by side: mortgage payments, condo fees, utilities, insurance premiums, property taxes, and other expenses. That stack is the comparison. A condo's lower purchase price shrinks only the first line; the fee adds a second line that a freehold budget instead fills with direct maintenance spending.

The honest way to compare is arithmetic, not averages — fee levels vary too much by building to quote as fact. Work it with your own two candidate properties: take the freehold's monthly mortgage payment, add a monthly maintenance reserve you set yourself (roof, furnace, exterior — irregular, but never zero), and compare against the condo's mortgage payment plus its actual fee, then check what the fee includes. Fees that cover heat, water, or building insurance are buying things the freehold column pays separately; fees heavy with amenities you will not use are the mirror-image waste CMHC warns about. Two cautions belong in every version of this math: fees can increase, and CMHC's checklist tells buyers to find out what the fee includes and when it is likely to increase; and a fee that looks low because the reserve fund is starved is not a saving — it is a special assessment that has not been scheduled yet.

Insurance: two policies on one side, one on the other

Insurance is where the structures diverge in a way buyers routinely get wrong. A mortgage requires home insurance in either case. On the freehold side the picture is simple: one owner, one property, one homeowner policy covering the building and its contents. On the condo side there are two policies over the same address. The corporation's policy covers the building and common elements — and, lender guidance states plainly, it will not cover the unit owner's personal losses. The owner therefore needs their own condo policy for the contents of the unit and personal liability, arranged so the certificate reaches the lawyer before closing with coverage starting on the day of possession. A buyer who mentally files "insurance" under "the fee covers that" is uninsured for everything inside their own front door. CMHC's guide makes the related diligence question part of the purchase itself: is the condominium well managed, in good physical, financial, and legal condition — and does it have sufficient insurance coverage?

Due diligence: read the corporation before you buy into it

A freehold purchase is vetted with an inspection and a title search. A condo purchase needs those plus a financial investigation of a small corporation the buyer is about to co-own. CMHC's checklist for buying a resale condominium is, in effect, the procedure — run in this order:

  1. Get the estoppel (status) certificate, the annual operating budget, and the end-of-year financial statements. CMHC lists exactly these as the financial documents to review; together they show what the corporation spends, what it charges, and what this specific unit owes.
  2. Read the reserve fund position against the reserve fund study. Consult the corporation's technical audit and/or reserve fund study for the building's condition, and verify — in CMHC's words — that there is enough money in the reserve fund to cover the cost of major repairs and renewal projects.
  3. Ask the assessment question directly: are any special assessments anticipated, what are they for, how much might they cost, and when will they need to be paid?
  4. Find out what the monthly fee includes and when it is likely to increase — a fee's contents decide whether it is expensive or merely bundled.
  5. Hunt the hidden costs: long-term leases on building fixtures passed along to owners, and any legal actions against the corporation — CMHC says to confirm there are none.
  6. Confirm the physical and legal boundaries: exactly where the unit's boundaries lie, whether the unit factor is reasonable, and a home inspector's evaluation of the unit and the building as a whole.
  7. Have a lawyer review before signing. CMHC's checklist ends there, and on a document set this size it is not a formality.

What success looks like: before the offer firms up, the buyer can state the corporation's reserve fund position, whether an assessment or fee increase is on the record, what the fee actually buys, and which rules (pets, parking, rentals, renovations) would govern their own daily life — all from documents, not from the listing.

Resale: how each structure reads to the next buyer

A first purchase is rarely the last home, so run the exit test on both structures. A freehold resale is a comparatively clean read for the next buyer: condition, location, and the inspection tell the story, and the buyer's monthly math is mortgage plus predictable ownership costs. A condo resale carries the corporation into the negotiation: the next buyer's own status-certificate review will show the fee level, the reserve fund's health, and any pending assessment — and their monthly math adds the fee on top of the mortgage, which caps what some buyers can offer regardless of how they feel about the unit. This cuts in both directions, and honestly: a well-run corporation with a healthy reserve fund and a fee that visibly buys maintained grounds and a sound building is a selling feature, because the next buyer's diligence comes back clean. A corporation with a starved reserve fund is a discount the seller ends up funding. The practical conclusion is not that one structure resells better; it is that on the condo side, part of the resale value is decided in board meetings the owner does not control — which belongs in the purchase decision, priced in from day one.

The decision framework

  • Choose the condo structure when: the all-in monthly stack (mortgage + fee + the rest of CMHC's list) genuinely fits with room to spare, the diligence in the procedure above comes back clean, shared maintenance is worth real money to you in time, and the corporation's rules on the things you care about — a dog, a second car, a future rental — permit your actual life.
  • Choose freehold when: control over the property and its projects matters more than shared upkeep, the budget can absorb irregular repair bills without a monthly fee's smoothing, and no set of bylaws would sit comfortably over your plans for the place.
  • Walk away from the specific property, whichever structure, when: the numbers only work with the fee treated as fixed forever, the reserve fund story does not add up, or the freehold's deferred maintenance is visible but unpriced. Structure is a preference; a bad building or a bad corporation is a defect.

FAQ

Are condo fees included in the mortgage payment? No. Lender guidance is explicit: condo fees are paid separately to the condominium corporation after possession. Budget them as their own line, the way CMHC's affordability list does.

Does the condo corporation's insurance cover my belongings? No — the corporation's policy covers the building and common elements, not an owner's personal losses. A condo owner needs their own policy for contents and liability, in place from the day of possession.

What is a status certificate (estoppel certificate)? The corporation's document package for a unit — CMHC's checklist pairs it with the operating budget and financial statements as the financial record a resale buyer reviews: fees owed, the corporation's finances, its insurance, and its obligations.

What is a special assessment? An extra charge to owners beyond the regular fee, typically when the corporation faces costs its budget and reserve fund cannot absorb. CMHC's checklist treats "are any anticipated, for how much, and when" as a standard pre-purchase question for exactly this reason.
