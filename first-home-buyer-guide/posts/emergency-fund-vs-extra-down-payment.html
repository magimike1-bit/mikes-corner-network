Emergency Fund vs Extra Down Payment: Where the Next Dollar Should Go

Down payment · 6 min read

Keep the emergency fund. That is the direct answer for most first-time buyers, most of the time — with one exception that has actual numbers behind it: when the next dollars would carry the down payment across a CMHC premium threshold at 10%, 15%, or 20%, and the fund left behind still covers a starter cushion. The comparison table comes first here, because the size of the gap between thresholds is what decides the question.

The thresholds matter because CMHC premium tiers make crossing 10%, 15%, and 20% down financially meaningful — the premium charged on an insured mortgage steps down at those lines rather than sliding smoothly with every extra dollar. A dollar that crosses a line buys two things: a smaller mortgage, and a cheaper premium tier. A dollar sitting between lines buys only the smaller mortgage. That asymmetry is the whole argument, and it cuts both ways — it is also why "a bit more than the minimum, nowhere near the next threshold" is the weakest position on the table.

One honest limit on the math: premium percentages by tier change and are set by the insurer, so check the current CMHC premium table before pricing a threshold crossing in dollars. The threshold amounts above are arithmetic from the home price; the premium saving has to be priced at decision time.

What the fund is actually for

The sourced guidance is consistent: keep three to six months of expenses in a separate high-interest savings account or TFSA, never raided for the purchase. The fund exists as protection against job loss and unexpected repairs in the first year of ownership — the year when the household has the least slack it will ever have, because the down payment and closing costs have just gone out the door.

Putting every dollar into the down payment is named in the sources as a common first-buyer mistake, and the mechanism is easy to picture. A furnace failure or a stretch without income, landing two months after closing, gets paid from a credit card or a credit line. The household then owns a slightly smaller mortgage and a new, more expensive debt — the reverse of what the extra down payment was meant to achieve.

Closing day sharpens the point. Cash needs at closing extend beyond the down payment itself, and while Ontario first-time buyers can claim a land transfer tax refund of up to $4,000, that refund reduces one line item; it does not remove the need for money left over after the keys change hands.

The staged plan: mini-fund, down payment, rebuild

  1. Stage one — a starter fund, before the down payment push. Build a small cushion first, held separately, sized to cover the household's most likely single shock. This stage is deliberately short: its job is to make the long saving stage survivable, so one car repair does not consume a month of down payment money.
  2. Stage two — the down payment, aimed at a line. Save toward a named target: the minimum, or a specific threshold from the table. If a threshold is the target, the fund stays untouched while the down payment grows toward it — raiding the cushion to cross the line defeats the staging.
  3. Stage three — rebuild the fund to full size after closing. The three-to-six-months-of-expenses target belongs here, after purchase, funded from the monthly amount that used to go to down payment saving. The saving habit does not stop at closing; only its destination changes.

The exception clause, stated plainly: if stage two stalls just short of a threshold — close enough that a defined, short period of extra saving crosses it — finishing the crossing before rebuilding the fund further can be the better use of the next dollars, provided the starter cushion from stage one stays intact. Just short of a line is a position with a finish date. Halfway between lines is not.

Your decision worksheet

  • Write down three to six months of household expenses. That range is the fund's full-size target.
  • Write down the down payment at the minimum, and at the next threshold above it, for the target price. The gap between the two numbers is the real decision.
  • If the gap can be saved in a short, defined period without touching the starter cushion, aim at the threshold. If the gap is open-ended, buy at the level the savings honestly reach and rebuild the fund.
  • Hold the fund in its own account, separate from purchase money, and leave it alone for anything short of the job loss or repair it exists for.
  • Price the current CMHC premium at both down payment levels before deciding — the tier saving is part of the return on crossing, and it belongs in the comparison in current dollars.
