Home inspection vs appraisal: two reports, two different questions

Know the process · 6 min read

First-time buyers routinely use "inspection" and "appraisal" as if they were the same appointment. They are not: they answer different questions, they are performed by different professionals, one of them works for you and the other usually works for your lender, and confusing them leads to a classic mistake — assuming that because the bank valued the home, somebody must have checked its condition. Nobody did. This guide separates the two, adds the third report buyers confuse with both, and shows where each one fits in a Canadian purchase.

What a home inspection actually covers

An inspection is a visual assessment of the home's condition at the time of the visit. The Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation's homebuying guidance lists what a proper inspection should include: the foundation; doors and windows; the roof and exterior walls; attics; plumbing and electrical systems where visible; heating and air conditioning; ceilings, walls, and floors; insulation where visible; ventilation; the lot, including drainage away from the building; and an overall opinion of structural integrity — plus common areas for a condominium or co-operative. The inspector's job is to tell you what is not working properly, what needs changing, what is unsafe, and where there were problems in the past.

Two limits matter. First, a visual inspection does not open walls or guarantee what hides behind them; it reports what could be observed. Second, CMHC recommends including an inspection as a condition in the offer — that condition is what turns a bad report into a protected exit or a renegotiation, instead of an expensive fact you learn after firming up.

Choosing an inspector is a step, not a shrug

CMHC's guidance is blunt about the Canadian reality: in most areas there are no licensing or certification requirements for home inspectors, and anyone can claim the title. The practical screen it suggests: look for membership in a provincial or industry association, accreditation that demonstrates training and experience, written inspection reports, insurance, references, and strong experience with the type of home being inspected. CMHC does not recommend individual inspectors or associations — the checking is the buyer's job.

What an appraisal actually does

An appraisal assesses the property's fair market value from its size, condition, age, and location, set against the selling prices of comparable homes. The most common trigger is the buyer's own mortgage: the lender orders it to confirm the home supports the loan before releasing funds. Appraisals also appear on refinances and home-equity applications, and occasionally a seller commissions one before listing for pricing confidence; they are also used for estate settlements and separations.

The consequence buyers miss: if the appraisal comes in below the agreed price, the lender lends against the appraised value, not the contract price — and the difference has to come from somewhere, usually a larger down payment, a renegotiated price, or a collapsed deal if a financing condition still protects it. An appraisal is not a comment on whether the roof leaks. A home can appraise at full value with a furnace on its last winter.

How to sequence both in a real purchase

  1. Before offering: decide your inspection strategy. In a calm market, write the inspection in as a condition. In a hot market, the middle path some buyers use is a pre-offer inspection — an inspector attends viewings or does a shortened visit before the bid — accepting that it is a quicker, less complete look.
  2. During the conditional period: book the full inspection within days of acceptance; inspection conditions run on short deadlines, and findings are only useful while the condition is alive.
  3. In parallel: expect the lender's appraisal as part of mortgage approval, and keep a contingency in your cash plan for an appraisal gap rather than assuming value and price will match.
  4. Before waiving conditions: read the inspection report for deal-breakers — structure, water, electrical, heating — and confirm financing is firm, appraisal included. Only then does the purchase go firm.

What success looks like: you hold two different documents answering two different questions — a condition report you commissioned and control, and a value opinion your lender commissioned — and neither one is being asked to do the other's job.

Questions people commonly ask

Is an inspection mandatory in Canada? No — but CMHC recommends it as an offer condition, and "not mandatory" describes the law, not the risk. Skipping it in competition is a gamble whose size is the cost of the defects nobody saw.

How much does an inspection cost? It varies with the property's size, age, location, deadline, and scope; a typical figure quoted by Canadian consumer sources is a few hundred dollars, often in the $500–$600 range. Get quotes — and weigh the fee against the repair bills it exists to reveal.

Does a condo need an inspection? It is not mandatory either, and it remains useful for the unit's own systems and condition — separate from the building-level review of the status certificate, which is a different document answering different questions.

What neither report covers

Knowing the limits of both documents prevents the last category of disappointment. An inspection is visual and present-tense: it will not predict when a mid-life furnace will fail, certify what is sealed inside walls, or test for every environmental concern — specialized testing exists for questions like asbestos or pests, and it is commissioned separately when a property's age or history raises them. An appraisal is a value opinion at a point in time, built substantially on comparable sales: it does not warrant the property's condition, and it can move with the market after closing without anyone having made an error. And neither document replaces the condo status certificate review, which examines the corporation — its reserve fund, planned assessments, and legal exposure — rather than the unit or its market value. A first purchase goes smoothly when each question is routed to the document that actually answers it: condition to the inspector, value to the appraiser, the building's finances to the lawyer's certificate review, and pricing tactics to the agent's market analysis.
