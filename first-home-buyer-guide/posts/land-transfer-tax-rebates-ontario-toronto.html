Land transfer tax rebates for first-time buyers: Ontario and Toronto

Closing costs · 7 min read · Figures checked October 13, 2026

Land transfer tax is the closing cost that surprises buyers most, partly because it is large and partly because it is due in cash on closing day, on top of the down payment. In Ontario every buyer pays the provincial tax. Inside the City of Toronto, buyers pay a second, municipal tax as well. First-time buyers get money back from both levels — up to $4,000 from Ontario and up to $4,475 from Toronto — but only if they meet eligibility rules that are stricter than most people expect. This guide works the math by hand, shows the net cash due at three prices, and turns claiming the refunds into a short procedure to run before an offer goes in.

Two taxes, one closing day

Ontario charges land transfer tax whenever land changes hands in the province, calculated on the value of the consideration — in plain terms, the purchase price. The City of Toronto charges its own Municipal Land Transfer Tax, in addition to the provincial tax, on properties inside Toronto. A buyer in Mississauga, Ottawa, or Markham pays the provincial tax only. A buyer in Toronto pays both, which is why the same home can carry roughly double the transfer tax a few kilometres apart.

Neither tax can be added to a mortgage. Both are settled through the buyer's real estate lawyer at closing, which means the net amount belongs in the cash-to-close budget alongside the deposit, the balance of the down payment, legal fees, and adjustments — not in the mortgage math.

The Ontario brackets, worked by hand

Ontario's tax is marginal, like income tax: each rate applies only to the slice of the price inside its bracket. Under the Land Transfer Tax Act, the rates for a home are 0.5% on the first $55,000, 1.0% on the portion from $55,000 to $250,000, 1.5% on the portion from $250,000 to $400,000, and — because of an additional 0.5% levy above $400,000 on land containing one or two single-family residences — an effective 2.0% on the portion above $400,000 at the prices most first-time buyers pay.

Worked through on an $800,000 home outside Toronto, the provincial tax is: $275 on the first $55,000, plus $1,950 on the next $195,000, plus $2,250 on the next $150,000, plus $8,000 on the remaining $400,000 — a total of $12,475. On a $500,000 home the same steps give $6,475. Those two totals are worth memorizing as anchors: roughly $6,500 of provincial tax at $500,000, and roughly $12,500 at $800,000, before any first-time buyer refund.

Toronto adds a second tax

Toronto's municipal tax uses bracket rates that match the provincial structure at common first-home prices, so at an $800,000 purchase the municipal bill is also $12,475 and the combined gross tax is $24,950. The city's first-time buyer rebate then applies to the municipal layer only, just as the Ontario refund applies to the provincial layer only. Neither rebate can be used against the other government's tax.

Who actually qualifies for the refunds

Ontario's refund is worth up to $4,000. Since January 1, 2017, qualifying first-time purchasers pay no provincial tax on the first $368,000 of the price; above that price the refund is capped at the $4,000 maximum. Toronto's rebate is worth up to $4,475 on the municipal tax, for a combined maximum of $8,475 for an eligible Toronto buyer.

The eligibility rules are where buyers get caught. For the Ontario refund, a purchaser must:

  • Be at least 18 years old.
  • Occupy the home as a principal residence within nine months of the transfer.
  • Never have owned a home, or an interest in a home, anywhere in the world, at any time — ownership abroad counts, and so does ownership acquired by gift or inheritance.
  • Have a spouse who has not owned a home anywhere in the world while they were spouses; if a spouse did, neither spouse qualifies.
  • Be a Canadian citizen or permanent resident — a requirement in place since January 1, 2017, with an 18-month window after registration to become eligible and then apply.

Toronto's rebate carries the same core tests: 18 or older, principal residence within nine months, never owned anywhere in the world, the same spouse rule, and Canadian citizenship or permanent residence, again with the option to qualify within 18 months of the transfer. Two further points matter. First, Ontario is explicit that a buyer cannot re-qualify as a first-time homebuyer for this refund later — this program's test is stricter than federal programs that use a look-back window. Second, the refund is proportionate when not every buyer on title qualifies: Ontario's own example is a parent and child buying with equal 50/50 interests, where the qualifying child can claim only 50% of the refund. A non-qualifying name on title is not a paperwork detail; it directly shrinks the cheque.

The net cash, side by side

The table below applies the brackets and both refunds, assuming a fully eligible first-time buyer and checked against the official sources on October 13, 2026. It is an estimate for planning — a lawyer confirms the exact figures on the statement of adjustments.

$500,000$6,475$2,475$4,475
$800,000$12,475$8,475$16,475

Reading the Toronto column: at $500,000 the municipal tax of $6,475 is reduced by the $4,475 city rebate to $2,000, and the provincial layer nets to $2,475, so the combined net is $4,475. At $800,000 the two net layers are $8,475 provincial and $8,000 municipal, for $16,475 combined. The lesson is the same in either column: even after both refunds, a Toronto first-time buyer should expect five figures of transfer tax in cash at the prices common in that market, and the refund does not change the need to budget for it early.

How to claim: a step-by-step procedure

  1. Check eligibility before house hunting. Run the five Ontario tests above for every person who will be on title, including the spouse rule and any past ownership outside Canada.
  2. Estimate the tax at the target price. Apply the brackets — 0.5%, then 1.0%, then 1.5%, then 2.0% — and, inside Toronto, run the same calculation a second time for the municipal tax.
  3. Subtract the refunds. Up to $4,000 provincial, plus up to $4,475 municipal in Toronto, reduced proportionally if any buyer on title does not qualify.
  4. Set the net amount aside in cash. Add it to the closing-costs budget; it cannot be financed into the mortgage.
  5. Tell the lawyer about first-time status early. The Ontario refund is normally claimed at registration through the electronic land registration statements, so the tax payable at closing is already reduced.
  6. If the refund was missed at closing, apply afterwards. Ontario allows a refund claim to the Ministry of Finance within 18 months after registration; keep the registered transfer, the agreement of purchase and sale, the statement of adjustments, proof of occupancy, and proof of citizenship or permanent residence ready.

What success looks like: the statement of adjustments from the lawyer shows the land transfer tax reduced by the expected refund amounts, and the cash the buyer brings to closing matches the net figure estimated before the offer — no gap that has to be covered by a credit card or a rushed loan.

FAQ

Does the refund apply to resale homes, or only new builds? Both. For agreements entered into after December 13, 2007, Ontario's refund applies to newly constructed and resale homes alike, and Toronto's rebate likewise covers new and resale residential properties.

A parent is going on title for mortgage reasons. Is the whole refund lost? Not necessarily, but it can be reduced. If the parent acquires a real ownership interest, the refund is proportionate to the qualifying buyers' interests. Ontario will treat a parent who is on title only as a trustee for the child — for example, at a lender's insistence — as not acquiring a beneficial interest, provided evidence of the trust is submitted.

Can the tax be rolled into the mortgage to spread the cost? No. Land transfer tax is settled in cash at closing through the lawyer, which is why it belongs in the cash-to-close plan rather than the mortgage comparison.
