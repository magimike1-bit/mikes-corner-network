Mortgage Brokers vs Bank Lenders for First-Time Buyers in Canada

Mortgages · 7 min read

If a mortgage broker costs you nothing, who is paying — and what are they paying for? That question, followed honestly, explains most of the difference between the broker channel and walking into your bank. Neither channel is free. The money moves differently, the shelf of products is a different size, and the largest cost difference between the two is not the rate at all. It is the penalty if you break the mortgage early — a live risk for first-time buyers, whose first term is the one most likely to end in a move, a refinance, or a life change.

One warning before the numbers: most online explainers on this topic are written by brokerages. That does not make them wrong, but it does mean the bank-side case is usually argued by its opponents. Read what follows with the incentives in view — including the ones described below.

Who pays whom: the teardown

On a standard prime (A-lender) deal, a broker is typically paid about 1% of the mortgage amount by the lender, at no direct cost to the borrower. On a $500,000 mortgage, that is roughly $5,000 — real money, paid by the lender that gets your business, and worth knowing about when a broker steers you toward one lender over another. The service is usually free to the borrower on prime deals precisely because that finder's fee exists.

The borrower's own wallet opens on harder placements. Borrower-paid broker fees of roughly 0.5% to 2% arise mainly on alternative or B-lender placements — on that same $500,000 mortgage, $2,500 to $10,000. If a fee is quoted to you, the first question is which kind of placement you are actually in, because the fee pattern is a signal about it.

A bank charges no broker fee, because there is no broker. The bank's cost is built into the rate it offers on its own products — which is the whole of its shelf, as the next section covers.

Access: whose products each channel can actually sell

Banks offer only their own products. A broker accesses multiple lenders, including monoline lenders (lenders that sell only through brokers) and credit unions. That wider shelf is the broker channel's genuine advantage, and it matters most when a file is unusual — uneven income, a thin credit history, a property a bank's template dislikes.

But the shelf is not the whole market. Not all lenders work with brokers, and some bank products are only available in-branch. A broker cannot sell you those, and a bank cannot sell you a monoline's. Neither channel shows you everything, which is why the practical version of shopping around is one conversation in each channel, not five in one.

Brokers can also submit one application, with one credit pull, to several lenders. For a first-time buyer, that is a real convenience — and it makes the comparison in the next section easier to run, because the offers arriving back can be compared on more than the headline rate.

The hidden difference: break penalties

Ask both channels one question most buyers never ask: if this mortgage ends two years early, what do I pay? Prepayment penalties are usually calculated as the greater of three months' interest or the interest rate differential — the IRD, the lender's estimate of the interest it loses when your rate is above what it can charge today for the remaining term. A closed variable-rate mortgage usually charges three months' interest. A closed fixed-rate mortgage charges the greater of the two figures, and the IRD is where the money gets large.

Worked example, with every assumption labelled. Take a $400,000 balance at 5%, two years left on the term, and a comparison rate of 3.5% for the remaining term:

  • Three months' interest: $400,000 × 5% × 3/12 = about $5,000.
  • IRD: (5% − 3.5%) × $400,000 × 2 years = about $12,000.
  • Penalty payable (fixed rate, greater of the two): about $12,000 — a $7,000 difference created by the formula, not the rate.

One further wrinkle decides how large that IRD gets in practice: the IRD is usually based on banks' posted rates, and because posted rates move, the penalty can change substantially — and penalties are often larger than consumers expect, as the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada has told Parliament. Two lenders can quote you the same rate on day one and be $7,000 apart on the day you need to leave. A tenth of a point saved on the rate does not survive contact with a five-figure penalty difference.

So the comparison to run, in either channel, is: rate, plus the penalty formula, plus which rate the formula compares against. Get the formula in writing before you sign, not the estimate after you need it.

How to verify an Ontario broker's licence

Bank staff sell their own institution's products. An independent broker or agent in Ontario, by contrast, holds a licence you can check — so check it. The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) publishes the listing of licensed mortgage brokerages, administrators, agents, and brokers, and it is searchable by the public:

  1. Open FSRA's licensed mortgage brokerages, administrators, agents, and brokers listing from FSRA's website.
  2. Choose the registrant type you are checking: mortgage agents or brokers, mortgage brokerages, or mortgage administrators.
  3. Search for the person or brokerage by name.
  4. Confirm the licence is present and current, and that the agent works under the brokerage named in your paperwork.

What success looks like: the name on the business card and the name in FSRA's listing match, the licence shows as active, and the brokerage on the listing is the brokerage on your documents. A name that does not appear at all is a stop sign, not a detail.

Which channel fits which first-time buyer

There is no universal winner here, and any source that names one without asking about your file is selling something. The fit runs like this:

  • Straightforward file, strong credit, and a long relationship with one bank? Get the bank's offer — then make it compete. Ask what it can do on rate and, in writing, how its break penalty is calculated.
  • Uneven income, bruised credit, or a file a template might reject? A broker's wider shelf, including B-lenders, is built for this — with eyes open about the 0.5–2% borrower-paid fee range that can come with those placements.
  • Likely to move or refinance inside the first term? Decide on the penalty formula first and the rate second, whichever channel you use. The worked example above is the math to redo with your own balance and term.
  • Either way: one application in each channel, the penalty question asked of both, and — in Ontario — the broker's licence verified on FSRA's listing before any documents change hands.

The channel is the smaller decision. The contract terms travelling with it — who was paid, what shelf it came from, and what it costs to leave — are the decision itself.
