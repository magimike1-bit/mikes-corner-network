Mortgage payment frequency and prepayment privileges: the two decisions hiding in one menu

Mortgages · 8 min read

At signing, a first-time buyer is offered a payment frequency menu and, somewhere in the commitment paperwork, a set of prepayment privileges. The two are usually explained in about ninety seconds, as if they were one decision about "how often you pay." They are not. Frequency is mostly a slicing decision — how the same annual amount is divided up. Privileges are the payoff decision — how much extra principal you are allowed to pay, and through which doors. And buried in the frequency menu is a single word, accelerated, that quietly changes the annual total itself. This guide separates the two decisions, works the arithmetic on one clearly-labelled illustrative payment, and ends with a procedure for reading your own privileges before you sign — because the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) requires federally regulated lenders to print them in one prominent information box, and almost nobody reads the box.

The six frequencies, with the formulas lenders actually use

FCAC lists the payment frequency options a Canadian borrower may be able to choose from, and — unusually helpfully — gives the formula behind each one. Payment amounts below are all derived from the monthly payment, which the rest of this article calls M:

  • Monthly: 1 payment per month, of M. Twelve payments a year.
  • Semi-monthly: 2 payments per month, each M ÷ 2. Twenty-four payments a year — note this is not the same schedule as biweekly, however similar they sound.
  • Biweekly: 1 payment every 2 weeks, each M × 12 ÷ 26. Twenty-six payments a year.
  • Weekly: 1 payment per week, each M × 12 ÷ 52. Fifty-two payments a year.
  • Accelerated biweekly: 1 payment every 2 weeks, each M ÷ 2. Twenty-six payments a year.
  • Accelerated weekly: 1 payment per week, each M ÷ 4. Fifty-two payments a year.

Look at the two families. The four regular options all annualize the monthly amount — multiply by 12, then slice by the number of payments — so the yearly total is identical by construction. The two accelerated options skip the annualizing step: they simply take a fraction of the monthly payment and collect it on the more frequent calendar. That difference in the formula is the entire story of the next section.

Three readings of that table matter. First, regular frequency changes almost nothing: monthly, semi-monthly, biweekly, and weekly all deliver the same $24,000 a year, so among those four, choose purely by cash flow — match the schedule to how you are paid, and stop there. (Paying a little earlier within the year trims a small amount of interest, but it is not the decision that moves your payoff date.) Second, the accelerated pair pays $2,000 more per year — exactly one extra monthly payment, as FCAC puts it: 26 half-payments are 13 monthly payments wearing a different calendar. Third, that extra payment is why FCAC says accelerated schedules can reduce your amortization and save thousands, or over a full mortgage tens of thousands, of dollars in interest: the extra money lands on principal, and principal paid early is principal that stops accruing interest for the remaining life of the loan. The exact interest saving on a real mortgage depends on the balance, the rate, and the amortization, which is why FCAC's own mortgage calculator — not a rule of thumb — is the right tool for your numbers.

One practical wrinkle from lender guidance: changing frequency after the fact is usually possible, but a mid-stream change can require an interest adjustment amount for the period between your last payment and the revised payment date. Choosing correctly at signing avoids the paperwork entirely.

Prepayment privileges: the three levers

Frequency decides the schedule. Privileges decide how far beyond the schedule you may go. FCAC's framing is the legal one: on a closed mortgage there is a limit to the extra principal you can pay each year, but the agreement may include prepayment privileges that let you pay more than the regular payments without triggering a prepayment charge. Those privileges typically arrive through three levers:

  • The lump-sum privilege. Once (or in some agreements, several times) per year, you may pay a stated percentage of the original principal directly against the balance. The percentage is whatever your contract says — read it there, never from an article. The arithmetic of reading it: if a contract states 15 percent and the original principal was $400,000, the annual lump-sum room is $60,000. Same contract, different original principal, different room — the percentage travels with the original amount, not with today's balance, which is why the room feels surprisingly large in the early years.
  • The payment-increase privilege. The agreement may let you raise your regular payment by a stated percentage — one major lender's published wording allows increasing, even doubling, the regular payment without additional fees or penalties. Lender guidance is explicit about where the extra goes: any amount over the regular payment is applied straight to principal. This is the set-and-forget lever: no annual decision, no lump to save up.
  • The accelerated frequency itself. Choosing an accelerated schedule at signing is the third lever, and the only one that runs entirely on autopilot — the extra monthly payment's worth of principal arrives in small slices all year. It is also the lever with no separate cap to track, because it is the schedule, not an extra payment on top of it.

The levers stack, within the contract's limits: an accelerated schedule plus a payment increase plus an occasional lump sum is a coherent plan, provided each stays inside its own stated privilege. What they share is direction — every dollar through any of the three lands on principal earlier than scheduled, and FCAC's calculator is built to show what that does to amortization and total interest for a specific mortgage.

Penalties: the fence around the privileges

Privileges matter because the fence around them is real. FCAC lists the actions that can trigger a prepayment penalty on a closed mortgage: paying more than the allowed additional amount, breaking the contract, transferring the mortgage to another lender before the end of the term, or paying the mortgage back in full before term end — including when the home is sold. An open mortgage sits outside this fence: extra payments can be made without a penalty, which is the flexibility an open mortgage's pricing pays for. Two protections work in the borrower's favour. A federally regulated lender must set out the prepayment privileges, charges, and other key details in a single prominently displayed information box in the mortgage agreement, and it must describe how prepayment charges are calculated — with an example, illustration, or simplified method when the calculation is complex. The information is required to be findable; the only step left is reading it.

Read your own commitment: a five-line procedure

Before signing — or in the first week after, while the file is fresh — pull five lines out of the agreement and write them on one page:

  1. Open or closed? This one word decides whether the penalty fence exists at all.
  2. The lump-sum percentage, and its base. Percentage of the original principal, per year — and whether it can be used in one payment or several, and on which dates.
  3. The payment-increase percentage. How far the regular payment can be raised, and whether an increase, once made, can later be reversed within the term.
  4. The frequency you actually selected, and whether "accelerated" appears in its name. This is the line where a buyer who wanted the extra payment discovers they chose regular biweekly instead.
  5. The prepayment charge description in the information box, including how the charge is calculated if a privilege is exceeded.

What success looks like: you can state, from the document rather than from memory, your annual lump-sum room in dollars, your payment-increase room as a percentage, and which of the six frequencies — accelerated or not — your payments actually follow.

Choosing your combination

A simple decision order works for most first purchases. First, match a regular frequency to your pay cycle for cash-flow comfort — this choice is nearly free, so spend no anxiety on it. Second, decide the acceleration question on its own merits: if the budget can carry the equivalent of one extra monthly payment per year without strain, an accelerated schedule is the lowest-effort principal reduction available, and FCAC's figures say the long-run interest effect is real money. Third, treat the privileges as a tiebreaker between lenders offering similar rates: wider lump-sum and payment-increase room is worth having precisely in the years something good happens — a bonus, a raise, an inheritance — and costs nothing in the years it goes unused. Fourth, if there is a realistic chance of selling or paying out within the term, weigh open-versus-closed and the penalty description more heavily than small rate differences; FCAC's penalty list is the checklist of exactly those events.

FAQ

Is accelerated biweekly really different from regular biweekly? Yes, and the difference is the whole point: regular biweekly is the monthly payment times 12 divided by 26 — the same annual total as monthly. Accelerated biweekly is the monthly payment divided by 2, collected 26 times — one extra monthly payment per year, applied to principal.

Does semi-monthly mean the same as biweekly? No. Semi-monthly is 24 payments a year, twice per calendar month. Biweekly is 26 payments a year, every two weeks — which is why two months a year contain three biweekly payments, a budgeting detail to plan around.

Where do I find my prepayment privileges? In the mortgage agreement itself. A federally regulated lender must present the privileges, charges, and key details in a prominently displayed information box, and must explain how prepayment charges are calculated.

Can I change frequency or use a privilege later if I skip it now? Generally yes within the contract's terms — privileges exist to be used during the term — but a frequency change can involve an interest adjustment amount, and lump-sum privileges usually reset annually rather than accumulating. The privileges in your signed agreement govern; ask the lender to confirm in writing before relying on either.
