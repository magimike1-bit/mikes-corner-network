Pre-approval vs pre-qualification: what a rate hold actually guarantees

Qualifying · 7 min read

Two buyers can both say they are "pre-approved" and hold completely different things. One answered five questions on a website and got a number. The other submitted pay stubs, tax documents, and down payment statements, had a credit check, and left with a written rate hold. Sellers, lenders, and the buyers' own budgets treat those two pieces of paper very differently — and in Canada the labels themselves are part of the problem, because lenders do not use them consistently.

The labels are not standardized: ask what was verified

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) makes a point most guides skip: the preapproval process "may also be called mortgage prequalification or mortgage preauthorization," and different lenders have different definitions and criteria for each step they offer. So the useful question is never "am I pre-approved?" It is "what did you actually check?" The distinction that matters in practice:

Pre-qualification (unverified estimate)Pre-approval (verified)
BasisFigures the buyer states — income, debts, down paymentDocuments the lender or broker reviews
Credit checkSoft check or none to startA credit check is run (FCAC: the lender "likely" runs one)
Rate holdNoYes — 60 to 130 days, depending on the lender (FCAC)
Time to getMinutes, often an online calculator resultDays, once documents are in
Weight with sellersLittle — nothing has been verifiedReal, but still not a guarantee of final approval
Property checked?NoNo — the property is verified later, at final approval

Broker guides commonly quote the rate hold as "90 to 120 days." FCAC's official range is wider — 60 to 130 days depending on the lender — and the difference is not pedantry. A buyer shopping in a slow market on a 60-day hold has a very different clock than a buyer holding 130 days. The hold length is a specific term of a specific lender's offer, and it belongs on the list of questions asked before relying on it.

What a pre-approval actually checks, and what it does not

At pre-approval, the lender looks at the buyer's side of the transaction. FCAC lists what is examined and provided: personal information and identification; proof of employment — a recent pay stub, position, and length of time with the employer, plus notices of assessment from the Canada Revenue Agency for the past two years for self-employed applicants; proof the buyer can pay the down payment and closing costs, which may mean recent bank or investment statements; information about other assets; and a full accounting of debts — credit card balances, car loans, lines of credit, student loans, support payments, and other obligations. Those figures are run through the debt-service ratios at the minimum qualifying rate: the greater of the contract rate plus 2 percentage points or a 5.25% floor. The resulting maximum is the pre-approval amount.

What has not been checked is the other side of the transaction: the house. FCAC is explicit that preapproval "does not guarantee your approval for a mortgage," that the approved amount depends on the value of the property and the down payment, and that before final approval the lender will verify the property meets its standards — standards that vary by lender. A lender can refuse even a pre-approved buyer, or respond with a lower amount, a higher rate, a larger required down payment, or a co-signer requirement. An appraisal that comes in below the purchase price is the classic version of this: the maximum was calculated against a price the lender has not yet agreed the home is worth.

Maximum is not a target: FCAC warns that the pre-approved amount is the maximum a buyer may get, and suggests looking at properties in a lower price range so the budget is not stretched to its limit — with money still needed for closing costs, moving costs, and ongoing maintenance on top of the purchase.

Pricing the rate hold in dollars

A rate hold is insurance against rates rising while a buyer shops. Its value can be computed instead of guessed. Take a $500,000 mortgage on a 25-year amortization, and compare a held rate of 4.00% with the same mortgage written after a quarter-point rise to 4.25% — payments computed with Canadian semi-annual compounding. The rates are worked examples, not quotes.

Held at 4.00%After a 0.25-point rise, 4.25%Difference
Monthly payment$2,630$2,698$68 a month
Interest paid in the first 5-year term$93,076$99,043$5,968
Balance after 5 years$435,270$437,145$1,876 higher

So on this loan, a single quarter-point move during the shopping window costs roughly $68 a month and about $5,968 of extra interest across the first term. A half-point move roughly doubles that. That is what the hold protects — and it explains the three questions FCAC tells buyers to ask about it: how long the rate is guaranteed, whether the buyer automatically gets the lowest rate if interest rates go down during the hold, and whether the pre-approval can be extended. A hold with a float-down is worth more than one without; a hold that cannot be extended is worth less in a slow search. None of those answers is in the headline rate.

The window where pre-approvals die: a protect-the-file checklist

Between pre-approval and closing, the file that was verified has to stay true. This is a procedure, and it has a success check at the end:

  • Calendar the hold expiry on day one. Count back from 60 to 130 days — whatever the lender actually granted — and treat two weeks before expiry as the deadline to either have an accepted offer or ask about an extension or a fresh hold.
  • Freeze the debt picture. No new car loan, no new credit card, no financing a sofa. New payments change the debt ratios the maximum was calculated from, and a fresh credit inquiry invites questions.
  • Keep employment boring. A job change — especially to a new field, a probationary role, or self-employment — can unwind the income verification. If a change is unavoidable, disclose it to the lender immediately rather than at final approval.
  • Keep the down payment traceable. The statements used at pre-approval established where the money is. Large unexplained deposits, or moving the funds through several accounts, create a paper-trail problem at final approval; gifted funds need the gift documentation the lender expects.
  • Keep a financing condition in the offer unless the lender has confirmed in writing that it can be waived. The condition is the window in which the property itself — appraisal, type, condition — gets verified.
  • Success check: at final approval, the lender confirms the same income, the same debts, the same down payment, and an acceptable property. If all four match the pre-approval file, closing is a paperwork exercise. If one has changed, that is where the delay or refusal appears.

A decision framework: when each step is enough

Early exploring, no timeline: a pre-qualification is enough. It costs nothing, checks nothing, and gives a rough price range for browsing — as long as nobody mistakes it for more than that. Actively shopping, offers plausible within a few months: get the verified pre-approval, in writing, with the hold length, float-down, and extension terms answered. The rate-hold math above is the reason: the protection is worth thousands on a typical loan if rates move during the search. Writing an offer: the pre-approval plus a financing condition is the standard of care; going firm is a deliberate risk decision, not a shortcut. Hold expiring with no purchase: ask about extension first, then a fresh pre-approval — and expect the file to be re-verified, which is one more reason to have kept it unchanged.

FAQ

Does a pre-approval guarantee the mortgage? No. FCAC states this directly, and lists the lender's options on refusal: a lower amount, a higher rate, a larger down payment, or a co-signer. The property still has to pass the lender's standards at final approval.

How long does a rate hold really last? FCAC's range is 60 to 130 days depending on the lender — not a universal 90 or 120. Ask the specific lender, and ask whether the hold can be extended and whether a lower rate during the hold is passed on automatically.

Can a buyer get pre-approved by more than one lender? FCAC encourages talking to several lenders, since rates and conditions differ and some products are only available directly or only through brokers. Each full pre-approval involves a credit check, so sequencing them close together and knowing what each one verified matters more than collecting letters.
