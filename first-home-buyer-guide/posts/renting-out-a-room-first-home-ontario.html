Renting out a room in your first home in Ontario: rules, mortgage and tax basics

After the keys · 8 min read

The common assumption is that anyone who pays to live in your house is a tenant, with the full protection of Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act. For a spare room in the home the owner lives in, that assumption is often wrong. If the occupant has to share a kitchen or a bathroom with the owner, the arrangement can sit completely outside the Act, which changes the notice rules, the dispute route, and the paperwork on both sides. Sorting out which arrangement a first buyer is actually creating comes before the rent amount, because the same room, rented two different ways, runs under two different rule books.

Two arrangements, two rule books

A room in the home you live in, sharing a kitchen or bathroomA self contained unit, such as a legal basement suite
Tenancy lawRoom and board arrangement, excluded from the Residential Tenancies Act when the occupant shares a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or the owner's close family living in the same building (Act, section 5(i))A regular tenancy: a separate unit with exclusive possession and full Act protection, including Landlord and Tenant Board dispute resolution
PaperworkNo standard lease required; the written agreement the two sides sign sets the terms, and putting it in writing is strongly encouragedOntario's standard lease framework and the Act's notice and rent rules apply
Building rulesNo separate unit is created, so no suite construction is involvedA separate unit raises zoning, building code, and fire safety requirements that a room rental does not
Mortgage angleNo published insurer rule in the sources retrieved for this guide treats a shared room as rental income for qualifying; treat any benefit as a question for the lender, not a givenFor an owner occupied home with one legal suite, CMHC lets a lender count up to 100 percent of the suite's gross rent toward qualifying income, and lenders may still count less
Tax angleThe same in both columns: rent is income, reported to the CRA, with expenses split between the personal and rented sharesThe same in both columns: rent is income, reported to the CRA, with expenses split between the personal and rented shares

The exemption has edges worth knowing. Guidance from the Landlord's Self-Help Centre turns on timing as well as sharing: the shared home framing assumes the owner already lives in the property when the occupant moves in, and where a tenant was in place first and the owner moves in later, the Act can still apply despite the shared facilities. Labels do not decide the question either. Calling someone a roommate or a guest does not settle which law governs; the living arrangement does. Where the answer is genuinely unclear, the Centre notes the only sure route is an A1 application asking the Landlord and Tenant Board to determine whether the Act applies.

None of this makes the exempt arrangement lawless. It makes it contractual. Rent, notice, deposits for keys or damage, house rules, and an end date are whatever the written agreement says, which is why Queen's University's Off-Campus Living Advisor guidance encourages getting that agreement in writing even though no formal lease is required. A boarder who is unhappy has contract remedies rather than the Board, and an owner who wants the room back follows the agreement rather than the Act's eviction forms.

The mortgage and insurance questions to ask before, not after

A first buyer's mortgage and home insurance were both written for an owner occupied home, and a paying occupant is a change to that picture. The sources retrieved for this guide publish no insurer rule that counts a single shared room the way a legal suite is counted, so the honest guidance is a set of questions to put in writing before the room is advertised:

  • Ask the lender. Does taking in a boarder who shares the home breach any term of the mortgage or the owner occupancy representation made at approval? Get the answer in writing and keep it with the mortgage documents.
  • Ask the insurer. Does a paying occupant change the home policy, require a rider, or affect liability cover if the occupant or a guest is injured in the home? A policy priced for a single household can treat undisclosed occupants as a material change.
  • Check the incentives file. A qualifying withdrawal from a First Home Savings Account requires intending to occupy the home as a principal residence within one year of buying it. Renting a spare room while living in the home as a principal residence is a different fact pattern from moving out and renting the whole property, and buyers unsure which side of a program condition they sit on should confirm with the CRA or a tax professional before acting.
  • Check the building rules too. Condo declarations and municipal rules can restrict occupants and rentals in ways that sit on top of provincial law. A condo buyer needs the corporation's rules checked before a room is promised to anyone.

Short term stays are a separate topic and are deliberately not covered here. Nightly rental platforms raise municipal licensing questions that differ by city, and the tax rules for short term rentals carry their own compliance tests.

The tax side: rent is income, expenses split by share

Whatever the tenancy status, the CRA treats the money the same way. Rental income can arrive as cash or cheques, in kind, or as services, and it is reported on Form T776, Statement of Real Estate Rentals, with gross rents on line 8141 and gross rental income carried to line 12599 of the return. There is no spare room exception that makes the rent tax free.

Expenses follow the share of the home that is rented. The CRA's rule for renting part of the building you live in is to claim the expenses that relate to the rented area and divide whole property expenses between the personal part and the rented area, splitting by square metres or by the number of rooms. Its own worked example makes the arithmetic concrete: renting 4 rooms of a 10 room house allows 100 percent of the expenses that relate only to the rented rooms, such as repairs to those rooms, plus 40 percent of the expenses that relate to the whole building, such as property taxes and insurance.

For a lodger or roommate, the CRA adds that all expenses for the rented part can be claimed, plus a portion for shared rooms both sides use, using factors such as availability for use or the number of people sharing the room.

The cannot deduct list is short and worth memorizing. Mortgage principal repayments are not deductible, only interest is an expense line. Land transfer tax paid at purchase is not deductible; it is added to the cost of the property. Penalties on an assessment and the value of your own labour cannot be deducted either. And expenses cannot be claimed for renting part of a property where there is no reasonable expectation of profit, which is the line that catches below market arrangements made as a favour. Keep the agreement, the rent records, and the expense receipts together; the split is only as defensible as the paper behind it.

The decision checklist: should this room be rented at all?

  • The arrangement is named correctly. Shared kitchen or bathroom with the owner living in the home points to a room and board arrangement outside the Act; a separate self contained unit points to a full tenancy. If the facts are mixed, the A1 determination route exists.
  • The money still works after tax. The rent is gross income. The comparison that matters is the rent minus the tax on the net rental income and minus any insurance cost increase, not the rent alone.
  • The lender and insurer have answered in writing. Verbal comfort is not a record. Both answers belong in the purchase file.
  • The agreement is written before move in. Rent, payment day, notice period, shared space rules, and how the arrangement ends, signed by both sides while everyone is still friendly.
  • Skip it when: the household budget only balances with the room permanently occupied, the mortgage or insurance answer is no, or the home is a condo whose rules do not allow it. A room that has to stay rented is not a cushion. It is a second obligation.

What success looks like: the occupant moves in under a signed agreement, the tenancy status was determined from the living arrangement rather than assumed, the lender and insurer questions are answered on paper, and the first T776 filing holds no surprises because the rent and the expense split were recorded from month one.

Confirm your situation with a lawyer, your lender and insurer, and the Canada Revenue Agency or a tax professional before renting a room.
