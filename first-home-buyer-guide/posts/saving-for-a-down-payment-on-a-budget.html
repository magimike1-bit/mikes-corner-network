Saving for a Down Payment on a Budget: A Realistic Month-by-Month Plan

Down payment · 6 min read

$556 a month. That is the whole plan, reduced to one number, for the worked example below: a $400,000 home, a minimum down payment, and three years to get there. The price and the timeline are labelled assumptions — change either one and the monthly number changes with it, using the same arithmetic. What does not change is the order of operations: set the target from the minimum-down-payment rules, divide by the months available, then route each month's saving through the accounts in the order that fits a modest income.

Step one: the target comes from the minimum rules

The minimum down payment in Canada is 5% on the first $500,000 of the price, then 5% of the first $500,000 plus 10% of the portion up to $1.5 million, and 20% at $1.5 million and above. On the worked example's $400,000 price, the minimum is a flat 5%:

Target price (assumption)$400,000

Timeline (assumption)36 months

Faster variant: at $667 a monthTarget reached in about 30 months

Two warnings belong beside that table. First, $20,000 is the down payment only. A purchase also needs cash at closing for costs beyond the down payment itself, so a complete plan carries a second savings line for closing costs, sized from a quote for the actual purchase — not folded into the $556 and forgotten. Second, the minimum is a floor, not a recommendation. It is simply the smallest target the rules allow, which is what makes it the right starting point for a tight budget.

Step two: FHSA first, TFSA for the overflow

The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) allows $8,000 of contributions per year up to a $40,000 lifetime limit, deductible going in and tax-free coming out for a qualifying first home. That combination — a deduction on the way in, no tax on the way out — is why the FHSA takes the first dollars in this plan. At $556 a month, annual contributions reach $6,672, inside the $8,000 yearly room, so in the worked example every dollar can sit in the FHSA. Saving at the full $667 a month would max the account's yearly room; anything above that belongs in the next account.

The Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is the overflow account. The packet's source cites TFSA room at $7,000 for 2026. Money that cannot fit the FHSA's yearly room, or that may be needed for the closing-cost line rather than the down payment itself, goes here instead of sitting in a taxed account by default.

An RRSP holding earlier savings is the third bucket, through the Home Buyers' Plan route. Treat it as a check-before-moving item: confirm the current HBP rules and repayment terms before counting RRSP money in the plan, and do not move money into an RRSP purely to chase the plan without that check.

Open the FHSA before there is money to put in it. FHSA room only starts once the account is opened, and carry-forward of unused room is capped at $8,000. A year spent saving in a regular account before opening the FHSA is a year of room that never gets created.

Read the table as a rhythm, not a promise. The transfer leaves on payday, before the month's spending starts, and the amount stays boringly identical. A raise, a tax refund, or a side-income month can shorten the timeline; none of those windfalls should be written into the base plan, because a plan that needs a good month every month is not a plan.

Eligibility is worth one early check, because the FHSA test is specific: it turns on not having lived in a home owned by the buyer or their spouse in the current year or the previous four years. The account also has an end date — it must close by the 15th year after opening, or the year the holder turns 71. Neither fact changes the monthly amount. Both argue for opening the account deliberately, with the purchase timeline in mind.

When the timeline slips

A missed month is arithmetic, not failure. One skipped $556 payment adds roughly one month at the end, or about $16 more per month spread across the remaining 35 months. What actually breaks plans is quieter: the monthly amount gets trimmed a little every few months until the end date drifts out of sight. The honest fixes are the visible ones — extend the date, raise the amount, or lower the target price — chosen on paper, not by drift.

There is also a price-side version of the same choice. Dropping the target from $400,000 to $380,000 lowers the 5% minimum by $1,000, which is nearly two months of saving at $556. Sometimes the fastest month in the plan is a cheaper listing.

Paper trail: the 90 days before anyone asks

  • Keep three months of statements. Lenders generally want three months of statements showing the source of down payment funds. Regular transfers from a pay account into the FHSA or TFSA produce exactly that trail without extra work.
  • Document any gift in writing. Gifted down payment funds require a signed gift letter confirming no repayment is expected. Get the letter when the money arrives, not during the purchase.
  • Keep the accounts separate. Down payment money mixed into daily spending is hard to document and easy to spend. One labelled account per goal — down payment, closing costs — keeps both problems away.

What success looks like: at any point in the three years, one glance shows the running total against the $20,000 target, every month's transfer is visible in statements, and the closing-cost line is growing on its own track instead of waiting as a surprise.

General information only, not personal financial advice. Account limits, eligibility rules, and minimum down payments are set by the CRA and federal regulators and can change — confirm current FHSA, TFSA, and Home Buyers' Plan rules before acting.
