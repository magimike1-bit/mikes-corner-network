Toronto condo fees explained: what first-time buyers are really paying for

Choosing a home · 8 min read

Is this condo fee normal? It is the first question most Toronto buyers ask about a listing, and it has no useful citywide answer. A fee is a share of one specific building's budget. Two units of the same size, on the same street, can carry fees hundreds of dollars apart and both be priced correctly, because the buildings buy different things with the money. What a buyer can do is learn what the fee pays for, check what the building's own documents say, and test whether the payment still fits after several years of increases. That is the order the sections below follow.

What the monthly fee actually pays for

Start with the structure. A condominium owner owns a private unit plus a portion of all the shared common elements, which CMHC lists as lobbies, hallways, elevators, recreational facilities, walkways, gardens, structural elements, and the mechanical and electrical systems. The monthly fee is the owner's share of looking after all of that. In addition to the purchase price, CMHC notes, owners pay a monthly fee to cover the upkeep and replacement of the common elements.

Inside that one payment sit two different kinds of money. The first is operating money: cleaning, concierge and security where the building has them, management, repairs, and utilities in buildings where some utilities are bundled into the fee. The second is reserve money. The Condominium Authority of Ontario (CAO) describes the reserve fund as a distinct fund that can only be used for major repair and replacement of the common elements and assets. It is funded by owners through their common expenses fees, and collecting those contributions is mandatory under section 93(4) of Ontario's Condominium Act, 1998.

That split matters when fees are compared. A building with heat and water inside the fee is not more expensive than a building billing those utilities separately. It is just collecting for them in a different line. CMHC's own affordability question for a condo buyer puts the full stack on the table at once: mortgage payments, condo fees, utilities, insurance premiums, property taxes, and other expenses. Compare the stack, not the fee.

Why the per square foot benchmarks disagree

Buyers usually meet the fee as a per square foot figure, and the figures in circulation do not agree. The sources retrieved for the research packet are market observations from broker and portal sites, not official benchmarks, and no Ontario body publishes an official normal fee. Presented that way, the spread itself is informative:

All three can be accurate and none of them can price a specific listing. The averages blend buildings that bundle utilities with buildings that do not, new buildings with old ones, and full amenity buildings with walk-ups. Parking and a locker also sit outside the headline figure in these sources, commonly an extra $50 to $100 a month for parking and $15 to $25 for a locker. The per square foot number is a first screen for spotting an outlier. It is not a verdict on a building.

The status certificate: where the real answer lives

The document that turns a guess into a check is the status certificate. The CAO calls it a mandatory form under the Condominium Act, and confirms it carries the balance of the reserve fund for the current fiscal year and whether the board anticipates that the fund will or will not be adequate for the major repairs and replacements ahead. CMHC's checklist for a resale condo asks for the same package from the other direction: the corporation's annual operating budget, its financial statements, and the estoppel or status certificate, plus a direct question about how large the reserve fund is. The certificate itself is a section 76 document under the Condominium Act, 1998: the corporation may charge no more than $100 including all applicable taxes for it, and must deliver it within 10 days of a request.

Verified against that guidance on November 3, 2026, a first buyer's read of the package runs in this order:

  1. The unit's own fee and arrears. Confirm the exact common expenses amount for the unit being bought and whether the current owner is behind on payments.
  2. Declared increases and assessments. Look for any increase in common expenses the board has already declared and any special assessment already levied, in the certificate itself rather than in the listing remarks.
  3. The reserve fund balance against the study. The CAO notes that reserve fund studies test whether the fund and its contributions are adequate for expected major repair and replacement costs over time, on a timeline of at least 30 years. A balance is only meaningful beside that study.
  4. The budget and audited statements. These show what the fee is actually buying this year, including which utilities, if any, sit inside it.
  5. Litigation and insurance. Legal actions involving the corporation and the corporation's insurance position both belong in the package and both can become owner costs later.

What success looks like: before an offer firms up, the buyer can state the unit's fee, what it includes, the reserve fund balance, and whether an increase or assessment is already on the record, all from the certificate and its attachments, reviewed with a lawyer.

Low fees: bargain or underfunded reserve?

A low fee deserves the same scrutiny as a high one. Canadian Mortgage Trends warns that unusually low fees can signal an underfunded reserve fund rather than good management. The CAO sets out what underfunding leads to: the corporation may have to borrow the money, levy a special assessment, increase common expenses fees, or combine those steps. One retrieved source put special assessments in older buildings at $5,000 to $30,000, which is a market claim rather than an official figure, but the mechanism behind it is the CAO's own.

The CAO also cautions against judging a reserve fund from one date. A fund can look large right before a roof replacement and small right after one, and both readings can be healthy. Its health, the CAO says, should be judged by its consistent ability to fund expected and unexpected major repairs and replacements. A fee that has barely moved in years while the building aged is not evidence of discipline. It can be a repair bill being handed, with interest in the form of urgency, to whoever owns the unit when the work can no longer wait.

High fees carry their own honest warning. CMHC notes that condo living can mean paying for amenities an owner rarely uses and having less say in deciding when repairs get done. A fee heavy with facilities the buyer will never open is not a bargain at any reserve fund balance.

Stress test: this fee at 3, 5, and 7 percent growth

Retrieved sources disagreed on expected fee growth, citing both 3 to 5 percent and 5 to 7 percent a year in different places, without settling the question. Rather than pick one, run all three against the actual fee. The table below is a worked example, not a forecast: a $500 monthly fee, compounded once a year, with no change in what the fee includes.

Assumed yearly increaseMonthly fee after 5 yearsMonthly fee after 10 years
3 percentabout $580about $672
5 percentabout $638about $814
7 percentabout $701about $983

The decision framework is then short. First, normalize the fee: add back any utilities the building bills separately so two buildings are compared on the same total. Second, verify it in the status certificate, including the reserve position and any declared increase. Third, budget at the middle growth case and confirm the payment still fits at the high case in year five, alongside the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and utilities in CMHC's stack. A condo that only works if its fee never rises is not affordable at the listed fee. It is affordable at a fee nobody is offering.
