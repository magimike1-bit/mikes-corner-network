Double NAT: symptoms, the two-minute check, and the three fixes

Troubleshooting · 8 min read

Double NAT gets blamed for slow internet constantly, and it almost never deserves it. Two routers doing address translation in a chain does not slow ordinary browsing, streaming, email, or video calls in any way most people can measure. Google's own guidance says that for most people a double NAT configuration creates no noticeable effect. So if pages load slowly, this is the wrong suspect. What double NAT actually breaks is a specific, annoying list, and if your problem is on that list, one two-minute check settles whether you have it.

What double NAT breaks, and what it does not

NAT, network address translation, is how one public address serves every device in a house: the router swaps private addresses for the public one as traffic passes. Double NAT is what happens when two devices do that job in a chain, usually the provider's gateway and your own router plugged into it. Outbound traffic survives the double translation fine, which is why everyday use feels normal. Inbound and peer-to-peer traffic is where the second translation bites:

  • A game console reporting a moderate, strict, or double NAT type on its network test.
  • A port forwarding rule that is set correctly on your router, yet the service stays unreachable from outside, because the gateway in front of the router never passes the traffic through.
  • UPnP opening a port on the inner router only, with no matching path through the outer one.
  • Hosting a game, reaching a home camera, NAS, or media server from away, VPN hosting, remote access, or DDNS misbehaving.
  • Devices connected to the gateway's network and devices on your router's network failing to discover each other for casting, printing, or local sharing.

And the negative list matters just as much: double NAT does not automatically explain slow downloads, high ping, jitter, or packet loss. Chasing it as a speed fix wastes an afternoon. One more false suspect to clear: a plain cable modem or a fibre ONT that only converts the signal and hands your router a public address is not a router and creates no double NAT at all.

The two-minute check: read the WAN address

Every router shows the address it received on its internet connection. That one number separates three different situations.

  1. Open your router's status page. Use the maker's app, or log in at the address on the router's label (commonly 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1), and find the Internet or WAN status section.
  2. Read the WAN IP address and match its range:
    • 10.x.x.x, 172.16.x.x to 172.31.x.x, or 192.168.x.x: a private RFC 1918 address. Something in front of your router is routing. That is home double NAT, and the fixes below apply.
    • 100.64.x.x to 100.127.x.x: the shared address space reserved for carrier-grade NAT (RFC 6598). The second translation is happening at the provider, not in your house. No home setting removes it; skip to the decision section.
    • Anything else: a public address. Your router is already the only translator, and double NAT is not your problem.
  3. Photograph or write down the current settings on both the gateway and your router before changing anything, so every step below is reversible.

What success looks like at this stage: you can name which of the three situations you are in from one number, instead of guessing from symptoms.

Three fixes, and what each one switches off

Every real fix does the same thing: it leaves exactly one device routing. They differ in which box keeps the job, and in what you give up. Never put both devices into bridge or access point mode; a home network still needs one device routing, assigning addresses, and running the firewall.

The fix, step by step

Worked for the most common case, an Xfinity gateway with your own router behind it, with the AT&T and AP mode variations marked where they branch.

  1. Confirm the diagnosis first with the WAN address check above. A private WAN address on your router is the green light.
  2. Xfinity: open the gateway Admin Tool. From a device connected to the gateway, go to 10.0.0.1 and sign in. Select Gateway, then At a Glance.
  3. Enable Bridge Mode and click OK on the warning. The gateway restarts. Only one device can connect to it afterwards, on any of its Ethernet ports, and that device must be your router, so plug your router's WAN port into the gateway now.
    AT&T instead: go to 192.168.1.254, open Firewall, then IP Passthrough. Set Allocation Mode to Passthrough and Passthrough Mode to DHCPS-fixed, then enter your router's MAC address in the Passthrough Fixed MAC Address field and save.
  4. Restart in order. Restart the gateway first and wait for its connection light to stabilise, then restart your router. On AT&T, a restart of both boxes may be needed before the router picks up the new address.
  5. AP mode variation: if your gateway offers neither bridge mode nor passthrough, leave the gateway routing and convert your router instead. On a NETGEAR router, sign in at routerlogin.net, select ADVANCED, then Advanced Setup, then Wireless AP, select AP Mode, and click Apply. Other brands place the same setting under an operation mode or AP mode menu.
  6. Re-run the WAN address check on your router, then re-test the thing that was broken: the console network test, the port forward from outside, or the camera from your phone with Wi-Fi off.

What success looks like: after a bridge or passthrough fix, your router's WAN address is a public address, not a 10, 172, or 192.168 one, and the console reports an open or moderate NAT type instead of double NAT, or the forwarded service answers from outside. After an AP mode fix, every device in the house sits on the gateway's single network and can discover the others. If the WAN address still starts with 100.64 to 100.127, the remaining translation is the provider's, and the honest options are to ask the provider about a public address or a static IP, or to accept the limit for inbound connections while everything outbound works normally. To undo the Xfinity change, connect a computer to the gateway by Ethernet, sign in at 10.0.0.1, and set Bridge Mode back to Disable.

Bottom line: which box should route

The decision is simpler than the menu names suggest. If you bought your own router or mesh because you wanted it in charge, bridge the gateway, or use passthrough where bridge mode does not exist, and let your router hold the public address, the firewall, and every rule in one place. If the gateway is newer than the router behind it, or the gateway cannot be bridged, reverse the arrangement: gateway routes, router becomes an access point, and the second network disappears. Either direction fixes the fault. The only wrong answers are leaving both boxes routing once you have proven double NAT is your problem, and bridging both boxes so nothing routes at all.
