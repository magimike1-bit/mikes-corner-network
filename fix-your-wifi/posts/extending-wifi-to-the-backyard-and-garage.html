Extending Wi-Fi to the backyard and garage

Coverage · 8 min read

Every backyard Wi-Fi guide wants to sell a category. Almost none of them start where the problem actually starts: with the router indoors, behind two walls and a refrigerator, beaming its best signal at the front street. Outdoor coverage is a geometry problem before it is a shopping problem. Work the geometry first — it is free — and the shopping, if any is still needed, shrinks to the one option the property actually calls for. One placement fact belongs here because it is so often gotten wrong: kitchens are rarely a good router spot, since the microwave oven sits there interfering, and external antennae do more angled than aligned — some vertical, some tilted — because devices outdoors and downstairs sit at different angles to the router than the laptop on the desk.

Step zero: the free moves, tested properly

  1. Move the test, not the router. Stand where the coverage is wanted — the patio chair, the garage bench — and run a speed test there, then at the house wall nearest that spot, then in the router's room. The three numbers map exactly where the signal dies: wall, distance, or everywhere.
  2. Shift the router toward the yard. If the router can move to a central room or the wall facing the yard without wrecking indoor coverage, move it and repeat the outdoor test. Exterior walls and windows pass signal far better than a signal crossing the whole house first.
  3. Angle the antennae. On routers with adjustable antennae, set them at varied angles rather than all upright — one vertical, one tilted serves devices at yard level and on other floors better than a uniform row.
  4. Check the 2.4 GHz band outdoors. At range, the slower 2.4 GHz band travels farther and through walls better than 5 GHz; a patio that fails on 5 GHz often works acceptably on 2.4 GHz for music, cameras, and browsing.

If the patio test is now usable, stop: the problem was geometry, and it cost nothing. If a detached garage still shows nothing, the signal was never going to make that trip unaided, and the honest options begin.

Two specification anchors keep the table honest. TP-Link's outdoor guide rates an outdoor EAP's omni-directional coverage at roughly a 100-metre (300-foot) radius outdoors — outdoor gear is not a slightly tougher indoor router, it is built for open-air distances — and notes the focused-beam CPE alternative for one targeted area, weatherproof housings on both, and Power over Ethernet carrying power and data on the single cable, which is what makes a wall- or pole-mounted unit practical where no outdoor outlet exists. The detached-garage analysis adds the mesh refinement that matters most: a dedicated mesh node does not halve bandwidth the way an old repeater does, because mesh keeps a separate backhaul channel — and for a thick-walled or distant garage, the hybrid wins: an outdoor node under the garage eave, fed by a short Ethernet run inside the building.

The decision order for a real property

Attached garage or patio within sight of the house wall: extender first, mesh node if the household already runs mesh — the hop is short and the extender's bandwidth penalty is tolerable at that range. Detached garage on the same electrical service: powerline earns a trial before any cable is buried, because the wiring test costs one returnable adapter and an afternoon. Detached structure where streaming, cameras, or a workshop's worth of tools must simply work: skip directly to the wired outdoor access point and spend the effort once, on the cable run, with shielded outdoor cable and a properly grounded PoE injector as TP-Link's guide specifies. Large yard with coverage wanted everywhere rather than somewhere: an outdoor EAP on the wall facing the open space, or a central pole mount for pool-and-lawn properties. The order is deliberately boring — cheapest sufficient option first, permanent infrastructure only where the property proves it necessary — because outdoor networking punishes both underbuying (a summer of dropouts) and overbuying (a buried cable where an adapter would have done).

Security and upkeep once the signal leaves the house

Outdoor coverage changes the network's audience: a signal that used to fade at the exterior wall now reaches the street, the neighbours, and the parked cars. The countermeasures are the same ones this site's security guide sets out indoors — a strong, unique password on the main network, current firmware on every node and access point (outdoor units are the ones owners forget to update, precisely because they are out of sight), and any cameras or smart plugs outdoors placed on the guest network rather than the main one where the household's computers live. Add one outdoor-specific habit: after the install, stand at the street with a phone and check what the property advertises. The goal is a strong signal in the yard and a merely present one at the kerb — and if the kerb signal is the stronger of the two, the unit is mounted facing the wrong way.

Install-and-verify procedure for the chosen option

  1. Mount before you fix permanently: tape or temporarily clamp the unit at the candidate spot — exterior wall facing the space, pole centre-yard, or under the garage eave — and power it by its final method (outlet or PoE) on a trial cable.
  2. Walk the coverage area with a phone, speed-testing at the spots that matter (patio table, garage bench, far fence), not at the unit itself.
  3. Check the awkward corners deliberately: behind the garage, inside it with the door closed, and at the property's far edge — metal doors and vehicles eat signal that open air does not.
  4. Only when the walk passes, make the mounting and cable permanent: outdoor-rated cable, drip loops at entries, shielded cable and grounded injector on PoE runs.
  5. Re-run the walk after a week of weather; rain-soaked foliage and a parked vehicle are the two variables a dry install day hides.

Success looks like this: a video call taken from the garage bench holds without a freeze, the patio streams music at the far fence, and the speed at the spots that matter is a healthy fraction of the indoor speed rather than a rounding error. Outdoor Wi-Fi that merely connects is not success — outdoor Wi-Fi that holds a call is.

FAQ

Will an extender fix a detached garage? Rarely. An extender rebroadcasts the signal it receives; at a detached garage that signal is usually the problem. Powerline or a wired access point addresses the actual hop.

How far can outdoor gear really reach? TP-Link rates an outdoor EAP's omni coverage at about 100 metres (300 feet) radius outdoors — open air is far kinder than walls, which is the entire argument for putting the radio outside.

Is mesh worth it if the house does not have it? As a yard-only purchase it is the expensive route; its value case is a household already on mesh indoors, where one outdoor node extends a system the family already owns.
