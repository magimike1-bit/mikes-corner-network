Router placement that actually matters

Start here · 6 min read

Before you buy anything, move your router. Placement is the single biggest free upgrade in home networking, and most routers sit exactly where the installer left them — next to the modem, behind the TV, or in a far corner of the house. Wi-Fi signal weakens with distance and every wall, floor, and large object between the router and your device. A better spot fixes more problems than any setting.

Central, elevated, open

Wi-Fi spreads outward from the router in all directions, so the ideal spot is as close to the center of your living space as you can manage, roughly at chest height or higher, and out in the open — not inside a cabinet, behind books, or under a desk. Signal that has to punch through a closet door on its way to your bedroom is already losing before it starts. A shelf or bookcase in a hallway often beats a desk in a corner room.

If you live on multiple floors, a spot near the stairs or an open landing lets signal reach both levels more evenly. No single position is perfect for every room, so aim for the spot that is fair to everyone rather than perfect for one room.

The signal killers in your house

Some common household items and structures block or interfere with Wi-Fi more than you'd expect:

  • Metal and mirrors. Metal filing cabinets, large appliances, and mirrors reflect Wi-Fi signal. Keep the router out from behind them.
  • Dense walls. Brick, concrete, and plaster walls absorb far more signal than drywall. If your router and your desk are on opposite sides of a brick fireplace wall, that wall is your problem.
  • Microwave ovens. Microwaves operate near the same 2.4 GHz frequencies as older Wi-Fi bands, and a running microwave can briefly disrupt nearby wireless connections. Don't park the router on top of one.
  • Fish tanks and water. Water absorbs Wi-Fi signal well. A large aquarium between the router and your office is a surprisingly effective signal blocker.
  • Other electronics. Baby monitors, cordless phones, and crowded power strips with lots of cables add noise. A little separation helps.

Antenna basics

If your router has external antennas, stand them upright for typical same-floor coverage — most router antennas are designed to spread signal horizontally. If you need coverage on another floor, try angling one antenna sideways. And don't stack things on top of the router: it needs airflow, and overheating routers behave exactly like broken ones.

Move your router in 15 minutes

Grab your phone and a pen. Do this once, properly:

  1. Test before you touch anything. Stand in each room you care about and run a speed test. Write down the numbers, room by room. This is your "before."
  2. Pick the new spot. As close to the center of the house as practical, at least chest height (a hallway shelf is ideal), out in the open, and away from the signal killers above — metal, mirrors, microwaves, brick walls, fish tanks.
  3. Move it and stand the antennas upright. Keep antennas vertical for same-floor coverage; angle one sideways if you need signal upstairs or downstairs. Leave space around the router for airflow.
  4. Wait a minute, then re-test. Give the router a moment to settle, then run the speed test in the same rooms, in the same spots.
  5. Compare the numbers. If the weak rooms improved, the move worked — lock it in. If a different room is now the worst one, try a small adjustment and test again.

What success looks like: numbers, not feelings. The before/after sheet tells you whether this fixed it; a room that went from 8 Mbps to 40 Mbps is a solved room.

Test before and after

Move the router, then walk around with your phone and run a speed test in each room (any free speed-test app or website works). Write down the numbers before and after the move so you're comparing reality, not feelings. If the far room went from 8 Mbps to 60 Mbps, the problem was never your internet plan — it was geography.

The two-minute version: central location, about chest height, out in the open, away from metal, mirrors, microwaves, and thick walls. This one move solves more Wi-Fi complaints than everything else on this site combined.
