Negotiating with Marketplace buyers

Reselling · 8 min read

Negotiation on Facebook Marketplace is not a personality contest. It is a numbers problem wearing a chat window. The sellers who come out ahead are rarely the wittiest talkers — they are the ones who decided, before the listing ever went live, exactly how low they can go, and then ran the conversation inside that fence. Everything below follows from one move: know your floor.

Set your floor before anyone messages you

Your floor is not a feeling. It is your all-in cost plus the minimum profit that makes the item worth your shelf space and your evening. All-in means the whole stack: hammer price, buyer's premium, tax, any per-lot fee, your share of the pickup trip, and a realistic allowance for the selling costs. (Our guide to pricing for quick resale works backwards from resale price the same way.) Write the floor down where you track the item. When a message arrives, you are not deciding anything under pressure — you are comparing one number to another number you already chose with a clear head.

This is also what keeps negotiation fast. Common handling advice for lowball offers starts with evaluating the offer against your price and how long the item has been listed — that evaluation takes ten seconds if the floor is already on paper, and ten anxious minutes if it is not.

Price with room to give some back

Marketplace buyers expect to haggle; many treat the listed price as an opening bid. One seller who documented 457 of their own listings totalling $12,060 in asking prices closed $6,827 in actual sales — and, just as instructive, threw out or donated 173 listings, 38% of them, that never sold at all. That seller's method: mark an absolute minimum privately, then list 25–50% above it, so the haggling the platform guarantees lands on a number that still works. You do not need to copy the percentages, but the structure is sound — the discount the buyer "wins" should be money you budgeted to give, not money you needed to keep.

The same seller's data carries the second lesson: more than a third of listings never sold. Negotiation skill cannot rescue an item nobody wants at any price, and a stale listing is information — after a few weeks, a firmer price cut or a better channel usually beats another month of defending the number.

Triage the offer: serious, fishing, or wasting your evening

That documented seller sorts buyers into three types, and any experienced Marketplace seller will recognize them: ghosts, who tap "Is this available?" and vanish; questioners, who ask a dozen questions and then vanish; and deserters, who agree to a price and a time, then never show up. Triage accordingly:

  • A real number, near your range, with a pickup time attached — a serious buyer. Reply fast and move to close. Buyers message several sellers at once, so a quick, concrete reply is itself a negotiating tool.
  • A number far below your floor, with nothing else — fishing. One short counter (below) is worth it; a debate is not.
  • "What's your lowest?" with no offer — a longtime classifieds seller's classic answer is to hand the question back: "What's your highest offer?" It costs you nothing and makes the buyer name the first real number.
  • Anything involving overpayment, gift cards, couriers the buyer arranges, or pressure to move off Messenger — not a negotiation. End it. Facebook's own guidance: if you see signs of suspicious activity, cancel the transaction, stop communicating, and report the person.

The counter: once, with a reason, with a deadline

A counteroffer works when it reads as a decision, not an invitation to keep pushing. Three parts, one message:

  1. The number. One counter, meaningfully above the offer and at or above your floor. Moving in tiny steps teaches the buyer that every "no" is soft.
  2. A reason. A short, true anchor: the item is tested and working, it includes the accessories others sell separately, a part was replaced. Reasons are where honest preparation pays — an item you checked before listing, with any scuffs visible in the photos, gives you facts to point at instead of adjectives to defend. A buyer arguing against a disclosed, photographed flaw has very little leverage.
  3. A deadline. "I can do $65 if you can pick up by tomorrow evening — after that it goes back to the listed price / to the next buyer." A deadline converts your counter from an opening position into a closing one.

If the buyer accepts, confirm the full agreement in the thread — item, price, time, place — before anyone drives anywhere. If they counter below your floor, decline once, politely, and stop: "I can't go that low, but thanks for asking — the listing stays up if your budget changes." Then let it go. Your floor was the decision; the chat is just delivery.

Holds, no-shows, and deposits

The most expensive word in local selling is "hold." An unpaid hold takes your item off the market for exactly the buyer type — the deserter — most likely to waste it. Working rules:

  • Short holds are free; long holds are not. Holding until tonight costs little. Holding until Saturday is a deposit conversation.
  • If you take a deposit, state the terms in the thread before money moves: the amount, how it is paid, and the exact date and time the hold expires and the deposit is forfeit or returned. Vague deposits create the worst arguments in local selling.
  • Never mark the item sold until you are actually paid. "Pending pickup" in the title is honest; "sold" before money changes hands just hides your listing from real buyers.
  • After a no-show, one grace message, then release. "Are you still coming? I'll hold it until 7 — after that I'll let it go to the next person." Then do exactly that. Repeat deserters get no second hold.

The meetup is part of the negotiation

Deals die at the meetup when the item is not what the chat promised — which is why the strongest negotiation move happens before the buyer arrives: test or check the item one more time, and make sure it matches the listing exactly. A buyer who finds a surprise at the handoff has just been handed a reason to re-open the price, and they will use it. A buyer who finds exactly what was photographed has nothing to argue with.

For the meetup itself, follow Facebook's published safety guidance: meet in a public, well-lit area — a police station is their own example — and tell someone where you are going. Marketplace builds this in: from the Messenger thread you can create a meetup plan with the method, time, and location, and share it with a trusted friend or family member; Facebook notes the feature covers local pickup transactions, not shipped checkout orders. Agree the price in the thread beforehand and say so at the handoff: "We landed at $65 — good?" Renegotiating on the driveway is a tactic; naming the agreed number first removes it.

FAQ

Should I ever accept a lowball offer? Yes — when it is still above your floor, or when the item has sat long enough that storage and staleness cost more than the discount. The floor decides, not pride. An offer below the floor on a fresh, well-priced listing gets one counter and a polite no.

Is "price is firm" a good strategy? It filters messages but also filters buyers — the documented seller data above suggests most Marketplace sales close below asking. A small, budgeted cushion usually outsells a firm price. Firm works best on items priced demonstrably under the going rate, where the listing itself is the argument.

How do I stop wasting evenings on no-shows? Confirm in the thread an hour before, keep unpaid holds short, take deposits for long holds with written terms, and release the item after one grace message. Success looks like this: every negotiation ends in a confirmed agreement, a clean decline, or a released item — never an open-ended maybe.
