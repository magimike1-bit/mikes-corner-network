Photos and listings that actually sell

Reselling · 6 min read

You can win the lot, price it perfectly, and still watch it sit unsold — because the listing did the item no favors. Buyers of secondhand goods can't touch what they're buying, so your photos and words are doing all the work. The good news: clear photos and honest descriptions are free, and they reliably separate listings that sell from listings that linger.

A photo setup that takes five minutes

You don't need a studio. You need daylight, a plain background, and a clean item. Here's the setup that works for most small items:

  1. Clean the item first. Wipe it down. A dusty item reads as neglected; a clean one reads as cared for. This is the highest-return step on this list.
  2. Use daylight. Photograph near a window or outside in open shade. Daylight shows true color and texture; yellow indoor lighting makes everything look tired.
  3. Plain background. A white sheet, a clean table, a swept floor. Clutter in the frame makes buyers distrust the listing.
  4. Fill the frame. Get close enough that the item fills most of the photo. Buyers pinch-zoom on phones; start them close.
  5. Skip the filters. Editing that changes how the item looks causes returns and disputes. Crop and rotate if you must; don't change colors or hide flaws.

Show what a buyer would check in person

Think about what you'd inspect if you could hold the item, and photograph those things: the front, back, sides, and bottom; screens powered on if the item has one; labels and model plates; and — this is the one that builds trust — the flaws. A close-up of the scratch, the worn corner, the missing button. Photographing a flaw doesn't scare buyers away; it proves you're honest, and honest listings get bought.

For items that come with accessories or packaging, lay it all out and include it in the shots. Buyers pay more for complete sets, and a photo of everything included preempts half the questions you'd get in messages. If something in the photos isn't included, say so plainly in the description — better than a disappointed buyer at your door.

Most marketplaces let you upload many photos; use them. Six to eight clear shots from different angles beat two artsy ones. Your first photo is the thumbnail that decides whether anyone clicks at all — make it the cleanest, best-lit overall shot.

Writing a description that answers everything

Buyers message when the description leaves them guessing. A good description answers every reasonable question up front:

  • What it is — brand, model, and key specs. Copy these from the item's label, not from memory.
  • Condition — be specific. "Used, works well, light scratches on the top" beats "good condition" every time.
  • What's included — everything in the box or bundle. If it originally came with a charger and you don't have it, say so.
  • How you tested it — "powered on and ran for 10 minutes" reassures more than "works."
  • Why you're selling — one honest line ("bought at auction, tested, works fine") tells the buyer there's nothing wrong with it.
  • Pickup and payment — your area, available times, and how you'll take payment.

Keep the tone plain and factual. Superlatives ("AMAZING DEAL!!!", "MUST SEE") read as pressure, and pressure makes secondhand buyers cautious. Let the photos and the facts do the persuading.

The honesty dividend

Here's the uncomfortable truth about secondhand selling: the seller who discloses the flaw sells faster than the seller who hides it. Hidden flaws surface at pickup or after delivery, and they come back as cancelled sales, bad ratings, or refund demands — all of which cost more than the flaw was ever worth.

Describe every defect you know about, price accordingly, and sleep well. Your listing is a promise; keeping it builds the reputation that makes your next fifty sales easier. Auction finds are already cheap inventory — you don't need to squeeze extra dollars out by hiding the scratch.
