Reselling used bicycles from auctions: measure it, brake-test it, and price the service before you list

Reselling · 9 min read

What is a used bicycle worth if the listing cannot say what size it is, whether the brakes stop it, or whether it is recalled? At auction that question arrives before the hammer, not after. A bicycle is a safety product with a service bill attached, and the resellers who make money on them treat the check, the measurement, and the service estimate as part of the bid — not as surprises discovered in the garage afterwards.

Before you bid: the recall and photo gate

Start with the gate that can end the deal outright. In Canada, the Canada Consumer Product Safety Act prohibits recalled products from being redistributed, sold, or even given away. Health Canada's recall database carries bicycle recalls in which the instruction is to stop using the bike immediately and take it to an authorized dealer for a free repair — a recalled fork on one Cannondale cyclocross model is the example in the sources checked for this guide on October 29, 2026. So the model name in the lot photo gets checked against the recall database before a bid ceiling is set. A hit does not mean a bargain with a free repair attached. It means the lot is out, unless the recall remedy is already documented.

Then read the photos the way a buyer will. Unclear frame size and vague listings with no specifications are listed among the things that slow a bicycle sale, and poor photos of the drivetrain and frame do the same. An auction lot that hides the drivetrain side, the frame joints, and the tyre sidewalls is asking the bidder to pay for information the seller should have provided. Discount for that, or pass.

The bench check, in order

Run the check the same way every time, at pickup or as soon as the bike is home, and record the result per bike:

  1. Brakes first. Squeeze each lever, roll the bike, and confirm it stops. Brake cables and housing should be intact and the pads should not be worn out. A bike that does not stop is not listed until that is fixed.
  2. Bearings. The bearings live in the headset, the bottom bracket, and the wheels. Spin each wheel, turn the bars, and rock the cranks. Healthy bearings are not seized, offer no resistance as they spin, and do not creak.
  3. Shifting and wheels. Shift across all the gears, check the headset and bottom bracket for play, and confirm the wheels run true enough with hubs spinning smoothly.
  4. Tyres. Check for wear and cuts, then inflate to the pressure marked on the sidewall and look again for worn spots or cracks that only show under pressure.
  5. Frame, up close. Clean the bike before judging it — never with a pressure washer, which forces water where it does not belong — and re-lubricate the chain after washing. Look along the tubes and joints for dents, cracks, or repair paint, and remove personal stickers that add nothing for the next owner.

Success looks like this: every line above has a written pass or a written fault, and any fault has a price attached from a shop quote or a parts listing you have actually seen. A fault with no price is not a detail. It is an open cost.

Measure it before you describe it

Size is the specification buyers filter on, and "medium, I think" is not a specification. Use the measurement a buyer can repeat:

Field in the listingHow it is verifiedWhy it matters
Frame sizeMeasure from the centre of the bottom bracket to the centre of the top tube and seat tube intersection; list it in that form, for example as a measured number with "measured centre to centre" beside itThe buyer can check the same two points on arrival; a disputed size becomes a measurement, not an argument
Standover heightMeasure from the ground to the top of the top tubeA second fit check a buyer can compare against a bike that already fits them
ComponentsName the brand and model of the components and wheels, taken from the parts themselvesComponent level is much of what separates two otherwise similar used bikes on price
DefectsList defects, missing parts, and problems explicitlyDisclosed faults hold a price; discovered faults reopen it

Price the service before you set the ceiling

The honest math for an auction bicycle has one extra line that a board game does not: the service bill. Cables, a chain, and other wearable parts are the usual replacement candidates, and having the bike checked by a bicycle mechanic is the step that confirms it is safe and in correct working order. Get that quote before the auction closes, from a shop you would actually use, and run the ceiling like this:

  • Ceiling sale price: the price supported by sold listings for the same model, size, and component level — your estimate, labelled as such, never a promise.
  • Minus: your all-in auction cost (hammer, buyer's premium, tax, and pickup), the mechanic's quote, any wearable parts, and the selling channel's fees.
  • Equals: the most the hammer should reach. If the bidding passes it, the service bill is being paid out of your margin, and then out of your pocket.

If the wearable parts are not replaced, the listing says so. Honest disclosure is cheaper than a return trip and a buyer who feels misled.

Walk away, part it out, or list it

Three outcomes, decided by the record from the bench check. A bike that passes, measures cleanly, and clears the ceiling math gets listed whole, locally first — a bicycle is bulky, and a local buyer who can stand over the frame removes the size dispute entirely. A bike with a sound frame but a failed drivetrain or wheels becomes a judgement call: price the repair honestly, and if the quote consumes the margin, sell it explicitly as needing work, at a price that reflects that, or do not sell it at all. A recalled bike, a cracked frame, or a bike that cannot be made to stop properly is not inventory. It is a liability with a price tag, and the kill criterion is simple: if the safety faults cannot be fixed and documented inside the ceiling math, the lot is passed — before the bid, not after the pickup.
