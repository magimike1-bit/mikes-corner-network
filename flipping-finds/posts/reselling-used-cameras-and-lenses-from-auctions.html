Reselling used cameras and lenses from auctions: the glass decides, the shutter count prices

Reselling · 9 min read

200,000 and 500,000. Those are the shutter ratings a retailer buying guide gives for two Canon bodies, the EOS R6 Mark II and the EOS R5, and they explain the whole category. A camera body is a machine with a counted life, sold attached to a lens whose value lives or dies on the state of its glass. Auction lots show you neither number. You bid from photographs, as-is, and the verification that a private buyer does in a shop with a returns policy behind them happens on your bench, after the money is gone. This guide is built for that order of events.

The opportunity in one paragraph

Photographers pay for used bodies and lenses because new glass is expensive and good lenses outlast the bodies they were sold with. That is who pays, and why. What they pay for is verified condition: a shutter count they can trust, a lens they can see through, test shots that prove the pair works. An auction lot offers none of that verification, which is exactly the discount you are buying. Your margin is the gap between the unverified price and the verified price, minus everything the verification costs you.

Two numbers that set the ceiling before you bid

The shutter count is the body's mileage. The mechanical shutter is a moving part with a finite rated life, every model carries its own rating, and shooting on the electronic shutter adds no wear to the figure. A count near the rating does not mean the body dies tomorrow. It means the buyer pool prices in a future repair, and so should your bid.

The second number is the one you cannot get. Some bodies write the count into each photo's metadata where reader tools can find it. Others refuse: the EOS R5 is a documented case of a body that does not embed the count in EXIF data, so upload tools fail and the count needs an app and a USB connection, and even then may only read to the nearest 1,000. Canon's own support page for bodies with a system status display confirms the shape of the problem: the screen shows shutter release cycles in units of 1,000, a rounded figure by design. And one trap to refuse outright: the file number on a photo is not the shutter count. It rolls over and can be reset, so a low file number in a lot photo proves nothing. Bid rule that follows: a lot whose count you cannot verify gets priced as a high-count body. If the seller of the lot wanted the low-count price, the count would be in the listing.

Now fail the lens. Fungus inside the glass sends the lens to a specialist clean at best and to a parts listing at worst, and the combined price line falls by roughly the lens's whole share. That single failure is why camera lots get bid as two products with two ceilings, never as one hopeful kit price.

The verification runbook after pickup

  1. Identify before you clean. Read the model and serial numbers off the body and the lens barrel and record them. A wrongly identified model is one of the things that cuts offers, and your listing will carry the numbers you read, not the lot's guess.
  2. Read the shutter count the verifiable way. Where the body offers a system status display, read the release cycles there. Where the model writes the count into photo metadata, read it from an unedited file. Where neither works, the count is unverified, and the listing says so in those words rather than borrowing a number from the file counter.
  3. The light test for every lens. Shine a bright light through the lens from both ends, at an angle, aperture open. Fungus shows as thin threads or a spider's web pattern inside the glass. Haze is an even, milky fog across an element. A few specks of internal dust are normal on a lens several years old and rarely show in photos, so dust alone does not fail a lens. Fungus does, on sight.
  4. Quarantine a fungus lens immediately. Mould can spread to other optics, so a failed lens goes in a sealed bag, away from clean inventory, the moment it fails. It is never stored on the shelf with saleable glass while you think about it.
  5. Aperture and autofocus, working checks. Shoot fully open and stopped down in aperture priority and confirm the exposure and depth of field actually change. On older lenses, look for oil on the aperture blades, which can spatter onto the optics and soften images. Autofocus on a near subject, then a far one, repeatedly: smooth and quiet passes, hunting or grinding gets written down as a fault.
  6. Test shots as evidence. A dropped lens can be knocked out of alignment and go soft, so shoot test frames and inspect sharpness at different apertures. Keep the good frames. They go in the listing, because a buyer paying verified prices wants proof, not adjectives. Finish with the small sweep: every button, dial, port, and card slot, plus the battery health reading where the body shows one, since a tired battery is a replacement cost the buyer will price in if you do not.

Success looks like this: the listing states a shutter count you actually read, shows a test shot the actual lens took, and names every fault the runbook found. A buyer can check all three claims in the first minute of owning the gear.

Price the body and the lens as separate products

Pricing method, from the used-camera pricing sources: work from what buyers recently paid, not from asking prices. Pull a handful of sold listings for the exact model in the same condition, throw out the extremes, and price inside the range that remains. Then adjust: shutter count, battery health, glass condition, and accessories all move the figure, and a high count is one of the common reasons offers come in lower.

Splitting a kit is not always right. A body and lens that verify cleanly and photograph well together can sell as a ready-to-shoot kit to a first-time buyer who values completeness over the last dollar. The decision rule is arithmetic, not habit: split when the separate sold ranges, minus the second set of fees, shipping, and listing time, beat the kit range by enough to matter.

Risks, and who should not bid on camera lots

Skip this category if you have no camera of your own to mount auction lenses on for testing, no way to read a shutter count for the brands you bid on, and no parts route for failed glass. The risks are specific: a shutter near the end of its rated life, fungus that turns a lens into a cleaning bill, a body whose count cannot be verified at any price, and provenance you cannot check on grey-market gear if a buyer later needs a repair. None of those risks is a reason to avoid the category. All of them are reasons the bid, not the listing, is where they get paid for.

Kill criterion

Per unit: a lens with fungus is never listed as clean glass and never stored with clean inventory, and a body whose count you could not read is never described with a count. Per category: track the verification pass rate on your last ten camera lots. If fewer than half the pieces pass the runbook, the problem is the supply, and the correct move is to stop bidding that source, not to loosen the runbook until the numbers look better.
