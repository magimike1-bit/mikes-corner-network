Reselling used exercise equipment from auctions: pass first, test what remains, and sell it locally

Reselling · 9 min read

The misconception that costs money in this category is that exercise equipment is one category. It is at least three. A dumbbell set has no motor, no belt, and no account attached to it. A connected treadmill has all three, plus a shipping weight that decides the sale before the listing is written: the NordicTrack Commercial 1750, the anchor model used in this guide, ships in a box weighing 311.5 lb, and the retailer selling it new delivers curbside only, with a note that its delivery vendors will not bring the item into the home. Flipping guides quote cheerful per-unit ranges for the whole category at once. An auction buyer cannot afford to. The pass comes first.

Pass first: the lots that end the conversation

These are passes at any hammer price, decided before the auction opens:

  • A treadmill with no safety key and no way to test the emergency stop. Treadmill manuals are consistent on this point: the machine is not to be used before the safety key clip is secured to clothing, and the emergency stop procedure is to be read, understood, and tested before use. A unit whose stop cannot be demonstrated cannot be honestly listed as working.
  • A connected machine still tied to a previous owner's account, where the lot gives no way to clear it. On the 1750, the trainer-led iFIT content and connected features require a compatible paid membership and an internet connection. A buyer is pricing the screen and the membership path, not just the belt. If the console is locked to someone else's account, the reseller is selling a smaller machine than the lot title describes.
  • Grinding, squealing, or a belt that slips under load on a machine priced as working. One flipping guide for this category puts it plainly: treadmills run 150 to 300 plus pounds, they require testing before purchase, and motor and belt issues can be expensive to repair. Expensive is the operative word. A motor fault on a machine bought as a quick turn is not a project, it is the margin leaving.
  • Anything the crew and the vehicle cannot move twice. The machine has to be moved at pickup and moved again at sale, and the buyer has to get it into their home from the curb, the same constraint the new retailer imposes. A lot that needs a rented truck both ways has a transport bill that belongs in the math before the bid, and for most single treadmills it ends the math.
  • Commercial gym equipment in a residential operation. Different buyers, different warranty expectations, different moving requirements. It is a real market, and it is not this one.

What survives is narrower and better: free weights and dumbbell sets with no moving parts to fail, exercise bikes that power up and clear their accounts, and treadmills from known lines, NordicTrack Commercial series, Sole, and Life Fitness commercial grade are the names one category guide rates strongest, that pass the bench test below.

What survives the pass: the bench test

Run the test after pickup, in order, and record the result per machine. Testing an item before a buyer ever sees it is what keeps an unhappy buyer from becoming a refund.

  1. Power and console. Plug in, power on, and confirm the console lights, the display reads cleanly, and every button responds. Unplug the machine before any cleaning or maintenance, per the manual.
  2. Safety key and emergency stop. Insert the safety key, start the belt at its lowest speed from beside the machine, never standing on the belt, then pull the key and confirm the belt stops. This is the demonstration a buyer will ask for, so it happens on the bench first.
  3. Belt under load. Walk the belt at low speed, then jog it briefly. Listen for grinding or squealing, and watch for slipping or drifting to one side. A slipping belt can mean loose tension, and the manual warns that overtightening can damage the motor, belt, and roller, so adjust in small turns and retest.
  4. Speed and incline through the range. Run the machine up and down its speed range and through its incline range. On the 1750 that range is 0 to 12 mph and minus 3 percent decline to 12 percent incline. A function that is not tested is a function the listing does not claim.
  5. Belt condition and lubrication. Check the belt surface for fraying and cracks. Belt lubrication is silicone oil applied under the belt; the manual schedule for light use, under 3 hours a week, is about once every 5 months. A dry, neglected belt is a cost to record, not a detail to hide.
  6. Account and reset. Sign out of any prior account the console still holds, reset the machine, and confirm it boots to a clean setup. Note the iFIT membership position in the listing: the connected features need a paid membership, and the buyer should learn that from the listing, not from the first workout.

Success looks like this: the emergency stop is demonstrated, the belt runs true under load, every claimed function was exercised on the bench, and the listing matches the machine, scuffs and all, in words and in photos.

Price a used unit off sold listings for the same model and condition, never off the new retail anchor and never off the retail claim in an auction lot, which is unreliable by design. The anchor matters for one thing: it tells the reseller which machines were built to a price worth testing carefully, and which were built cheaply enough that a fault ends them.

One sale price, two channels: the fee math

Bulky exercise equipment is a local sale in practice, and the fee schedules show why. Take one sale price, $800, with local pickup in both cases so the comparison is fee against fee:

ChannelFee on an $800 local pickup saleBasis

eBay Canada, local pickup$109.2013.6 percent of the total amount of the sale, plus the $0.40 per-order fee on orders over $10

The eBay fee is calculated on the total amount of the sale, which includes any shipping collected and sales tax, so a shipped treadmill would cost more in fees on top of the freight itself. One category guide's advice matches the math: sell bulky exercise gear locally, pickup only, and do not ship. eBay still earns its place for the parts of this category that travel well, weight plates and small components, where the wider audience can outweigh the fee. For the machine itself, the $109.20 is a second transport bill, charged by the channel.

For contrast, one category guide's worked example buys a NordicTrack Commercial 2950 for $600 and resells it for $1,100, presented as $500 of profit. That is the publisher's example, not a verified result, and it arrives before the guide's own warning that motor and belt repairs are expensive. Treat every such example, including the table above, as arithmetic to test against sold listings, not as an expectation. Seasonality helps the seller who plans for it: treadmills peak in January, in resolution season, which is an argument for testing and listing in December rather than discovering a belt fault in the peak week. What the data on unsold stock teaches, season after season, is to watch the unsold count first: promote the older listings, fix the photos, and reprice before the peak passes.

Kill criterion

Two dated lines. One: a machine that fails the bench test on motor, belt, or emergency stop, and cannot be made to pass with the parts allowance already in its math, is sold honestly as a parts or repair unit within 14 days of pickup, or not bought at all the next time its twin appears. Two: a tested treadmill listed for the January peak and still unsold on February 28 gets repriced to its break-even and moved, because the next peak is eleven months away and a 300 lb machine charges rent in space every week it stays.
