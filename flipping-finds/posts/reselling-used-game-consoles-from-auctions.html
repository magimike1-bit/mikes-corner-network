Reselling used game consoles from auctions: test it, reset it, and sell the bundle you actually have

Reselling · 9 min read

A console that powers on has passed almost nothing. The screen lighting up tells a buyer nothing about the disc drive, the controller sticks, the account still signed in to it, or whether the storage is the size the lot description claimed. Auction consoles fail in the details, so this guide starts where the money is lost: with the reasons to walk away.

Who should not bid, and the kill criterion

Skip auction consoles if you have no screen, controller, and cables of your own to test with, no time to run a full reset before listing, or no route for a parts-only sale. A reseller who can only sell working units has no exit for the unit that fails, and failed units are a normal part of this category.

The kill criterion, applied per unit before it is listed: if the console cannot be reset to a clean sign-in screen, if it will not hold a network sign-in under your own account, or if a safety fault shows — a damaged power supply, a swollen battery in a handheld, heat or a burning smell under load — it does not get listed as a working console. Parts-only, with the fault named, or not at all. A second rule sits beside it: if two or more of the bench tests below fail on the same unit, stop spending time on it. Testing time is a cost, and it does not come back.

Restore is not reset: what each platform actually deletes

The most common preparation error is choosing the soft option. On PlayStation, restoring default settings returns visual and audio settings to their factory versions and does not erase user or game data. Resetting the console is the different, stronger action: it deletes user and game data and personalised settings, it cannot be undone, and deleted data cannot be restored. The reset path on a PS5 runs through Settings, System, System Software, then Reset Options.

One Switch detail belongs in the listing notes: software saved on a microSD card, apart from screenshots, becomes unusable after the console is initialized, even though the data on the card is not deleted. A card bundled with a reset Switch is storage for the buyer's own downloads, not a library of games, and the listing should say exactly that.

The bench test, in order

  1. Read the label before the listing copy. Check the model number on the console itself against what the lot claimed. Listings get storage wrong: one second-hand guide documents a console described as 1TB and another called 500GB when both models shipped with 825GB. Trust the label, not the listing.
  2. Picture and sound. Connect it and confirm a clean picture. A dead or flickering output is one of the common faults in a used unit, and it ends the test early.
  3. Fan under load. Some fan noise under load is normal. Grinding, rattling, or a fan running very loud at the home screen is a fault sign, not a quirk.
  4. Disc drive, if it has one. Insert a disc, confirm it reads, and eject it. Clicking or grinding on the way in or out is recorded as a fault.
  5. Controller, every input. Leave the sticks alone and watch for the cursor or camera drifting on its own. Then press every button, both triggers, and the touchpad where one is fitted. Plug the controller in by cable as well — that checks that the ports charge it.
  6. Network and storage. Sign in with your own account and download a system update. That confirms the console is not banned from the network and that the Wi-Fi and storage actually work.
  7. Reset, then test the first screen again. Run the full reset for the platform from the table above, restart, and confirm the console arrives at a clean set-up or sign-in screen with no previous account present.

Success looks like this: the buyer powers the console on, reaches set-up, signs in with their own account, and finds exactly the bundle the listing described — nothing of a previous owner, and nothing promised that is not in the box.

Sell the bundle you actually have

A complete sale normally includes the console, a power cable, an HDMI cable, and a controller, with a stand where the model uses one. Price from completeness, not from hope:

  • Complete and reset: list as a tested, complete bundle, with the bench results stated.
  • Working, incomplete: name the missing piece in the title line, not the fine print, and price the unit as the buyer will experience it — as a console they still have to spend on before they can play.
  • Untested or failed: parts-only, fault named, no working language anywhere in the listing.

The honest math line

No fee percentage is quoted here, on purpose: the rate for the channel you use is the rate on that channel's own fee page on the day you list, and it belongs in your calculation as a line you filled in, not a number remembered from a guide. The calculation itself is short. Expected sale price, minus all-in auction cost, minus a replacement controller or cable where the bundle needs one, minus channel fees and shipping, minus your testing time valued honestly. If that line is not positive on a complete, reset unit, the category is not the problem — the bid was.

General information only, not financial advice. Reset paths and menu names are as published by the platforms and can change with system updates; follow the console maker's current support page for your model. Resale results vary and no profit is promised or guaranteed.
