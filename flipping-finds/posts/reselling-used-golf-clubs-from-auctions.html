Reselling used golf clubs from auctions: verify the spec on the shaft before you price the set

Reselling · 8 min read

The value of a used golf club sits in its specification: the model, the loft printed on the head, the shaft fitted to it, and the flex marked on that shaft. A buyer pays for those four facts and for the condition of the head, shaft, and grip. An auction lot, though, is usually photographed as a bag of clubs and described in a single line — which model, how many clubs, what shaft, what flex, and whether anything has been shortened, lengthened, or reshafted are all left unanswered. The reseller's job is to answer those questions club by club before a price exists. A lot that survives that audit is a product. A lot that does not is a guess with a shipping cost.

The opportunity, in one paragraph

Golfers replace individual clubs and whole sets constantly, and a used buyer pays to avoid the new price while still getting a known model in a known specification. That is who pays, and why: the specification is the product. Auction lots supply the clubs cheaply because the specification work has not been done — mixed brands in one bag, a replaced shaft here, a missing wedge there. Doing that work, and publishing it in the listing, is the whole margin in this category.

Verify the spec before you trust the lot photo

Work through every club with the same record. Photograph each club number and its shaft label — for a set you are buying from photos, those are the pictures to request, along with clear shots of the faces, soles, shafts, and grips. For a driver, confirm the model and the loft, and confirm whether what is being sold is the complete club or only the head.

Inspect club by club

  1. Heads. Look for dents, scratches, rust, cracks, or chips, and note and document anything found. A cracked head is not a cosmetic issue.
  2. Shafts. Sight down each shaft for kinks or bends, which affect performance. A bent or twisted shaft may need replacement before the club can be sold as playable.
  3. Grips. Check for tears or cracks in the grip material and whether the grip is still securely attached. A worn or slippery grip is a fixable cost; an unlisted one is a complaint.
  4. Clean last, after the record. Clean each club thoroughly, paying attention to the head and the grip — after the condition has been recorded, so the listing describes the club as found, not as polished.

Assess the grips, the shaft, and the clubhead separately for every club. Each of the three carries its own condition and its own cost, and averaging them into one grade for the whole bag hides exactly the club that will be returned.

Set, singles, or pass: the split decision

With the audit done, the lot resolves into one of three sales. A complete, matched set with consistent shafts is sold whole: the set is the product a buyer was searching for, and completeness is what they are paying for. A mixed lot with one or two verified, desirable clubs and a tail of ordinary ones is usually split — the verified clubs listed singly to the wider pool that searches by model, the rest sold locally as a starter bundle with its mixed contents stated plainly. A lot whose audit fails — cracked heads, bent shafts across several clubs, specifications that cannot be verified — is passed or cleared as-is with the faults named, never dressed up as a set.

Before choosing, total the fix cost across the whole lot: a grip replacement, a reshaft, or a repair quoted per club, multiplied by every club that needs it. Small per-club costs are how a cheap lot stops being cheap. Put that total, your all-in auction cost, and the channel cost beside the price the verified specification can support. That comparison, not the hammer price, decides whether the lot was a buy.

Who should not do this, and the kill criterion

Skip golf lots if you cannot read a shaft label, measure a club, or tell a matched set from a mixed bag — in this category the specification work is the business, and a seller who cannot do it is selling descriptions they cannot stand behind. Skip them, too, if your only channel requires shipping long clubs at a cost the lot cannot carry.

The kill criterion: after the audit, if the clubs that can be verified and listed do not cover the all-in cost plus the total fix cost, with the unverifiable remainder valued at zero, the lot is a pass. And for stock already bought: if a set has been listed through a full local season with its specification published and has drawn no sale at a price that clears its costs, split it once — after that, clear it. Clubs do not appreciate in a garage.
