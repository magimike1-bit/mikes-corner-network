Reselling used guitars from auctions: check the neck before you price the name on the headstock

Reselling · 7 min read

Start with the pass. A used guitar with a twisted neck, a repaired headstock break, or a lifting bridge is not a bargain at any hammer price a casual bidder will pay, it is a repair bill with strings on it. The brand on the headstock is a claim about what the instrument might be. The neck, the frets and the electronics are the evidence. Auction buying reverses the private-sale order: there is no owner to question and often no chance to play the instrument before bidding, so the checks split into two groups, photo gates that decide the bid, and a pickup inspection that decides whether the listing can say what the bid assumed.

A note on money: this is general information about reselling, not financial advice. No guitar is guaranteed to sell, or to sell at any particular price.

Who should not do this, and the kill criterion

Skip guitar lots if there is no way to test an electric guitar through an amplifier at pickup or at home, and no budget for a technician's opinion on a questionable neck. Without those two things, the most expensive faults in this category cannot be checked at all, and every listing becomes a guess a buyer can return.

The kill criterion, stated before any bidding: if the photo gates below cannot clear the neck, headstock and bridge, the lot is valued as parts and decor, not as a playable instrument, and if the hammer rises past what that lower value supports, the lot is a pass. For stock already bought: if the pickup inspection finds a structural fault that was not in the listing plan, the guitar is sold honestly as a project or for parts, at the price that description earns. It is never quietly relisted as a player.

Photo gates before a bid exists

Used-guitar checklists written for private sales assume a buyer standing in a room with the instrument. An auction buyer gets photographs. These are the photographs that carry the decision, and the faults each one is there to catch.

Missing photos are information too. A lot that shows the body from four angles and the neck from none has answered the neck question in the least useful way, and the bid should be sized for the parts value until pickup proves more.

The pickup inspection, in order

Work in a clean, well-lit space, in this order, and write the results down before any cleaning or string change, testing guidance starts from exactly that setup. On the buying runs behind this site, the standing rule is to check the item before the buyer ever gets there; in this category that means the full sequence below, not a strum in the car park.

  1. Body, neck and hardware first. Examine all three for damage and document what is found, systematically. Sight the neck, check the neck joint for gaps, and confirm the truss rod has free movement if it is adjustable.
  2. Playability next. Check neck alignment and action, then play every string up and down the board, listening for buzzing and for a string that sounds dull or dominant against the others.
  3. Electronics, through an amplifier. Test each pickup and each control for consistency, clarity and unwanted noise, buzzing and signal loss are the faults this step exists to catch. Work the volume and tone controls through their whole travel.
  4. Identity last, from the instrument itself. Record the maker, model and serial number from the labels, stamps and markings on the guitar, for an acoustic, the serial is most often inside the sound hole, and note the case and any paperwork that came with it.

Success looks like a completed record: structural verdict, playability verdict, electronics verdict, identity recorded. A guitar with four verdicts can be listed with a straight face. A guitar with three gets the fourth question answered before it is listed, or the listing says plainly that the item was not tested that way.

Identity, modifications, and the unknown-provenance listing

Treat a serial number as a guide, not a verdict. Fender's own support pages say exactly that for dating: records for some early Japanese-made instruments are not complete and are not completely definitive, and serial numbers should be used only as a guide, in combination with known age-related specifications. One maker stating that limit in print is a good reason to apply the same caution to every maker whose records were not checked.

Modifications get the same treatment. Valuation guidance is clear on the direction: check for modifications and replacements, because a guitar with swapped pickups, a refretted neck or a refinished body is a different product from an original one, and document what is original and what has changed. Percentage premiums for originality or provenance do circulate in valuation writing; none is used here, because a premium that traces to one guide is a claim, not a price.

So the auction listing standard is simple. State what the inspection verified: model, serial, structural and electronics results, modifications found. State what it did not: playing history, ownership history, originality of any part that could not be checked. Unknown provenance is a normal condition for an auction guitar, and naming it costs less than the return that follows from implying a history nobody knows. The item must match what the listing says, that rule, from the buying runs behind this site, is the whole of it.

Price only what the inspection proved

Build the price from comparable sales, valuation guidance suggests comparing five to ten comparable sales rather than trusting any single listing, matched on model, condition and originality, and taken from completed sales rather than asking prices. Then subtract the full cost stack: all-in auction cost, strings and setup supplies, any technician's time, packaging or local delivery cost, and channel costs. A playable, verified guitar and a project guitar with the same name on the headstock are priced from different comp sets, because they are different products.

Keep the per-guitar record with the money. Canadian peer-to-peer sellers report resale income as self-employment income, and GST/HST registration generally becomes required once taxable supplies pass $30,000 over four calendar quarters. The inspection record and the money record are, in practice, one document, and it is the document that shows, a season later, whether guitar lots are earning their space or merely passing through it.
