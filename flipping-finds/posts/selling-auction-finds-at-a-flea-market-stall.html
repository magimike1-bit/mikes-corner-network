Selling auction finds at a flea market stall: booth costs, pricing, and what actually moves

Reselling · 8 min read

$100 plus HST. That is what one published Canadian market — 613flea in Ottawa — charges for a 10-by-10 booth for a single day, before a table rental touches the bill. The booth is not overhead to be absorbed somewhere in the business. On a stall day it is a line in the margin of every item on the table, and the sellers who treat it that way are the ones who can say, by closing time, whether the day made money.

The opportunity, in one paragraph

A stall turns auction stock into face-to-face sales: no listing time per item, no shipping, and a buyer who can pick the piece up, test it, and carry it away. The buyer pays because the price beats retail and the item is in front of them now. What the channel charges for that is the booth, your transport, and a full day of your time — which is why the day has to be run as a small P&L, not as a garage sale with a fee.

The break-even target you set before loading

Worked example, built from the 613flea figures above. Every number is labelled:

  • Day costs (from the table): booth $100 + HST, plus one rented 6-foot table at $10 + HST — $110 before tax. Use $125 as the planning figure for the day with tax in (a labelled assumption: replace it with your market's actual invoice total), before fuel and your time.
  • Contribution per item (assumption): suppose the stock you bring averages $18 of margin per piece after its all-in auction cost. Replace this with your own average.
  • Break-even: $125 ÷ $18 ≈ 7 items. The first seven sales pay for the table the items are sitting on. Profit starts at sale eight.

That single number changes what you load. A stall stocked with pieces carrying $5 of margin each needs roughly twenty-five sales to clear the same booth — a much harder day. Bulky, slow stock makes the point sharper: it pays rent in your finite space at home, and it pays the booth again every day it comes back unsold. Fast, small wins are not a consolation prize here; on a fixed-cost day, turnover is the strategy.

What to bring: stock matched to the buyers walking past

Scout before you book. The vendor guidance in the sources is specific: visit the market as a buyer first, during peak hours — usually Saturday morning between 8 and noon — and judge traffic by whether buyers are carrying bags and purchases, not just walking. Note who is shopping: families, collectors, and bargain hunters buy different stock, and the demographic in the aisle determines what belongs on your table. A market where established vendors return regularly is a working signal the day pays for them.

Bring stock a passer-by can evaluate in ten seconds: tested, priced, and complete. Auction finds that need an explanation, a missing part, or a plug you cannot reach at the stall are poor stall stock — 613flea, for example, runs 10am to 4pm with move-in from 7:30 to 9:00, a short selling window that rewards items that sell on sight. Save the niche pieces for the online listings, where the right buyer can find them at leisure.

Pricing at a stall: tags, haggle room, and bundles

Price everything, visibly. Flea-market pricing guidance gives the formula plainly: price equals the cost of the item, plus expenses, plus the desired profit margin — and at a stall, the day's booth cost is the expense most sellers forget to spread across the stock. The same guidance treats bargaining as part of the culture: price slightly higher to leave room — wanting $20, tag around $25 — and use bundles or multi-item discounts to move a second and third piece in one transaction. Each extra item in a sale carries no extra booth cost, which is exactly why bundles outperform single-item haggling on a fixed-cost day.

Cash and e-Transfer at the table

Take both. Interac's guidance for stand and booth sellers is direct: accepting Interac e-Transfer at a booth means a customer who is not carrying cash is not a lost sale, and a Statistics Canada survey it cites found 50 per cent of businesses accept e-Transfer as a payment method. Set up Autodeposit before market day — funds deposit without coordinating a security question and answer, which makes the transaction faster, though deposits still follow a routine fraud check by the financial institution, so confirm the deposit has landed before a higher-value piece leaves the table. Interac also notes Tap to Pay on an eligible phone accepts contactless Interac Debit with no extra hardware — worth setting up before the day, not during the morning rush.

Cash still earns its float: it settles instantly, closes the small sales without a phone in the loop, and makes change-driven rounding — two items for a round bill — a selling tool instead of an inconvenience.

After close: triage the unsold stock the same day

  1. Sold well, more in storage: bring it again next market, at the same price. It has earned its table space.
  2. Handled often, never bought: the price or the audience is wrong. List it online where a wider pool can judge it, or cut the price before it occupies another booth day.
  3. Ignored all day: do not re-stall it unchanged. Bundle it with a proven seller, or clear it — carrying it home, storing it, and carrying it back is a cost charged three times.
  4. Record the day: gross sales, day costs, items sold, items carried back. That record, not the feeling of a busy aisle, is what decides whether you book again.

Success looks like this: every piece that comes home has a new plan attached to it the same evening, and nothing returns to storage as a default.

Who should not book a stall, and the kill criterion

Skip the stall if your stock is mostly niche items that need a national buyer pool, if your average margin per piece forces a break-even count the market's traffic cannot plausibly deliver, or if the booth is non-refundable and the weather or date is a gamble you cannot afford to lose — at 613flea, a cancelled booth is not credited forward.

The kill criterion: after two market days at the same venue, if gross margin after booth, table, and transport has not beaten what the same stock would have netted listed online over the same period, stop booking that venue. Keep the market that pays; drop the one that only feels busy.
