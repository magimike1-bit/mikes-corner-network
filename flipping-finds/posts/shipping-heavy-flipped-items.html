Shipping heavy flipped items: parcel limits, freight, and the math that decides

Reselling · 9 min read

Every flipper eventually wins the lot that teaches this lesson: a solid, heavy, genuinely valuable item — a cast-iron machine base, a hardwood dresser, a commercial mixer — that costs more to move than it cost to buy. The mistake is rarely the purchase. The mistake is treating shipping as a problem to solve after the sale, when the only remaining choices are expensive ones. Heavy items are a shipping decision first and a listing second, and the decision is cheap exactly once: before you bid, or at the latest before you list.

The decision comes before the bid

Seller-focused shipping guidance makes the same point in so many words: determine the shipping method early, before listing, so the item is priced accurately and you avoid surprise accessorial fees and rejected packages (s3). For an auction buyer the logic runs one step earlier. A heavy item's deliverable market is set by how it can move. Sold locally, its buyers are whoever is within driving distance this month. Shipped by parcel, its buyers are a country. Shipped by freight, its buyers are a country plus patience. Those are three different prices for the same object, and the all-in bid that makes sense for one is a loss on the others. There is also a space argument the regulars on this site will recognize: bulky, slow-moving inventory pays rent in a finite room, and a heavy item with no shipping plan is the bulkiest kind of slow. Price the exit before you price the entry.

The thresholds that sort every heavy item

Three numbers do most of the sorting. First, 70 pounds: most parcel carriers start adding surcharges once a package passes 70 pounds, and oversized freight comparisons put the practical crossover at about the same place — anything over roughly 70 pounds, or past the carrier's dimensional limit, usually ships cheaper as LTL freight than as a parcel, with per-package savings often in the 30 to 50 percent range (s4, s5). Second, 150 pounds: parcel shipping is designed for boxed items typically under 150 lbs, and past that line a shipment usually has to move as freight — palletized, quoted, and scheduled rather than labelled and dropped off (s3, s4). Third, size: for standard ground service, a parcel over 130 inches in combined length and girth no longer qualifies and is treated as oversized or freight (s4), and freight guidance suggests palletizing once a shipment exceeds 108 inches in length or 165 inches in combined length and girth (s1). Weight alone does not decide; a light but enormous item can fail on dimensions while a dense, compact one sails through on weight.

The surcharge stack, in plain dollars

Between the base rate and the freight line sits the zone that eats margins: surcharges. Parcel pricing runs on dimensional weight — the box's size converted to a billable weight using a divisor of 139 — so a big, light box bills like a heavy one (s2). On top of that, a 2026 heavy-products shipping guide lists FedEx Additional Handling at $29.50 to $40.75 and the Oversize surcharge at $255 to $330 (checked October 23, 2026) (s2). Read those numbers as a flipper: the oversize surcharge alone can exceed the entire profit on a mid-priced item, and it arrives after the sale, when it can no longer be priced in. Freight has its own extras — liftgate and residential-delivery charges are the classic ones — which is why freight guidance insists on asking about them before agreeing a sale and getting the quote in writing (s1). None of these fees are hidden, exactly. They are simply never shown to a seller who did not ask before the buyer paid.

Two habits protect every row of that table. Rate-shop both parcel and freight on each oversized item instead of assuming by product type — the crossover moves with distance and dimensions (s5). And when several oversized items are going to the same buyer, consolidate them into one freight unit rather than shipping three parcels (s5). For furniture and antiques specifically, blanket wrap deserves its own line in the comparison: the carrier supplies the padding and handling, which removes the crating problem that makes flippers avoid good furniture in the first place (s3). One more freight-era rule: if a big item comes back, return freight can cost as much as the product itself, so a partial refund or a write-off is often the cheaper ending — decide your returns posture for heavy items before the first one sells, not during a dispute (s2).

Run the decision in six steps

  1. Weigh and measure the packed item, not the bare one: weight, length, width, height, and combined length and girth (length plus twice the width plus twice the height).
  2. Sort it against the thresholds: under 70 lbs and in-size points to parcel; 70–150 lbs means price parcel and freight quotes; over 150 lbs, over 108 inches long, or over the size limits means freight, blanket wrap, or local-only.
  3. Get real quotes before listing — at least two for freight — and ask explicitly about liftgate and residential-delivery charges; get the chosen quote in writing (s1, s3).
  4. Do the margin math with the quote inside it: expected sale price minus all-in purchase cost minus the quoted shipping minus platform fees. If the number only works with the cheapest imaginable shipping, it does not work.
  5. Write the listing to match the channel: state freight, blanket wrap, or local pickup plainly, with the delivery reality (pallet, liftgate, timing) a buyer needs to agree to.
  6. Book promptly and keep the paperwork: quotes expire and dimensions get re-measured by carriers; the written quote and your packed measurements are your defence in a billing dispute.

What success looks like: no sold item ever produces a shipping bill you had not already seen as a quote before listing, and heavy lots get bid on — or passed on — with the delivery channel already chosen.

FAQ

Is freight always cheaper over 70 pounds? Often, not always: freight comparisons put typical savings at 30 to 50 percent per package over parcel for oversized shipments, but distance, pallet size, and accessorial fees move the answer, which is why the rule is to quote both modes every time (s5).

Can I just charge the buyer whatever shipping costs? You can pass through a real quote; you cannot responsibly guess. A guessed shipping charge that comes in low leaves you paying the difference or cancelling the sale — both worse than a written quote obtained before listing (s1, s3).

What about selling heavy things only locally? A legitimate channel, and often the right one — provided the price reflects the smaller buyer pool and the item's space cost while it waits. Local-only is a decision; drifting into it after a shipping surprise is not.
