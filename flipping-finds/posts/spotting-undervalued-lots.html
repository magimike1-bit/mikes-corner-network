Spotting undervalued lots when you scan listings

Sourcing · 6 min read

The money in liquidation auctions is made before you bid — in the twenty minutes you spend scanning listings with a practiced eye. Most bidders scroll fast, bid on what's shiny, and miss the lots where the value is hiding in plain sight. Here's how to read a listing the way an experienced reseller reads it.

Read the lot composition, not just the headline

The title of a lot might say "assorted small electronics," but the photos show six brand-name power tools and a pile of cables. Headlines are written fast, often by warehouse staff, and they undersell the lot as often as they oversell it. Always scroll through every photo and count what's actually there.

Look for lots where one or two items carry most of the value and the rest is filler. Other bidders do the same rough math and bid accordingly — but they miss things: a premium brand hiding under a pile, accessories that sell well on their own (cases, docks, lenses), or a complete set of something that sells better together than apart. You're not looking for "a box of stuff." You're looking for the one item in the box that pays for the whole lot.

Brand names carry the floor price

In resale, brand matters more than almost anything else. A no-name drill and a brand-name drill in similar condition sell for very different amounts. When you scan listings, train your eye to spot recognizable brands first — in electronics, tools, appliances, and apparel alike. If you can't read a brand in the photos, zoom in; blurry logos in listing photos are common and worth the extra five seconds.

Learn the resale hierarchy in your categories. Some brands hold value for years; others were hot three seasons ago and have faded. The ones to watch are the brands people search for by name on marketplace sites — that's your demand signal, and you can check it in a minute with a search.

Photo clues that scare off other bidders

Other bidders' fear is your discount — when it's misplaced. Lots with bad photos (dim lighting, cluttered background, everything piled together) get fewer bids than clean, well-shot lots with identical contents. Ugly photos don't mean ugly items; they mean a rushed listing. If you can identify the items despite the photos, you get to bid against a smaller crowd.

Similarly, watch for vague descriptions: "customer returns," "untested," "shelf pulls." These are flags for other bidders to scroll past, but they're routine in liquidation and don't automatically mean the items are bad. Customer returns are often unopened or lightly used; people return things for reasons that have nothing to do with the product. The discount for uncertainty is real, but don't confuse "uncertain" with "worthless."

Category patterns worth learning

Some categories consistently offer better ratios of effort to margin:

  • Small electronics and accessories — easy to store, cheap to ship, and brands are easy to verify in photos.
  • Power tools — brand-driven demand, batteries and chargers add up fast in a lot.
  • Sealed or new-in-box items — lower risk, but watch for inflated "retail value" claims in the description; verify against actual sold listings.
  • Bulky or heavy lots — less competition because pickup and storage are a hassle. Only bite if you have the space and the vehicle.

And categories to approach carefully: anything you can't test cheaply, items with missing accessories (chargers, remotes, cables — they kill resale value), and fashion where sizing and counterfeits complicate everything.

The scanning routine

A practical routine for the night before an auction closes:

  1. Skim every lot in your categories, flagging anything with recognizable brands or interesting composition.
  2. Open the flagged lots and read every photo and the full description.
  3. Quick-check resale value for the 2–3 items you don't already know the price of — search sold listings, not asking prices.
  4. Set your max bid (all-in cost minus fees minus your margin) and don't move it during the auction.

That last step matters most. The lot is worth what the lot is worth. The soft close will stretch, the adrenaline will spike, and the bid button will look very tempting. Your ceiling was set by math, not by the moment — let it hold.
