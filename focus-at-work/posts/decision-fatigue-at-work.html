Decision fatigue at work: what the science supports, and what to do anyway

Focus · 7 min read · Published 2026-10-23

By 4 p.m. the decisions get worse in a recognizable way: you approve the thing you would have queried at 10 a.m., or you defer the thing that needed a straight answer, because deciding feels oddly heavy. The popular name for this is decision fatigue — and the popular explanation for it is in worse shape than most articles admit. This one starts with the correction, because the fixes that survive it are better than the tricks that don't.

The famous story, checked

The term was coined by social psychologist Roy F. Baumeister, as part of the ego depletion theory: self-control draws on a limited store of mental energy, and decisions spend it. The most-cited evidence is a study of parole judges, where approval rates reportedly fell from around 65 percent at the start of a session to near zero by its end, recovering after a break. It's a compelling story, and it launched a thousand productivity articles — many of which also repeat Kathleen Vohs' experimental finding that the most fatiguing part of deciding is figuring out what you actually want, more than weighing options or carrying out someone else's decision.

Here is the part the listicles skip. Ego depletion, the theory underneath the fuel metaphor, has faced serious replication trouble: a preregistered test of the core effect across 36 laboratories (Vohs and colleagues, 2021) found no evidence for it in confirmatory tests. Careful explainers of the research now separate the two ideas: decision fatigue — choice quality degrading as decisions accumulate through a day — has been documented in applied settings independently of the mechanism, while the claim that willpower is a tank of fuel that empties is contested and should not be the foundation for how you design a job, let alone a policy. The honest summary: the late-day slump in your deciding is real enough to design around; the glucose-tank story about why it happens is not settled science.

What survives the correction

Stripped of the metaphor, three practices hold up under either explanation — depletion, ordinary tiredness, or simple cognitive load. First, fewer decisions: every recurring decision converted into a default or a rule is one that can never arrive at 4 p.m. in a weakened state. Second, batched decisions: making the day's scheduling and priority calls in one planning session concentrates the cost instead of smearing it across every hour. Third, placement: for most people judgment is freshest earlier in the day after sleep, with the exact peak varying by chronotype — so consequential, irreversible decisions belong in your fresh window, and the tasks you can do without thinking belong at the end of the day. Even the older, uncritical advice converges on this much: front-load key decisions, hold persuasive meetings in the morning when the other side is fresh too, and notice that a tired decider's two failure modes are impulsiveness and making no decision at all.

The decision inventory

The fix starts with seeing the load. For one week, keep a rough tally of the decisions your job actually asks of you, in three buckets:

The three buckets of a decision inventory
BucketExamplesTreatment
Recurring and low-stakesMeeting lengths, report formats, routine approvals, scheduling orderDefault or rule — decide once, in writing, and stop re-deciding
Recurring and consequentialPrioritization calls, standard vendor or scope questions, escalation thresholdsA standing rule with named exceptions, reviewed monthly
One-off and consequentialHiring calls, budget commitments, strategy trade-offsYour fresh window, a written one-page case, and a night's sleep when the deadline allows

Most people who do this tally are surprised by the first bucket's size. The inventory's point is not the count; it's that bucket one is usually half the day's decisions and nearly all of them are eliminable this week. Vohs' finding earns its keep here too: the draining decisions are the open-ended “what do I want here?” ones — and a standing rule is precisely a device for having answered that question in advance, on a good day, with time to think.

Defaults, rules, and other people

Three conversion tools do the work. Defaults: the standing answer that applies unless someone argues otherwise — meetings are 25 minutes, the report goes out Fridays, the standard tool is the tool. Rules with thresholds: “expenses under this line are approved as submitted; scheduling conflicts resolve in favour of the earlier-booked commitment” — a rule decides a whole class at once. Delegated decisions: some of your decisions are only yours by habit. Naming who else decides — and genuinely letting them — removes the decision and the 4 p.m. version of it. Notice what all three have in common: they move the deciding to a moment of your choosing, done once, rather than letting it recur daily at whatever hour and energy level chance provides. That is the work-design version of the fatigue insight, and it needs no fuel tank to justify it.

Placing the decisions that remain

What survives the inventory gets placed deliberately. Consequential decisions go in your freshest window — and if a consequential decision arrives late in the day with a deadline that allows tomorrow morning, the professional move is to say so: “this deserves a fresh look; you'll have it by ten.” Decision batching handles the middle class: one short session, mid-morning, where the day's approvals, scheduling calls and priority questions get decided together, instead of ambushing you one at a time. And protect the edges — a day that starts with five trivial decisions (inbox triage by vibes, meeting roulette, message replies) has spent its freshest hour on its least important calls.

The two failure modes to watch in yourself

The older literature, whatever its mechanism troubles, described the symptoms precisely, and they are worth knowing as self-diagnostics. The first is impulsiveness: the late-day yes that skips the checking your morning self would have done — the approval, the commitment, the reply-all sent without the second read. The second is avoidance: the decision that quietly becomes no decision, the item that rolls to tomorrow's list for the third day not because it is hard but because deciding about it feels hard. Neither means you are tired in a way a snack fixes; both mean the decision arrived at the wrong hour or in the wrong form. The inventory's rules catch the first (a threshold the impulsive yes would have breached), and the decision session catches the second (a named time when avoided decisions get made in a batch, with the list in front of you instead of willpower).

The payoff: not more willpower — fewer occasions that need it. A job with an inventory, defaults and a decision session asks for a handful of real decisions a day, placed where you're best at them. The 4 p.m. version of you mostly executes, which is what 4 p.m. is for.

Frequently asked questions

So is decision fatigue real or not? The practical phenomenon — decisions getting sloppier, more impulsive or more avoidant as they pile up — is well observed. The specific ego-depletion mechanism (willpower as a depletable fuel) failed a large preregistered replication. Design for the phenomenon; don't quote the mechanism as fact.

Does food or glucose fix it? Eat lunch; hunger and fatigue are real. But the strong claim that decision quality runs on blood sugar is part of the contested theory — treat a break and a meal as rest, not as refuelling a measurable tank.

What's the fastest single change? The inventory. One week of tallying shows you the recurring decisions, and converting the top three into written defaults removes more daily load than any morning routine.
