Deep work habits for busy people

Focus · 6 min read

Deep work — sustained, undistracted concentration on something hard — is where the valuable output comes from: the analysis, the design, the writing, the problem-solving. But most advice about it assumes a quiet life and an empty calendar. If your day is full of meetings, messages, and other people, you don't need the idealized version. You need a workable one.

Forget four hours. Start with one block.

The fantasy is a whole morning of flow. The reality for most workers is that a single protected 60–90 minute block, done daily, outperforms occasional heroic sessions. Your brain needs roughly 15–20 minutes to fully settle into hard thinking, so anything shorter than 45 minutes barely counts — but one real block a day compounds fast.

Schedule it like a meeting with someone important (it is — it's with your future self). Morning works best for most people, before the day's demands accumulate, but the best block is the one you'll actually defend. Mark it busy on your calendar so meetings can't colonize it.

Build a shutdown ritual for distractions

Willpower is a terrible distraction strategy. Environment beats it:

  • Phone: face-down, on silent, in a drawer or another room. Out of sight genuinely works better than "I'll ignore it."
  • Notifications: turn off everything non-essential during the block — or use your OS's focus/do-not-disturb mode, which does it in one tap.
  • Tabs: close everything unrelated to the current task. One window, one job.
  • People: if you work near others, a visible signal helps — headphones on means "interrupt only for fires." Tell your team what the signal means once, and it works.

Have a way back in

Interruptions will still happen. The difference between people who recover and people who lose the whole block is a re-entry routine. When you're pulled away, take ten seconds to jot down exactly where you were: the next sentence, the next calculation, the next decision. When you return, you don't have to reconstruct your thinking — you just pick up the note.

Equally useful: a short wind-down at the end of the block. Spend the last five minutes writing down where you stopped and what's next. Tomorrow's block starts instantly instead of spending twenty minutes remembering what you were doing.

Match the work to the energy

Not all hours are equal. Most people have a daily peak — often mid-morning — and a trough — often early afternoon. Put the hard thinking in the peak and the email, admin, and easy tasks in the trough. Fighting your own energy curve is a losing game; scheduling around it is free.

The minimum viable deep-work system: one 60–90 minute calendar block per day, phone out of reach, notifications off, a ten-second "where I am" note when interrupted. That's it. Everything else is optimization.
