Focus in a noisy open office: rank the noise before you fight it

Focus environment · 8 min read · Published 2026-11-07

Why can you work through an air conditioner, a printer and a rumbling coffee machine, then lose your thread completely when two colleagues start talking three desks away? The answer matters, because the fix for a loud room and the fix for a talkative one are different tools, bought in a different order. Most advice treats office noise as one problem with one answer, usually headphones. Triage it instead: name the symptom, rank the causes the way the research ranks them, and spend effort where the ranking says the damage is.

The symptom, stated precisely

The complaint is rarely “the office is loud.” It is more specific: a sentence you were writing dissolves mid-clause, a spreadsheet check has to restart from the top, a name you were holding while you typed is simply gone. That pattern, losing held information rather than being startled, points at one category of sound. So does its opposite: the days when the floor is objectively busy yet the work flows, because nobody near you is saying anything you can follow.

Keep that distinction. It decides everything below.

The causes, ranked

First, intelligible speech. Research on irrelevant speech in open plan offices, including work by Jahncke and colleagues published in Applied Acoustics, found that how much background speech damages performance depends on how intelligible it is and on the task in front of you. A word memory task was disrupted more than a mathematical one, an information search task suffered only when the speech was highly intelligible, and some tasks were barely touched at all. The practical reading: the clearer the conversation you can overhear, the more it competes with verbal work like writing, reading and holding details in mind. A 2026 Chalmers University of Technology thesis on speech exposure reaches the matching conclusion from the other side: background speech reduced working memory performance compared with silence, louder speech disrupted more, and conversational speech is repeatedly identified as one of the most disruptive parts of office noise.

Second, the effort tax. The same Chalmers work found something quieter and more expensive: perceived workload rose under speech exposure even when performance held steady. Keeping your output level in a talkative room is possible. It is not free. The cost shows up as the 4 p.m. exhaustion that seems out of proportion to the day, and it is why “just concentrate harder” is a plan with a bill attached.

Third, steady mechanical noise. Air handling, traffic rumble, the hum of equipment: this is the sound most people blame and the one the research ranks last. It is constant, carries no information, and the brain files it as background quickly. It can annoy, and at high levels it adds to the speech problem by forcing voices up, but on its own it is the least damaging of the three.

One more finding belongs in the ranking, because it shapes the fixes. When two corporate headquarters moved to open plan floors, Bernstein and Turban measured what actually happened to interaction: face to face conversation fell by roughly 70 percent in both companies, while email and instant messaging rose. Their reading was that people socially withdraw when a space gives them no boundaries. A noisy floor is therefore not only an acoustic problem. It is a norms problem, and norms can be negotiated in a way acoustics cannot.

Fix one: position and timing, the free fixes first

  1. Move away from the talk, not just the noise. Distance is an intelligibility tool: every metre between you and a conversation makes it harder to follow, and harder to follow is the whole goal. A seat away from the kitchen, the printer queue and the meeting room door beats a seat in the middle of the floor even if the middle measures quieter.
  2. Face a wall or a window, not a walkway. Movement in peripheral vision pulls attention on its own. Turning your screen so the traffic passes behind you removes a second, silent interruption channel.
  3. Put verbal work in the quiet windows. Every floor has a rhythm: the first hour, the lunch trough, the late afternoon lull. Watch yours for two days, note when nearby talk peaks, and place writing, reading and analysis in the troughs. Save forms, filing and routine replies for the loud hours, because the research says task type decides how much speech can hurt you.

Fix two: make speech less intelligible, not the room silent

Since intelligibility is the mechanism, the target is not silence. It is blur. A steady, featureless sound played at modest volume, rainfall, fan noise, a purpose built masking track, raises the floor under nearby voices until the words stop resolving into sentences. Jahncke and colleagues put the principle plainly: attempts to minimize speech intelligibility yield increases in cognitive performance. You are not drowning the office out. You are taking the meaning out of it.

This is also where the headphone purchase needs an honest label. Active noise cancelling uses microphones and a processor to generate a cancelling sound, and it is very good at steady, low frequency noise: engines, air conditioning, the hum of an office. It works less well on sudden sounds and on high pitched sounds such as voices, which is to say it is weakest against the top ranked cause on your list. What handles voices is passive isolation, the padding and seal of the ear cups. So buy in the right order: a well sealed pair of ordinary headphones plus masking sound first, noise cancelling when the hum itself is also a problem, and expect the cancelling to earn its money on the commute and the mechanical drone, not on the sales call happening behind you. One legitimate use needs no audio at all: plenty of people wear noise cancelling headphones with nothing playing, purely to get quiet and to be left alone, which leads directly to the third fix.

A caution on music: lyrics are intelligible speech that you chose. For writing and reading, instrumental tracks or masking sound ask less of the verbal machinery you are trying to protect.

Fix three: signals and norms, agreed out loud

Headphones only work as a do not disturb signal if the team has agreed they are one. Unagreed, they are ambiguous: half the floor reads them as “deep in work,” the other half as “listening to music, interrupt freely.” The Bernstein and Turban result is the warning here. Left to improvise, people withdraw silently, move their conversations to chat and email, and the floor gets neither collaboration nor quiet. Norms are how a team chooses its withdrawal deliberately instead. Three are worth proposing, in these words or your own:

  • “Headphones on means interrupt only if someone is blocked. Anything else, send a message and it gets answered at the next break in work.”
  • “Phone calls and long conversations move to the call area, not because they are banned, but because one call at a desk is five other people’s background speech.”
  • “Two quiet hours on the shared calendar, late morning, when meetings are not booked and quick questions queue up instead.”

Propose them as an experiment with an end date, two weeks, then keep or drop them as a team. A trial is an easier yes than a rule, and it gives the sceptics data instead of an argument.

When personal fixes run out: the escalation decision

Some floors defeat individual fixes, and recognizing that point is part of the method rather than a failure of it. Run three questions. Is the damaging noise structural, a call heavy role seated inside a focus area, a meeting room with no door? Have the free fixes above run honestly for two weeks? Can you name the specific work that is suffering, in the research terms: verbal, memory heavy work during peak talk hours? Three yes answers make the ask an employer ask, and a specific one lands better than a complaint: a seat move away from the call cluster, a booked focus room for two blocks a week, or masking and absorption in the worst corner. Bring the two week experiment with you. It is evidence, and it shows the cheap options were tried first.

What success looks like: you finish a writing task at your own desk during a normal, busy hour without restarting a paragraph; the nearby conversation registers as murmur rather than sentences; and the end of day tiredness eases, because the effort tax is the first cost to fall when intelligibility does.

General information only: this article covers everyday workplace productivity, not occupational health. If noise at work is affecting your hearing, sleep or health, raise it with your employer or an occupational health professional.
