Managing up without more meetings

Visibility · 6 min read

Your boss can't see your work unless you show it. But "managing up" has a bad reputation — it sounds like politics, or like adding another meeting to a calendar that's already full. The good version is simpler: keep your manager informed in writing, in a few minutes a week, so there are no surprises and your contributions are visible when it matters.

The weekly update: five bullets, five minutes

Once a week — Friday afternoon or Monday morning — send your manager a short written update. Not a report, not a presentation. Five bullets:

  • Done this week: 2–3 concrete outcomes.
  • In progress: what's actively underway.
  • Next week: what you're planning to do.
  • Blocked on: anything you need from them or others (this is the most valuable line — it turns waiting into their action item).
  • FYI: anything they'd want to know before someone else tells them.

Keep it scannable: short lines, no paragraphs. A manager who can absorb your week in 30 seconds will actually read it every week — and that regular signal is worth more than any single impressive delivery.

Surface problems early, with options

Managers hate surprises far more than they hate bad news. When something is slipping or going wrong, say so early — and always bring options, not just the problem. "The vendor delivery slipped two weeks; I can either resequence the rollout or ship phase one without it — which do you prefer?" turns you from the bearer of bad news into the person handling it.

The same applies to disagreements: raise them privately, early, and framed around the work ("I'm concerned this approach misses the deadline — here's what I'd suggest instead"), not around personalities.

Ask what "good" looks like

A lot of managing-up failures are really alignment failures. When you're given a task, confirm the target before you start: "Just to make sure we're aligned — success here looks like X by Friday, right?" Ten seconds of clarification saves days of rework, and managers remember who checks.

Once or twice a year, ask directly: "What's the one thing I could do better?" Most managers rarely get asked, and the answer tells you exactly where to aim your effort.

Keep a private wins file

Keep a running document of your accomplishments — concrete outcomes with dates: "cut report prep from 3 hours to 40 minutes," "resolved the billing discrepancy that was blocking $X." You'll forget 80% of what you did by review season; this file means you don't have to. It also feeds your weekly updates, so nothing important goes unmentioned.

The principle: no surprises, in writing, regularly. A manager who always knows where your work stands trusts you — and trusted people get the interesting assignments, the flexibility, and the benefit of the doubt.
