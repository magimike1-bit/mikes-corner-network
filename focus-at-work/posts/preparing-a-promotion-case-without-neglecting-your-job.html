Preparing a promotion case without neglecting your current job

Career · 10 min read

Three hours a week. That is the whole budget this guide allows for building a promotion case, and the cap is the point. Most promotion advice lists virtues, master your current role, gain visibility, upskill, take stretch work, without ever pricing them in time. Priced honestly, the full list is a second job, and a second job is how the first one starts to slip. That matters because every serious guide to promotion starts in the same place: consistent delivery in the current role is the entry ticket, not a box you ticked last year. The e-Careers promotion guide puts mastering the current role and delivering on performance review objectives first, before visibility or pitching. CareerPathPI says the same thing in plainer terms, promotions usually go to people who meet expectations and then exceed them. A case built by neglecting the job it is based on destroys its own evidence. So the case gets a budget, one project, and a review date, run like any other capped project at work.

Set the budget before you set the goal

Start by confirming the target actually exists. Promotion criteria differ by organisation and can cover performance, leadership ability and communication skills, so the first task is factual, not motivational: find out what qualifications and milestones the next level requires, whether a defined path exists in your department, and who makes the decision. That is a thirty minute conversation and a page of notes, and it can save months. If nobody can describe the next role, you are not building a case yet, you are gathering intelligence, and the budget drops to thirty minutes a week until the picture clears.

Then state your interest out loud. CareerPathPI flags a quiet trap here: managers cannot advocate for an ambition they have never heard, and a short one on one statement, that you enjoy the role, want to grow in the company, and would like to know the steps, opens mentorship and sets expectations. Do this in week one. A case your manager learns about at the pitch meeting has been built without the one person who could have steered it.

The budget itself is simple. Three hours a week, in two blocks, is enough for one stretch project done properly plus fifteen minutes of portfolio upkeep. It is not enough for three projects, a course, and a committee, which is exactly why the next section exists.

Choose one stretch project, not five

Stretch work is the standard advice, and the standard examples are consistent: train or mentor new employees, lead a small team or initiative, present results to senior leadership, or work cross functionally with another department. The failure mode is collecting all four. Each one is visible, each one is defensible, and together they eat the core job that the case depends on. Score the candidates instead, using four questions that take ten minutes per candidate.

TestQuestionScore
Next level proofDoes this project require a skill the next role is actually judged on?0 to 3
Measurable resultCan the outcome be stated in numbers a review panel would accept, such as time saved, cost cut, or satisfaction improved?0 to 3
Seen by decidersWill the people who approve promotions see the work or its results directly?0 to 2
Core job costCan it run inside the weekly budget without displacing a core deliverable?0 to 2

Fund the highest scorer and decline or defer the rest, naming the trade off when you decline: you are concentrating on the project most likely to count at review. A project scoring under five is volunteering, not case building, and it should be judged as volunteering, taken on only if you would do it with no promotion at stake. Note what the framework is doing: the e-Careers guide describes the finished portfolio as accomplishments, future value, and a salary request, but says nothing about which work deserves to fill it. The score is the missing selection step, applied before the effort, not after.

The runbook, week by week

  1. Weeks 1 to 2, foundations. Hold the criteria conversation and the ambition conversation. Write down the next role requirements, the decision makers, and your current gaps. Success looks like a one page target description you could hand to a mentor without embarrassment.
  2. Weeks 3 to 4, selection. List the stretch candidates on offer or available to request, score each one with the table above, and commit to exactly one. Tell your manager which one you chose and why. Success looks like one project with a named result you intend to produce.
  3. Months 2 to 6, delivery inside the budget. Work the project in your two weekly blocks. Every Friday, spend fifteen minutes on the portfolio: one line per result, with the number attached while you still remember it, time saved, cost avoided, revenue touched, satisfaction moved. CareerPathPI recommends quantifying impact in exactly those terms, efficiency, revenue or cost, and satisfaction or engagement, and the weekly habit is what makes the numbers real instead of reconstructed in a panic before review season.
  4. Month 6, midpoint check. Compare the portfolio against the criteria page from week one. If a criterion has no evidence yet, the remaining months get reallocated to it, or the ask date moves. Success looks like no surprises: every criterion either has evidence or has a plan.
  5. The ask. Time it to the windows the guides agree on: after a strong review, around budget or compensation cycles, in a growth period, or just after a major project lands. Avoid frozen budgets and layoff periods, when even a strong case cannot be funded. Present the portfolio in its three parts, accomplishments with metrics, the future value of the responsibilities you are ready to carry, and a salary request grounded in market research on the next level.

The honest cost table

Promotion cases are usually discussed as pure upside. They are not. Here is the full cost of the capped version, stated the way any other investment at work would be stated.

LineCapped caseUncapped case
Time per week3 hours, two blocks6 to 10 hours, leaking into evenings
DurationAbout 6 to 12 months to a funded askOften 12 to 18 months, drifting
Stretch projectsOne, scoredThree or more, collected
Core job riskLow, the budget protects deliveryHigh, and delivery is the entry ticket
Cash costUsually zero; a course only if the criteria name oneCourses and certifications bought on hope

The long timeline in the uncapped column is not invented pessimism. The e-Careers guide itself plans on a 6, 12 and 18 month arc, new project at six months, measurable results at twelve, the ask at eighteen. A capped case does not shorten the organisation clock, but it stops the clock from consuming your evenings while it runs, and it keeps the evidence, your current performance, intact for the whole period. Break even, in plain terms, is the point where the raise and the enlarged role repay roughly 150 to 300 hours of case building. That arithmetic is why the score matters: a small raise at the end of an uncapped two year effort can be a poor trade, and it is better to know that in week three than at the pitch.

Who should not run this play

Skip it if your current performance is genuinely shaky. Both sources are blunt that mastery of the current role comes first, and a stretch project layered onto missed deadlines reads as distraction, not ambition. Skip it, for now, if the organisation is in a freeze or a layoff round, when the guides agree the timing cannot work. And skip the internal case entirely if the honest answer to the criteria conversation is that the next role does not exist in your team. That is not a failed case. It is information, and it points at the two real alternatives: a lateral move that builds the missing evidence somewhere it counts, which both guides treat as a legitimate step, or an external move, which deserves its own plan rather than a resentful half version of this one.

The kill criterion

Set this before you start, in writing, with a date. If, twelve months after the ambition conversation, you have delivered the scored project, hold evidence against every criterion, and still have no agreed next step, no funded role, and no dated commitment from your manager, the internal case is closed. Not paused, not extended by another quarter of patience. Closed. At that point the portfolio you built is worth more outside: it is, almost line for line, the evidence file an external application needs, and the same results that failed to move an internal panel tend to be priced quickly by a market that is hiring for the role now. The one exception is a documented near miss: if a promotion is refused, the e-Careers guide sets out a relaunch worth taking once, specific feedback, measurable goals agreed in writing, and a review date inside ninety days. Take that relaunch if it is offered in those concrete terms. Vague encouragement is not a relaunch, and a second year of it is the cost this whole plan was built to avoid.
