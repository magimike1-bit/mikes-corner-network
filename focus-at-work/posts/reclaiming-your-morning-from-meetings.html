Meetings that steal your morning: how to reclaim the first two hours

Deep work · 7 min read · Published 2026-10-27

Two hours. That is the block worth defending — the first two hours of the working day, before the calendar fills with other people's priorities. There is also a line worth drawing before any tactic: if your role is genuinely on-call for the morning — incident response, a trading desk, a newsroom at deadline — a meeting-free block is the wrong tool, and forcing it will fail by Wednesday. The method below is for the far more common case, where the morning meetings are habits, not obligations.

Your morning is being spent by other people

A meeting booked at nine rarely feels like a decision. It feels like weather. But every standing invite in the first two hours was placed there by someone choosing the earliest slot that looked free, and the cost lands on the same person every time: you, and the deep work that only fits in an unbroken stretch. One way to map the day is by energy zones, with the first two hours as the proactive zone — the hours for deep work, decision-making, and strategic tasks. Spend that zone in a status meeting and the demanding work does not disappear. It moves to the tired end of the day, where it gets a worse version of you.

Some companies have solved this at the policy level with a no-internal-meetings-before-10am rule. If yours has not, the individual version still works. It just takes negotiation, which is the part most advice skips.

Choose the block and mark it properly

  1. Pick the same two hours every workday. For most schedules that means the first two hours after you start. Consistency is what lets colleagues learn the pattern and stop proposing the slot.
  2. Put a recurring hold on the calendar, marked busy, with a short explanation in the title or description. A named block — “Focus block — reachable by message, meetings from 10:30” — gets respected in a way a blank private hold does not.
  3. Shrink the meetings that remain. Default to 25 or 50 minutes instead of 30 or 60, so overruns stop cascading into the next hour and the day keeps its edges.
  4. Apply the agenda test to new requests. A meeting needs an agenda and a decision point. If it has neither, propose handling it asynchronously instead — a written update the attendees can read in five minutes.

Success looks like a calendar where the first two hours show one block, five days a week, and colleagues have started booking around it without being asked twice.

Moving the standing meetings: scripts that work

The block collides with reality in one place: the recurring meetings already sitting inside it. Move them one at a time, privately, with a trade rather than a complaint.

To the organiser of a standing meeting: “I'm protecting my first two hours for project work this quarter. Could we move our Tuesday slot to late morning? I'll come with the update written up beforehand, so we can keep it to 25 minutes.” Note the structure: a reason tied to work, a specific alternative, and something offered in return.

To a manager who books over the block: “That hour is where the analysis for the quarterly report gets done. I can move the block on Thursday for this, but if it becomes weekly, the report timeline is what gives. Which matters more this month?” You are not refusing. You are pricing the request, in the open, and letting the person who owns the priorities choose.

Across time zones: a colleague whose afternoon is your morning has a real constraint, not a bad habit. Offer a rotation — the early slot alternates weeks — or one fixed early meeting a week inside the block, named as the standing exception. A rule with one named exception survives. A rule that pretends geography away does not.

Exceptions worth allowing (and the ones that eat the rule)

Allow three: a genuine incident, where waiting costs more than the block is worth; a customer or client meeting that can only happen then, agreed case by case; and your one named standing exception, if time zones force it. Each exception gets used, then the block resumes the next day without a renegotiation.

The exceptions that eat the rule look reasonable one at a time: a “quick sync” that recurs, a weekly team meeting that drifts earlier by fifteen minutes a month, a manager's standing request to “just grab you first thing.” The test is recurrence. Anything that happens twice in the block becomes a standing meeting and goes through the moving conversation like any other.

Deciding tonight what tomorrow's block is for

A protected block with nothing assigned to it fills with email within a week. The fix happens the night before: name your top three priorities before you log off, and start on them the next morning while focus is strong. Then narrow further. The block holds one main task, chosen because it needs unbroken attention — a draft, an analysis, a decision with the thinking done properly — plus, at most, one smaller task for the final stretch.

Two selection questions do the work. What would still matter if it were finished a week from now? And what gets harder every day it waits? A task that passes both belongs in the block. Email, expense forms, and inbox tidying pass neither, however satisfying they feel at nine in the morning.

The first two weeks: what to expect

Week one is negotiation: two or three conversations, some mild surprise that the block exists, and at least one booking over it that you handle with the trade script rather than silent surrender. Week two is quieter. Colleagues route around a boundary that has held, and the stranger effect appears — with the morning spoken for, the meetings that remain get shorter and better prepared, because the agenda test is doing its work in the background.

Judge the experiment at the end of week two by one measure: how many of the ten blocks survived intact. Seven or more means the system fits your job. Fewer means the exceptions are mispriced — tighten those before abandoning the block itself.

Frequently asked questions

My team is in three time zones. Is a morning block realistic? Yes, with the exception priced in: keep the block, name the one recurring early meeting geography forces, and rotate the inconvenience where you can. The block shrinks to four protected mornings a week and still does its job.

What if my manager flatly refuses? Shrink the ask before abandoning it: three mornings a week, or ninety minutes instead of two, trialled for a month against a named deliverable. A refused blanket rule often becomes an accepted experiment.

Should the block be the same time every day? Yes — predictability is what makes it cheap for everyone else to respect. A floating block has to be re-explained daily and will be booked over weekly.
