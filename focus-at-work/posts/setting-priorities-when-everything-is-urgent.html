Setting priorities when everything is "urgent"

Start here · 6 min read

Everything at work arrives labeled urgent: the email marked high priority, the message with three exclamation marks, the request "needed ASAP" with no actual deadline. When everything is urgent, nothing is — and the day gets spent reacting instead of working. Prioritizing isn't about finding more hours. It's about deciding, on purpose, what today's hours buy.

Separate urgent from important

The oldest useful framework in productivity still works: sort tasks into urgent (demands attention now) and important (moves something that matters forward). The trap is that urgent things shout and important things whisper. A ringing phone beats a quarterly goal every time — unless you decide in advance that it doesn't.

Each morning, look at your list and ask two questions: What happens if this doesn't get done today? and What moves the needle this week? The first question finds the truly urgent. The second finds the important. Your day should contain at least one item from the second list, protected before the first list eats everything.

The three-things rule

Pick three things that would make today a good day if you finished them. Not ten — three. Write them down where you'll see them. Everything else is bonus. This sounds trivially simple, and that's the point: a ten-item list trains you to fail by 3pm. A three-item list trains you to finish. On genuinely chaotic days, shrink it to one.

The three things should be outcomes, not activities: not "work on the report" but "send the draft of section two to Priya." Vague tasks expand to fill the day; defined tasks end.

Someone else's emergency is not automatically yours

When a request arrives screaming urgency, pause and ask three things before it rearranges your day:

  • What's the real deadline? "ASAP" is not a deadline. "The client meeting is Thursday at 10" is. Ask.
  • What happens if it waits until tomorrow? Often: nothing. The urgency was about the sender's anxiety, not the work.
  • What drops if I pick this up? Make the trade-off explicit — to them and to yourself. "I can do this today, but the invoice review slips to Monday. Which do you want first?"

This isn't refusal. It's triage, out loud. Most reasonable people accept it immediately, because you've shown them the real choice instead of silently absorbing it.

Protect the important stuff from the urgent stuff

Important work dies by a thousand interruptions. Give it armor: block the first 60–90 minutes of your day for your most important task before email and messages get a vote. Batch the small urgent things — reply to messages twice a day instead of all day. And end each day by choosing tomorrow's three things, so morning-you doesn't negotiate with a blank list.

The test: at the end of the day, can you name three things you finished that mattered? If yes, it was a good day — even if fifty small things are still waiting. The small things are always waiting. That's what makes them small.
