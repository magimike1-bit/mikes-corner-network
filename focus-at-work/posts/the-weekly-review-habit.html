The weekly review habit

Systems · 5 min read

Every system on this site leaks a little: priorities drift, the deep-work block gets colonized by meetings, the to-do list goes stale. The weekly review is the maintenance that keeps the whole thing working — thirty quiet minutes, once a week, that turn a chaotic week into next week's clear plan. It's the highest-leverage half hour in a working week.

When and where

Friday afternoon works best for most people: the week's events are fresh, and next week is close enough to plan concretely. Late Friday also means you can leave work at work — the review closes the mental tabs so the weekend is actually a weekend. If Fridays are chaotic, Thursday afternoon or Monday first thing both work; the day matters less than the regularity.

Give it a recurring calendar block so it survives. Somewhere quiet, with your task list, your calendar, and your notes from the week.

The review, step by step

  1. Clear the decks. Process everything that's piled up: inbox, messages, notes, the random scraps of paper. Decide each item's fate — do it now (if under two minutes), schedule it, delegate it, or delete it. The goal is zero "I should deal with that" items left floating.
  2. Look back. What got done this week? What slipped, and why? You're not grading yourself — you're collecting data. If the same kind of task slips three weeks running, that's information about your system, not your character.
  3. Look at the calendar. What's coming next week? Meetings, deadlines, travel. Spot the collisions now, while there's still time to move things.
  4. Choose next week's three things. Based on what matters and what's coming, pick the top priorities for next week — the three-things rule, applied at weekly scale. Put the most important one on Monday morning.
  5. Check the loose ends. Anything you're waiting on from someone else? Send one follow-up per item. Waiting silently is how things die.

Keep it small enough to keep

The review dies the moment it becomes a burden. Don't turn it into a journaling project or a productivity ceremony — it's maintenance, like checking tire pressure. If thirty minutes feels like too much, do fifteen: clear the decks, pick next week's three things, done. A short review you do every week beats a thorough one you abandon in February.

The payoff: Monday morning stops being a negotiation with a blank list. You sit down already knowing the week's three things, the calendar's already been checked, and nothing from last week is quietly rotting. Thirty minutes on Friday buys you a calm Monday — the rarest thing in working life.
