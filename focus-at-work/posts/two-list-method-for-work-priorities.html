The two-list method: separating must-do work from everything else

Systems · 6 min read · Published 2026-10-27

The daily two-list method is simple: keep one short list of what must get done today, capped at three to five items, and one longer list holding everything else. The short list gets your working hours. The long list gets a weekly look, not your guilt. That is the whole mechanism — and it only works if the cap holds, because the constraint is what forces the critical thinking about what actually moves the needle.

One warning before the steps. The name covers a second, very different method, and the two get mixed together constantly.

Two methods, one name

The famous version comes from Warren Buffett. In that telling, you write down 25 goals, circle the top five, and the remaining 20 do not become a someday list — they become an avoid-at-all-cost list. When the pilot Mike Flint described working on the second list intermittently until the top five were done, Buffett corrected him: no attention at all goes to the 20 until the five have succeeded. List two, in that method, is a wall.

The daily version treats list two as the opposite of a wall. You sort a brain dump into must-do items — time-sensitive, high-priority — and nice-to-do items, and you revisit the nice-to-do list without guilt if time or energy remains. Nothing on it is forbidden. It is simply not today.

Confusing the two produces the two common failure modes. Run the daily lists with Buffett severity and the holding list becomes a source of dread. Run the goal exercise with daily softness and the 20 keep eating the five. Pick the method that matches the timescale: goals measured in years get the wall; work measured in days gets the holding area.

Setting up the daily version

  1. Empty your head onto one page. Every open task, request, and half-remembered commitment goes into a single brain dump. Nothing gets sorted yet, and nothing stays in your head as a private second system.
  2. Sort the dump into the two lists. Must-do means time-sensitive and high-priority: it belongs to today, and someone or something real is affected if it slips. Everything else — worthwhile, interesting, merely loud — goes to the holding list.
  3. Cut the must-do list to three to five items. If it holds more, the sort was not honest. Demote the weakest items to the holding list rather than shrinking the writing.
  4. Write each must-do as a finishable outcome. “Send the draft” can end. “Work on the draft” cannot, and an item that cannot end will still be sitting there at six o'clock claiming the day failed.
  5. Close the holding list. It stays shut during working hours except for the two moments the next sections describe: a promotion decision and the weekly sweep.

Success looks like this: by day's end, the must-do list is done or honestly carried over, and the holding list is longer than it was in the morning — because new requests landed there instead of on your day.

The promotion rule: how items move up

A holding-list item earns a place on the must-do list in exactly three ways. First, a real deadline arrives: a date set by someone else, with a consequence attached. Second, it becomes the blocker: other committed work cannot move until it does. Third, it wins the weekly sweep, described below, and takes a slot at the next morning's sort.

Everything else is not a promotion reason. Urgency in a sender's voice is not one. Guilt is not one. Having a spare twenty minutes is not one — spare minutes are for finishing the current list, or for rest, not for quietly expanding the commitment.

Promotion is also a swap, not an addition, once the must-do list is full. A new item comes on only when a current item comes off: finished, delegated, or demoted back to holding with its reason noted. A list that only grows is just the old single list wearing a new name.

Mid-day intrusions and stalled must-dos

New work will arrive mid-day; the method assumes it. The routine is short. Write the intrusion on the holding list first, always — capture is not commitment. Then test it against the three promotion reasons. Most intrusions fail the test and stay parked, which is the system working, not you being difficult. The rare one that passes takes a slot by the swap rule, and the person who sent it hears what it displaced.

A stalled must-do needs a different move. If an item has sat untouched through two working blocks, it is usually too big, blocked on someone else, or quietly no longer required. Split it, chase the blocker as the new must-do, or demote it with a note. What it cannot do is ride the list for a week lending false shape to every day.

The weekly sweep of the everything-else list

Once a week — fifteen minutes is enough — read the holding list top to bottom and give every item one of four fates. Promote it into next week's early must-do slots. Keep it holding, because its deadline is real but not near. Delegate it, with a name attached. Or delete it, because the moment passed and nobody noticed, which is information, not failure.

The sweep is what keeps the holding list honest. Without it, the list becomes a guilt archive that grows until opening it feels bad, at which point the whole method collapses back into one undifferentiated pile.

Signs the method is breaking

Three signals, each with its own repair. The must-do list regularly runs past five items: the sort has gone soft, so re-apply the time-sensitive and high-priority test out loud. The holding list gets checked hourly: it has become a second inbox, so close it and let the sweep do its job. And finished days still feel like failures: the must-dos are written as activities rather than outcomes, so rewrite them until each one can visibly end.

None of this needs an app, a template, or a fresh start on Monday. Two lists, a cap, a promotion rule, and a weekly sweep — the discipline is in the refusals, and the refusals are the point.
