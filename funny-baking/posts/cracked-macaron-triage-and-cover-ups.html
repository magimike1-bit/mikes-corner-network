Cracked Macaron Triage and Cover-Ups: Wrinkles, Hollows, and Other Battle Scars

Rescue guide · 7 min read

Macarons are the only cookie with a casualty ward. The tray comes out and the inspection begins: this one cracked like a dried lakebed, that one wrinkled like it aged a decade in fourteen minutes, and the perfect-looking one — the smug one — turns out to be hollow, a beautiful shell around an empty room. The troubleshooting charts of the internet respond to this scene by immediately planning the next batch, which is excellent advice delivered at the worst possible moment. This guide starts with the batch in hand. Some scars are fatal. Some quietly repair themselves in the fridge. And some are pure cosmetics, which means they can be covered by anyone willing to melt chocolate with intent. Triage first, vanity second, self-pity nowhere.

What actually happened: the cause ledger

Every scar on the tray has a short list of usual suspects, and the established troubleshooting sources agree on the roster. Cracks are steam with nowhere polite to go: the piped shells did not rest long enough to form a dry skin — the standard rest is until the tops are dry to the touch, usually 20 to 40 minutes depending on humidity — so steam escaped through the top instead of pushing the base up into feet. An oven running too hot forces the same explosion, and untapped air bubbles in the batter become cracks with a head start. Hollows are an air-and-heat problem: meringue whipped past stiff, glossy peaks takes in too much air and the shells puff, then collapse as they cool; over-mixed batter loses its structure the other way; an oven too hot sets the shell before the interior can follow, and underbaking leaves the inside unrisen. Wrinkles are an ingredient confession: oily almond flour, or too much food colouring — especially liquid colouring — worked into the shell. And the small print matters for triage: wrinkled and lightly cracked shells are surface events, while hollows live inside the cookie, which is why they answer to completely different rescues.

The triage procedure: sort the batch in hand

  1. Cool completely before judging anyone. Shells removed too soon read as stickier and sadder than they are; sticky bottoms are often just under-baked or under-cooled shells. Let the tray cool fully on the sheet, then lift each shell and sort it into one of three regiments below.
  2. Regiment A — serve as-is. Smooth tops, decent feet, maybe a wrinkle or a faint blemish. These shells need nothing but filling. Wrinkles in particular are invisible the moment a shell is sandwiched and viewed from any normal human distance.
  3. Regiment B — fill and mature. Hollow shells (tap one: it sounds like a tiny drum), dry or slightly crunchy shells, and shells whose hollows are small. Fill them generously and refrigerate the assembled macarons for 24 hours. This is the step the panic usually skips: during maturation, moisture from the filling slowly moves into the shell, and small hollows can genuinely improve — troubleshooting guides list the 24-hour filled rest as the first fix for minor hollows, and the storage experts call it non-optional for final texture.
  4. Regiment C — disguise required. Cracked tops, pronounced wrinkles, lopsided shells. The interiors are usually fine; the faces need work. These go to the cover-up table below, where the rule is simple: a crack you can see gets a coating, a crack you can feel gets a sandwich with the good side out.
  5. Accept the genuine casualties. Shells that are burnt, raw-sticky after full cooling, or shattered are not cover-up candidates. Crumble them over ice cream or yogurt, where broken macaron is a topping rather than a failure, and let the ledger below improve the next batch.
  6. Mature before serving, whatever the regiment. Pierre Hermé's much-quoted verdict, carried in Food Nouveau's troubleshooting guide, is that macarons "are not ready to eat" when made and are at their best after 24 or even 48 hours, because osmosis between filling and shell tenderises the inside while the surface stays intact. Bring the matured macarons back to room temperature before serving — the triage is not finished until the fridge has had its shift.

Next batch: the short prevention ledger

Four habits prevent nearly every scar in the ledger. Rest properly: piped shells sit until the surface is dry and matte to a light touch — 20 to 40 minutes in most guidance, up to 30 to 60 on humid days — and the clock is a servant of the fingertip test, not a replacement for it. Macaronage to lava: fold only until the batter flows like lava and falls in a slow ribbon; stopping early leaves a weak structure, going past it deflates the batter toward hollows and spread. Respect the meringue and the bubbles: whip to stiff, glossy peaks and no further, tap the piped trays firmly on the counter three or four times to release trapped air, and pop the big survivors with a toothpick. Verify the oven: an oven thermometer settles every argument, and the standard correction for both cracks and hollows is to lower a hot oven by 10 to 15°F (5 to 8°C). Add the wrinkle clause — sift the almond flour and powdered sugar, and reach for gel or powdered colouring instead of liquid — and the next tray should need far less triage than this one did.

What success looks like

Success tonight is a plated batch in which no guest can tell which regiment any macaron served in: filled, matured, and — where needed — dipped, with the scars under chocolate and the hollows quietly filled by a day of fridge osmosis. Success next batch is a tray that skips triage entirely: dry-skinned shells, neat feet, and a bite that is crisp, then chewy, then filling, all the way through.

FAQ

Why did my macarons crack on top?
Usually insufficient resting — the skin never dried, so steam escaped through the top — or an oven running too hot, or air bubbles left untapped in the batter. Rest until dry to the touch, tap the trays firmly after piping, and verify the oven temperature.

Can hollow macarons be saved?
Small hollows, often yes: fill the shells and mature the assembled macarons in the fridge for 24 hours, and the filling's moisture moving into the shell can soften the hollow into a normal bite. Large hollows are a next-batch fix — meringue, macaronage, and oven heat are the levers.

Are cracked macarons still edible?
Entirely. A crack is a surface event; the flavour and texture inside are usually intact, which is why a chocolate dip or a good-side-out sandwich retires the evidence completely.
