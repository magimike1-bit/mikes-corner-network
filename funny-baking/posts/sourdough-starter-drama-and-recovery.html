Sourdough starter drama: neglect, hooch, and the comeback feed

Rescue guide · 7 min read

Every sourdough starter has a dramatic phase, and it always begins the same way: a jar, pushed to the back of the fridge behind the pickles, discovered weeks later wearing a layer of dark liquid and an expression of profound betrayal. The baker's heart sinks. The internet's verdict is swift and useless — half the comments say dead, half say feed it and pray, and nobody explains what the jar is actually saying. Here is the translation, followed by the comeback. A sourdough starter is a colony of wild yeast and bacteria in a flour-and-water apartment. It is almost never dead. It is hungry, it is acidic, and it is producing extremely legible signals. Drama, in a starter, is just communication with better staging.

Decode the drama: what each symptom means

The dark liquid on top is the famous one, and it has a name: hooch. It is alcohol given off by the wild yeast as it ferments, and its appearance signals one plain fact — it has been a while since the last feeding. King Arthur's bakers describe the full progression with a straight face: a hungry starter shows half an inch or so of hooch, which can simply be stirred back in before feeding; left longer, the liquid darkens, and a truly neglected starter can dry into a grey crust. Note what is absent from that description: any mention of death. Hooch is a hunger strike, not an obituary.

The table's last row matters: the guide's optimism has a boundary. Colour that belongs in a sunset — pink, orange — and anything fuzzy are contamination, not drama, and no comeback feed fixes them. Everything else on the list is theatre.

The comeback feed: a procedure

The recovery uses the standard maintenance feed, which King Arthur states with unusual precision: keep 50 grams of starter — weigh it; volume lies — and discard the rest, then add 50 grams of water and 50 grams of all-purpose flour, mix, cover, and rest at room temperature. Discarding is not waste; without it the starter grows too large and too acidic, which is the condition you are rescuing it from. The liquid question resolves simply: on a refrigerated starter, the hooch can be drained off or stirred in — baker's choice — but for a long-neglected jar, pouring off the darkest hooch before the feed gives the colony a less acidic room to recover in.

  1. Evening zero: the rescue feed. Take the jar out, pour off dark hooch, scrape away any dried crust, weigh out 50g of the best-looking starter from the middle, and feed it 50g flour and 50g water in a clean container.
  2. Stand 12 hours, covered, at room temperature. This is the fridge-revival sequence exactly: the evening feed, then a half-day on the counter while the colony wakes and eats.
  3. Next day: feed twice, about 12 hours apart. Morning and evening, same 50-50-50. Between feeds, watch the jar the way you would watch a kettle — the first doubling, however lazy, is the comeback's first review.
  4. The ripe test. A starter is ready when it has roughly doubled in volume and turned bubbly about 6 to 8 hours after a feeding — ripe starter is classically described as doubled and just beginning to deflate, generally around the 8-hour mark. Pass the test and the drama is over; the last feeding should land 6 to 12 hours before you bake.
  5. Return to a schedule it can keep. Regular bakers: counter, once a day. Everyone else — and honesty is the whole game here — the fridge, once a week, with a few hours on the counter after feeding before it goes back in.

What success looks like: by the second day's second feed, the jar rises on schedule, smells pleasantly tangy rather than like nail-polish remover, and doubles inside the 6-to-8-hour window. Bake something forgiving first — a flatbread, a simple loaf — and let the starter re-earn its name in the recipe box.

Keeping the peace afterwards

The fridge-versus-counter decision is the whole maintenance philosophy in one choice. Room temperature is the starter's preferred world — it rises faster, stays vigorous, and asks only a daily two-minute feed. The fridge is the compromise for casual bakers: a weekly feed keeps the colony going, at the price of the two-day revival waltz before each bake. Neither is wrong; the wrong schedule is the one the baker cannot keep, because the starter keeps the baker's schedule whether the baker keeps it or not. Pick the fridge, date the jar, and set the weekly feed on the same day as something else that never slips — bin night is the classic. A starter fed on bin night is a starter that outlives trends.

The naming ceremony, and other keeper's rituals

A final piece of starter culture, offered without irony because it works: name the jar. Bakers who name their starters feed them more reliably — the international evidence is a million pun-named jars (Clint Yeastwood has descendants on every continent) and the mechanism is plain: a named thing gets checked on, the way a houseplant with a name gets watered. Pair the name with two more keeper's rituals and the drama schedule thins to almost nothing. The first is the elastic band: a rubber band around the jar at the level of the just-fed starter turns the rise into a visible, glanceable gauge — doubled means the band is at half the jar's new height, and a starter checked by eye gets fed before it gets desperate. The second is the fridge-door note with two dates: last fed, and bake day. The revival calendar in the procedure above runs backwards from bake day — evening feed, twelve-hour stand, two feeds the next day — and a note on the door is what makes that calendar start on time instead of in a panic on Saturday morning. None of these rituals appears in the official feeding guides, and all of them serve the guides' only real requirement: a schedule the keeper actually keeps. The starter does not need devotion. It needs bin-night reliability and a name it can live up to.

FAQ

Can I skip the scale and feed by spoon? You can, and bakers do — but starter volume varies with how bubbly it is, so the scale is what makes the comeback repeatable. For the rescue feeds, weigh. For Tuesday maintenance, do what keeps you feeding.

How long is too long in the fridge? Weeks are drama; months are a research project. The revival sequence still applies — expect more feeds before the ripe test passes — but pink, orange, or fuzzy at any age ends the discussion.

The starter rose once and went flat again. Dead? No — hungry again. A risen-then-fallen starter has eaten its feed and is asking for the next one. Feed it; the rise will come back stronger and on a shorter clock.
