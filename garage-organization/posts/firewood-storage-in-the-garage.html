Firewood storage in the garage: a small buffer stack, not the winter supply

Storage systems · 6 min read

Can firewood live in the garage? The honest answer splits by quantity. A couple of days of seasoned, inspected wood, stacked off the floor and burned on a quick rotation, is a reasonable convenience. The whole winter supply, parked inside until spring, is a different decision, and it is the one the extension and fire safety guidance argues against. This guide works as a triage: three questions decide how much wood, if any, belongs inside, and the answers build a two stack system, the main supply outside under the standard stacking rules, and a small buffer inside the garage door.

Question one: is the wood actually seasoned?

Wood burns best at a moisture content of less than 20 percent, and the U.S. EPA’s Burn Wise guidance sets the seasoning time at a minimum of six months (s2). A garage is a poor place to finish that process. An enclosed space lacks the airflow that drying needs, so a tight stack inside traps moisture and dries slowly (s4). That makes the garage a holding spot for finished wood, never a seasoning chamber. Green or partly dried wood stays outside until it passes the meter check described below.

Question two: how long will it sit indoors?

Purdue Extension’s firewood bulletin, publication E-67-W, gives the quantity rule and the reason behind it. Bring firewood indoors only as needed, at most a couple of days’ supply at a time, because storing firewood indoors for long periods speeds insect development inside the wood, which allows the insects to emerge inside the home (s1). The wood arrives with passengers. Long-horned beetles, the most common borers in firewood, lay their eggs in green wood, so they are usually already in the wood when it is cut (s1). A short indoor stay burns the wood before warmth wakes anything up. A long one is an incubation period with a roof.

The reassuring half of the bulletin matters too. Those borers do not attack finished, seasoned, or dried wood inside the home, and the spiders, sowbugs, and other hitchhikers that overwinter in a stack generally leave the wood within a few days of it coming indoors (s1). The risk is managed by rotation speed, not eliminated by worry. The genuine exceptions are the ground stack pests: wood stacked on the ground outdoors for a long time can pick up carpenter ants, and ground stacked wood can become infested with termites, often unnoticed until the wood is moved (s1). That is an argument about where the outdoor stack sits, handled in the next section.

Question three: what else shares the garage?

A large stack is a large fuel load, and a garage is a room full of ignition sources, furnaces, water heaters, vehicles, chargers, and the chemicals on the shelf. The fire risk assessment in the garage storage literature is blunt about the combination: keeping large quantities of firewood in an enclosed structure is discouraged for exactly that reason (s4). A two day buffer near the door is a modest pile. A full cord against the wall is a stored fire. Size the indoor stack to the small end and the question answers itself.

The two stack system

The main stack lives outside. EPA Burn Wise stacking rules: store the wood outdoors, off the ground, with only the top covered, because covering the sides traps in moisture (s2). Purdue adds the building clearance: do not stack wood against the house or garage, and keep a minimum of 3 feet between the firewood and the building, which also improves air circulation and drying (s1). Off the ground is doing double duty in both sources, faster drying and fewer pest problems (s1, s2). Buy and burn locally cut firewood where possible, which lowers the risk of moving invasive forest pests to your property (s2).

The buffer stack lives inside the garage door. It holds the couple of days of wood the Purdue rule allows (s1), drawn from the seasoned end of the main stack, and it is refilled from outside as it empties. Nothing in the buffer is there to dry, and nothing in it stays long enough to hatch anything.

Setting up the buffer stack, step by step

  1. Put a low rack or a pair of rails by the door. The buffer follows the same off the ground rule as the main stack, which speeds drying and cuts pest problems (s1). Directly on the concrete is the one placement both sources rule out.
  2. Inspect every piece on its way in. Look for visible insect activity, and be most suspicious of pieces that sat at the bottom of a ground stack outdoors, which is where carpenter ants and termites get into wood (s1). A suspect piece goes back outside, not into the rotation.
  3. Load only seasoned wood. Every piece in the buffer has passed the moisture check below. Partly dried wood waits outside (s2).
  4. Size the stack to two days of burning, no more. That is the Purdue quantity, and it is also roughly the 24 to 48 hours of use that garage storage guidance lands on independently (s1, s4).
  5. Burn it in order, oldest pieces first, and refill from the main stack. Rotation is the pest control. A stack that turns over every two days never gives indoor warmth the time it needs (s1).
  6. Keep the buffer clear of the furnace, water heater, and parked vehicle heat. Distance from ignition sources is what keeps a small pile a small risk (s4).

The moisture check that decides what burns

“Seasoned” is a claim. The meter is the verification, and EPA Burn Wise gives the procedure its one line: test wood with a wood moisture meter before burning it (s2). The working method is to split a piece and press the meter pins into the fresh split face, because the outside of a log reads drier than the middle. The pass mark is a reading of 20 percent or less (s3). Wood that fails goes back to the main stack, where the remaining drying happens the way EPA specifies, split to 6 inches in diameter or smaller for faster drying and stacked off the ground (s3). Wet wood is not just poor fuel. The energy it wastes goes into boiling off its own water, and the excessive smoke it makes is lost heat leaving up the chimney (s3).

Run the garage as a buffer and it earns its floor space: dry wood within reach on the coldest nights, and no surprises waking up inside it. Run it as the woodlot and the garage inherits every problem the outdoor stack was supposed to keep outside.
