Garage cabinets vs open shelving: decide by item, not by wall

Storage systems · 8 min read

Most garages get this decision backwards. The choice gets made wall by wall — cabinets down one side because they look finished, open shelves down the other because they were on sale — and only afterwards does anyone ask whether the things that actually live in the garage fit the form they were given. The paint ends up on a cabinet shelf it is too heavy for, the daily-grab drill ends up behind a door, and the seasonal bins sit open, collecting the dust the doors were bought to stop. The better sequence is to sort the contents first, verify what each storage form can honestly hold, and let the items assign themselves.

That verification step matters because the published comparison tables get the headline number wrong. Enclosed storage is routinely described as the heavy-duty option. On the manufacturers' own ratings, a single cabinet shelf is one of the lightest-rated surfaces in the garage — and open industrial shelving is among the strongest. Both forms earn their place, but for almost opposite loads than the marketing suggests.

The capacity surprise: read the rating, not the category

Start with the enclosed side. Gladiator's Premier Jumbo Cabinet manual sets a maximum of 50 lb (22.7 kg) per shelf and 300 lb (136 kg) for the whole cabinet; the larger Ready-to-Assemble Extra Large GearBox is rated the same 50 lb per shelf with a 250 lb cabinet total (s1). Those are honest, useful numbers — a shelf of hand tools, spray cans, and small bins sits comfortably inside them — but they are not heavy-storage numbers. A single shelf of gallon paint cans can approach the limit faster than most people expect. At the top of the cabinet range, NewAge's Pro Series wall cabinet, a fully welded 18-gauge steel unit, is rated at 200 lb total and 100 lb per fixed shelf (s2). Double the Gladiator shelf rating is still the ceiling of what enclosed cabinets publish.

Now the open side. A household Husky 5-tier steel unit, 36 inches wide, is rated at 600 lb per shelf and 4,000 lb total when the load is evenly distributed (s3). The welded 48-inch Husky rack raises that to 1,000 lb per shelf and 5,000 lb total (s4). Read those against the cabinet figures: the open shelf a homeowner already owns can be rated at six to twenty times the per-shelf load of the cabinet beside it. Capacity claims in roundup tables — one widely circulated comparison lists enclosed units at 1,000–7,200 lb typical against 500–2,500 lb for open shelves, with no model cited for either range (s6) — invert the relationship that the spec sheets actually show. The lesson is not that cabinets are weak; it is that per-shelf ratings belong to specific models, and any comparison that does not name the model is decoration.

Two cautions keep the numbers honest. First, every one of these ratings assumes the load is evenly distributed across the shelf; a heavy item parked at the centre of a long shelf is a different and harder load than the same weight spread out (s3, s4). Second, a cabinet's total rating is not permission to fill every shelf to its own limit at once — the Gladiator figures make the arithmetic plain, with a 300 lb cabinet total sitting well under what four shelves at 50 lb each plus a loaded base could tempt you to stack in (s1).

What each form actually does well

If open shelving wins on raw capacity, why buy doors at all? Because capacity is only one of the jobs. Cabinets keep tools, automotive supplies, cleaning products, and seasonal gear out of sight and separated from active work areas, and their smooth exterior surfaces are easier to wipe down than a collection of exposed items and open shelves (s5). Dust is the daily version of that argument: contents on an open shelf stay exposed to everything the garage air carries, while a closed door is a complete barrier between the stored item and the room (s5, s6). Enclosed cabinets from both makers above also lock — the NewAge wall cabinet's doors are fully lockable, and Gladiator cabinets ship with a round key lock — which turns a cabinet into the one storage form that can also control access to what is inside it (s2, s1).

Open shelving's strengths are the mirror image. It keeps frequently used items visible and quick to grab, with no door standing between the hand and the tool; the same comparison that prices open shelves lower also lists their access as instant against 5–10 seconds to open a cabinet door (s5, s6). Open shelves adjust — the Husky units move in 1.5-inch or 2-inch increments — so a shelf height can follow the item instead of the item squeezing into a fixed cabinet interior (s3, s4). And the entry cost differs by an order of magnitude in the published ranges: roughly $60–$300 for open shelving against $200–$2,000 or more for enclosed units, with assembly measured in minutes rather than hours (s6). Treat those ranges as orientation rather than quotes, but the shape of the trade is real: open storage is cheaper, faster, stronger per shelf, and fully exposed; enclosed storage costs more, holds less per shelf, and protects, hides, and locks.

The item-by-item decision framework

Run every category in the garage through four questions, in this order. Does it need protection from dust or damp? Power tools with vents and chargers, clean seasonal gear, and anything stored for months at a time point to a door. Does it need access controlled? Anything hazardous or sharp that should stay behind a lock points to a cabinet, and only to a cabinet — an open shelf cannot answer this question at all (s2). How heavy is a full shelf of it? Add up the real load: if a shelf of the category runs past roughly 50 lb — paint, bulk fluids, dense hardware — it belongs on rated open steel, not on a standard cabinet shelf (s1, s3). How often is it grabbed? Weekly-use items should stay accessible and visible; the door tax of a few seconds, paid hundreds of times a year, is how cabinets full of daily gear end up standing open permanently (s5).

The framework sorts a typical garage quickly. Paint and bulk automotive fluids: heavy and shelf-filling, so open steel — unless child access is the concern, in which case a lockable cabinet wins on the second question and the load gets split across two shelves to respect the 50 lb shelf rating (s1). Power tools and their chargers: dust-sensitive and valuable, so cabinets, at weights a cabinet shelf handles easily (s5). Cleaning products and chemicals: cabinets, for the lock and the separation from work areas (s2, s5). Seasonal decorations and camping gear: bulky, light, and untouched for months — either form works, so they take whichever space is left after the first three questions are answered, with the door preferred for dust (s5). Daily-grab hand tools and sports gear in active rotation: open, visible, and instant (s5, s6). Oversized items that fit no cabinet interior go to an open rack or floor-level area by default (s5).

Splitting one garage between cabinets and shelves, step by step

  1. List every category stored in the garage and estimate the weight of one full shelf of each — weigh a representative bin or can on a bathroom scale rather than guessing.
  2. Run each category through the four framework questions in order: protection, controlled access, shelf weight, grab frequency. Write the assigned form beside each category.
  3. Place the lockable cabinet where it can be anchored into wall studs — anchoring every cabinet into studs to prevent tipping is the manufacturer's own installation guidance (s2) — and keep its shelves for the light, hazardous, and dust-sensitive categories.
  4. Place open shelving for the heavy and daily-grab categories, set shelf heights to the items using the units' adjustment increments, and load the heaviest categories on the lowest shelves (s3, s4).
  5. Do a final load check shelf by shelf: no cabinet shelf past its model's per-shelf rating, no cabinet past its total rating, and every open-shelf load spread evenly rather than centred (s1, s2, s3).

What success looks like: every shelf in the garage is inside its published rating, the hazardous and dust-sensitive items are behind a locked door, the heaviest shelf-loads sit low on open steel, and the items grabbed weekly are visible without opening anything.

FAQ

Are garage cabinets better than shelving for storage?

Neither, outright. Cabinets protect, hide, and lock, but standard models rate only 50 lb per shelf (s1); open steel shelving rates 600 lb per shelf and up at household sizes, with instant visible access, but leaves contents exposed (s3, s5). The item decides, which is why most garages end up hybrid (s5).

How much weight can a garage cabinet shelf hold?

On the models verified for this guide: 50 lb per shelf for the Gladiator Premier Jumbo and Extra Large GearBox, and 100 lb per fixed shelf for the welded NewAge Pro wall cabinet, with cabinet totals of 200–300 lb (s1, s2). Check your own model's manual rather than assuming — the rating is per model, not per category.

Can I combine garage cabinets and shelving?

Yes, and the combination is the recommendation rather than a compromise: enclosed cabinets around the less frequently used, hazardous, and dust-sensitive items, open shelving where visibility and quick access matter more (s5). Assign by item using the framework above instead of splitting the garage by wall.
