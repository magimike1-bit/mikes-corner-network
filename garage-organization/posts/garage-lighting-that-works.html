Garage lighting that works: size it in lumens, zone it by task

Workshop setup · 8 min read

Most garages are lit by whatever the builder screwed into the ceiling — one or two bulbs asked to cover parking, storage, and bench work at once. The result is a room that is technically lit and practically dim where it matters: the workbench lives in your own shadow, colours read wrong, and finding a dropped screw is a floor-crawling exercise. Lighting guides that answer with product rankings skip the step that decides everything: how much light this specific garage needs, and where.

Light is sized, not guessed. The sizing unit is the lumen — the light a fixture actually delivers — applied per square foot according to the work done in each zone. This guide runs the calculation for real garage sizes, compares the fixture types honestly, covers the two specs that change how work feels (colour temperature and CRI), and ends with the placement procedure that keeps the bench out of shadow.

The sizing formula, with your garage's numbers

The formula used across lighting guidance is one line: total lumens equals square footage multiplied by the lumens-per-square-foot rate for the use (s1). The rates are the whole decision. A widely used rule of thumb puts garage and workshop general lighting at about 50 lumens per square foot, and workbench and detailed task areas at 75 or more (s2). Parking-only space can live below the general rate; a bench where small parts get assembled cannot.

Run it for the standard cases. A single-car garage of about 200 square feet used for parking at 50 per square foot needs roughly 10,000 lumens. A two-car garage of 400 square feet for general use needs about 20,000 lumens (s1, s2). The realistic case is the split garage — part parking, part workshop: a published worked example sizes a 400-square-foot two-car garage as 300 square feet of general space at 50 (15,000 lumens) plus a 100-square-foot workshop zone at 75 (7,500), for 22,500 lumens total (s1). Zone the calculation the way you zone the floor plan, and the fixture count follows: a standard 4-foot LED shop light typically produces 4,000–5,000 lumens, so the general zone of that example is three to four fixtures before the bench gets its own light (s3).

Cross-check with the professional scale if you do serious mechanical work: the Illuminating Engineering Society ranges quoted in fixture guidance run 50–100 foot-candles for general workshop tasks and 100–200 for detailed mechanical work (s3). Foot-candles and lumens per square foot are the same arithmetic in different dress, so a bench zone planned at the top of the general range is honestly planned. And shop by lumens, not watts — watts measure the energy a fixture consumes, lumens measure the light you get, and LED efficiency varies enough between products that wattage comparisons mislead (s2).

Fixture types, compared on what matters

Four fixture families cover nearly every garage. Linear shop lights — the 4-foot batten style — are the default for a reason: they spread wide, uniform light that reduces shadows, many link together in series for long ceilings, and typical output runs from about 4,000 lumens upward per fixture (s5, s3). Integrated all-in-one fixtures trade replaceable tubes for a slim sealed unit with lifespans often beyond 50,000 hours — fit-and-forget lighting for a parking-and-storage garage (s5). Deformable screw-in fixtures, the multi-panel style that fits a single existing socket, put a lot of light into a one-bulb garage without wiring, with adjustable panels to aim it. Vapor-tight fixtures seal the electronics against dust and damp behind IP65-or-better ratings, which is the right call over a woodworking bench or in an unheated garage that sweats in spring (s5).

The honest trade-offs: linear fixtures need mounting and often wiring but give the most even light per dollar; integrated units are cheap and clean until the driver dies, at which point the whole fixture is replaced; deformable units concentrate light downward from one point, so shadows return at the room's edges; vapor-tight costs more for a seal that only dusty or damp garages actually need. Match the fixture to the failure you are trying to avoid — shadows, maintenance, moisture — rather than to the highest lumen number on the box.

Colour temperature and CRI: the specs that change the work

Colour temperature, in kelvin, decides whether the garage feels like a kitchen or a clinic. Fixture guidance recommends 5000K–6500K — cool white to daylight — for workshops, garages, and inspection areas, because neutral-to-cool light keeps contrast high and colours distinguishable at the bench (s3). Warmer light is pleasanter and worse for work; if the garage doubles as a hangout, put the warm light on a separate switch rather than compromising the bench.

CRI — colour rendering index — measures how accurately a light renders colour compared with natural daylight, on a scale to 100, and it is the spec that decides whether close work goes right (s4). Under a light around CRI 75, dark blue and black wires look nearly identical in shadow; under CRI 90 or better, the difference is obvious — which makes low CRI a genuine misidentification risk in electrical work and a source of paint-matching errors that only appear outdoors, after the work is done (s4). The working bands: 70–80 is standard budget fare, adequate for general tasks; 80–85 suits most bench and vehicle work; 90+ is the choice where colour accuracy carries consequences (s4). And treat an undisclosed CRI as a disclosed one: if a listing does not state CRI, assume 70–80, because manufacturers advertise high CRI prominently when they have it (s4).

Placement: killing the bench shadow

Total lumens can be right while the room is still wrong, because light that comes from behind you puts your own shadow on the work. The benches in most garages sit against a wall with the fixture centred in the room — the worst geometry available. The fix is zoning at the ceiling, not just on paper: general fixtures centred over the open floor, and a dedicated fixture directly over the bench, far enough out from the wall that the light lands on the work surface instead of the back of your head. An aimable task light earns its place alongside the fixed lighting for exactly this reason — it puts strong, focused light where your hands are instead of flooding the whole room to bench brightness (s2).

Storage aisles and the garage door zone have their own small rules. Fixtures over shelving should sit in the aisle, not over the shelves, so the shelves' contents are lit rather than shadowed. Nothing should hang where the door's travel or a lifted hatchback will meet it. And switches deserve a thought: bench light and general light on separate switches means the parking trip at night does not need full workshop brightness, and the bench session does not light the whole room for an audience of one.

Sizing and placing garage lighting, step by step

  1. Measure the garage floor and sketch the zones: parking, general work, bench — with each zone's square footage.
  2. Multiply each zone by its rate (about 50 lm/sq ft general, 75+ at the bench) and add the zones for your total lumen target.
  3. Divide the general-zone target by 4,000-5,000 lumens to get a first fixture count for standard 4ft shop lights; add a dedicated bench fixture on top.
  4. Choose colour temperature in the 5000K-6500K range for work areas, and CRI 80+ for the bench fixture (90+ for wiring or paint work).
  5. Mark fixture positions on the ceiling: centred over open floor, directly over the bench, in the aisle line over shelving — clear of the door's travel.
  6. Wire bench and general lights on separate switches; install, then test at night with a real task at the bench before calling it done.

What success looks like: Success looks like this: you can read small print at the bench without leaning into your own shadow, wire colours separate cleanly under the bench light, and the dropped screw on the floor is visible from standing height.

FAQ

How many shop lights for a two-car garage?

From the worked example: about 20,000 lumens for general use of a 400 sq ft garage, which is four to five standard 4ft fixtures at 4,000-5,000 lumens each — plus a dedicated bench light if part of the garage is a workshop, bringing the plan to roughly 22,500 lumens (s1, s3).

Is brighter always better?

No. Evenness beats raw output: one very bright fixture creates glare and hard shadows, while the same lumens spread across several fixtures light the room uniformly. Above the zone rates, extra light mostly buys glare.

What if a fixture listing hides its CRI?

Assume 70-80, the budget band (s4). That is fine over the car and risky over wiring or paint work, so put a fixture with a stated, high CRI over the bench and let the anonymous ones light the floor.
