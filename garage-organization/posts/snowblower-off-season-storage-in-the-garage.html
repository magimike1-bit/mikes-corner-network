Snowblower off-season storage in the garage: prep it in spring, start it in winter

Storage systems · 8 min read

The short answer: a gasoline snowblower going into a garage for the summer gets its fuel system emptied or treated in spring — not “dealt with in November” — its cylinder oiled, its body cleaned and tightened, and a parking spot in its operating position, on its wheels, away from ignition sources. Everything else is detail, but the detail that decides whether the machine starts at the first snowfall is the fuel, and the fuel decision depends on one question most guides never ask: how long, exactly, will this machine sit? This guide triages the prep by that answer. It covers gasoline machines; a battery blower has no fuel system to prep and needs only a clean, dry spot and whatever its manual says about battery storage.

Triage by how long it will sit

Sitting under about 30 days — a late-season machine that may still be called on: park it. Toro's manuals draw the first line there: do not store gasoline more than 30 days without fuel stabilizer (s1). Inside that window, no fuel work is owed.

Sitting 30 to 90 days: the fuel is now aging on a schedule — gasoline begins to degrade in as little as 30 days (s2). The minimum honest prep is stabilizer added as directed and the engine run long enough to circulate treated fuel through the system (s1, s2).

A full off-season — the normal Canadian case, spring to late fall: this is the branch the manuals actually write their storage procedures for, and Toro's is a drain procedure, not a treat-and-park procedure: stabilize the last fuel, circulate it, then drain the tank and run the system dry anyway (s1). Honda's storage guidance lands at the same place from the other direction: to store a snow blower for over 90 days or off season, drain all the fuel from the fuel tank and carburetor (s3). When the storage is measured in seasons rather than weeks, drain it.

The 90-day cap and the 24-month claim: which number governs

Here is the conflict, stated plainly because the guides usually print only one side of it. Toro's operator manuals say stabilized fuel should not be stored for more than 90 days (s1). Briggs & Stratton's stabilizer guidance says fuel stabilizer keeps fuel fresh for up to 24 months and prevents gum and varnish build-up (s2). Both are manufacturers; the numbers differ by a factor of eight.

The resolution is not to average them. The 24-month figure is a claim about what a stabilizer product can do for fuel. The 90-day figure is the machine manufacturer's instruction for fuel stored in that machine — and Toro pairs it with a drain procedure rather than relying on treated fuel sitting in the tank and carburetor all summer (s1). The machine's manual governs the machine. So the practical rule for a full off-season is Toro's sequence even on a non-Toro blower: treat the last tankful, circulate it so the stabilizer reaches the carburetor, then drain and run dry, and dispose of the unused fuel properly — recycled under local codes, or used in an automobile, as Toro directs (s1). The stabilizer in that sequence is protecting the fuel system during the circulation and the drained fuel in its container, not licensing a summer-long soak. For the short branch, the stabilizer itself is inexpensive: STA-BIL Storage, 236 mL, was C$11.99 at Amazon.ca, checked 2026-10-29 (s5).

Putting a snowblower away for the summer, step by step

  1. On the last refueling of the season, add fuel stabilizer to fresh fuel as directed on the label (s1).
  2. Run the engine for 10 minutes to distribute the conditioned fuel through the fuel system (s1).
  3. Stop the engine, let it cool, and drain the fuel tank into an approved fuel container; Honda's guidance adds the carburetor to the drain list for off-season storage (s1, s3).
  4. Run the engine until it stops from running out of fuel, then prime it and start it again, repeating until it can no longer start — Toro's test for a sufficiently dry fuel system (s1).
  5. Disconnect the spark plug wire, remove the ignition key if the machine has one, and remove the spark plug (s1).
  6. Oil the cylinder: Toro specifies 1 oz. (30 ml) of engine oil poured through the spark plug hole, then pull the starter rope slowly several times to distribute it against corrosion, and loosely install the spark plug (s1). Note that Honda's guidance specifies one teaspoon for its machines — the quantities differ by model, which is why this step follows your manual's number, not a generic one (s3).
  7. Check the supporting systems while the machine is in hand: clean or replace the air filter, inspect the spark plug for wear or buildup, check the tires for cracks or flat spots, and lubricate moving parts, including cables and chute controls, so they do not seize over the summer (s4).
  8. Clean the snowthrower thoroughly — packed snow residue and road salt left in the auger housing all summer are corrosion at work — and tighten all loose screws, bolts, and locknuts, repairing or replacing damaged parts now rather than in November (s1).
  9. Fold the handle if the model allows it — Honda's guidance folds the handlebar over the top of the machine after loosening the handlebar nuts — and only after the machine is parked in its final spot (s3).
  10. Cover it with a breathable, weather-resistant cover; a model-fitted cover keeps dust off without trapping moisture against the machine (s3, s4).

What success looks like: the fuel system is dry by the manual's test — the engine will not restart — the plug is in loosely over an oiled cylinder, the machine is clean, tight, and covered in its operating position, and a tag on the handle records the prep date and the one fall task left: fresh fuel.

Its summer footprint in the garage

Placement has three hard conditions and one planning choice. Hard conditions first: store the snowthrower in its operating position, on its wheels — storing it on its front housing may cause hard starting (s1). Never store it in a house living area or basement, or anywhere ignition sources are present, such as hot water heaters, space heaters, clothes dryers, and furnaces (s1). And keep it away from fertilizers and chemicals that can corrode parts — a common garage neighbour for exactly this machine (s4).

The planning choice is the footprint. A snowblower earns its garage space from roughly November to March and contests it from April to October, when bikes, mowers, and project work all want the same floor. Give it a defined summer spot — against the wall it will exit past in winter, handle folded, cover on — rather than the leftover corner that migrates all summer. A machine that gets shuffled monthly gets its chute controls knocked, its cover pulled off, and its fall recommissioning forgotten. The spot by the door also shortens the worst trip in winter: from storage to driveway, in the dark, in falling snow.

Bringing it back in the fall

Toro's removal-from-storage sequence closes the loop. Remove the spark plug and spin the engine rapidly with the starter to blow the excess oil out of the cylinder. Install the plug by hand, then torque it to 15 ft-lb (20.4 N·m), and connect the spark plug wire (s1). Check and tighten all fasteners, charge the battery on electric-start models as the manual directs, and fill the tank with fresh gasoline — not the drained fuel from spring, which was disposed of or used up months ago (s1). Do this on a dry October weekend, and the first snowfall is a demonstration instead of a diagnosis.
