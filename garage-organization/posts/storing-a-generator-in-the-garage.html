Storing a generator in the garage: store it there, never run it there

Storage systems · 9 min read

The dangerous sentence in generator ownership is not “I store it in the garage.” It is “I ran it in the garage, just for a few minutes, with the door open.” Storage and operation are opposite decisions, and garage guides that blur them do real harm: a cool, prepared generator parked in a dry corner is ordinary equipment storage, while a running one in the same corner is a carbon monoxide emergency. This guide keeps the two apart on purpose. First the operating rule, stated once and without exceptions. Then the storage job itself — fuel handled by how long the machine will sit, a numbered prep procedure, and a placement that respects the ignition sources already living in most garages.

Job summary: difficulty is low — this is draining, cleaning, and parking, not repair. Allow about an hour, most of it waiting for the engine to cool and the last fuel to run out. The hire-a-pro line is narrow and firm: a generator that fails its pre-storage test run goes to a service shop, not into storage to be forgotten, and anything involving house wiring is a licensed electrician's job, not a storage-weekend project.

The rule that outranks every storage tip

Never operate a portable generator inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed, or any other enclosed space — and opening the doors or windows does not make it safe, because that ventilation will not prevent the buildup of lethal levels of carbon monoxide (s1). Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless, it can kill in minutes, and an average of about 100 people die in the U.S. each year from CO poisoning from portable generators (s1). The operating position is outside only, at least 20 feet away from the house, with the exhaust directed away from the home and any other building someone could enter, and with nearby windows closed and vent openings sealed off from the exhaust path (s1).

That rule shapes the storage plan in one practical way: the generator's garage spot and its operating spot are two different places, and the machine has to travel between them. Store it where it can be wheeled straight out — not buried behind the seasonal bins it will be needed most urgently to bypass during an outage.

Drain it or stabilize it: the fuel decision by storage length

Gasoline begins to degrade in as little as 30 days, and the gum and varnish that form can clog the fuel system so the machine will not start the following season (s2). That 30-day threshold splits storage into two honest branches. Under roughly a month, with fresh fuel, the machine can simply be parked with its gas valve closed. Past that, the fuel system needs a decision, and the manufacturers offer exactly two: treat the fuel and keep it, or drain it and run the system dry.

Tools and materials

  • An approved fuel container, sized for the tank, for the drain branch (s4)
  • Fuel stabilizer for the keep-the-fuel branch — C$11.99 for 236 mL, checked 2026-10-29 (s5)
  • Fresh engine oil of the grade the manual specifies, for the pre-storage oil change (s3)
  • Clean engine oil, about 1/2 ounce (15 ml), for oiling the cylinder bore on long storage (s4)
  • A soft cloth for the exterior, and a protective cover that does not retain moisture (s3, s4)
  • The owner's manual for the specific model — torque, oil grade, and any model-specific storage steps live there (s3)

Storing a generator in the garage, step by step

  1. Test it before you store it: run the generator outdoors for 15–20 minutes and confirm it starts, runs, and produces power normally (s3). A fault found now is a shop visit in the off-season; a fault found during an outage is a crisis.
  2. Make the fuel decision from the table above. For the drain branch, work outdoors, shut the engine off, let it cool at least 2 minutes before removing the fuel cap, loosen the cap slowly, and drain into the approved container (s4). For the stabilized branch, add stabilizer as directed and run the engine a few minutes to circulate it (s2).
  3. If you drained, run the engine until it stops from lack of fuel, so the carburetor is not left holding untreated gasoline (s4).
  4. Close the gas valve (s3).
  5. Change the oil while the engine is still warm from the test run, refilling with the recommended grade — old oil left sitting carries the particles that cause wear (s3, s4).
  6. Clean or replace the air filter, and clean the spark arrestor, where carbon buildup can hinder performance and create a fire hazard (s3).
  7. For storage stretching into months, oil the cylinder bore: remove the spark plug, pour in about 1/2 ounce (15 ml) of clean engine oil, reinstall the plug, and pull the starter slowly to distribute it (s4).
  8. Wipe down the exterior, paying attention to the air intake and exhaust areas, and inspect the casing, fuel lines, and electrical connections for cracks or fraying before the machine disappears into its corner (s3).
  9. Let the generator cool completely. Only then wheel it to its spot and put the cover on — never cover a hot generator, and choose a cover that does not retain moisture (s3, s4).

What success looks like: the generator sits cool and covered in a dry, ventilated corner it can be wheeled straight out of, its fuel either drained and run dry or stabilized with the valve closed, its oil fresh, its lines and connections inspected — and a note of which fuel branch you chose, because in eight months nobody remembers.

Where in the garage it goes

The location spec from the manufacturers is a dry, cool spot away from direct sunlight, in a well-ventilated garage or shed, because excessive heat and humidity harm the components and the fuel system (s3). Add the manual's second condition, the one garage guides usually skip: store fuel, and equipment with fuel in its tank, away from furnaces, stoves, water heaters, clothes dryers, and any other appliance with a pilot light or ignition source, because those can ignite fuel vapors (s4). In a typical garage that rules out the utility corner entirely and points to the opposite wall — which is usually the wall nearest the door the generator has to leave by anyway. Keep the stored fuel container to the same rule: approved container, same ignition-source distance, never in living space.

The stored generator still needs a check

Storage is not a seal. Before storm season, repeat the short version of the prep in reverse: uncover it, look over the fuel lines and connections again, confirm the fuel decision you recorded is still inside its limit, and run it outdoors, in its operating position, long enough to prove it still works (s3). Champion's guidance is to check the condition of a stored generator routinely rather than discovering problems at the next outage (s3). A generator that starts on a calm Saturday is stored properly. One that has not run since it was parked is only hopefully stored.

General information only. Follow the owner's manual for your specific generator model for storage, fuel, and maintenance instructions, and the CPSC guidance above for operation: outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, never in the garage (s1, s3).
