Storing ladders in the garage: support it right, or store it damaged

Storage systems · 8 min read

A ladder is the worst-shaped object in most garages: too long for any shelf, too awkward for any corner, and light enough to lean against a wall — which is exactly how most of them end up stored, one knock away from sliding down the paintwork. The lean feels harmless because the damage storage does to a ladder is slow and mostly invisible. A ladder hung from its rungs, spanned between two lonely hooks, or left standing on damp concrete is being bent, loaded, or corroded a little at a time, and none of it announces itself until the ladder is back in use, under a person, at height.

Proper storage is not complicated, but it is specific. It starts with two measurements most guides skip, follows support rules that come straight from ladder guidance rather than garage folklore, and ends with the inspection habit that storage is supposed to make possible. Get those three pieces right and the ladder comes off the wall in the same condition it went up — which is the entire point.

Start with two numbers: collapsed length and actual weight

An extension ladder's working length is not its storage length, and planning from the wrong number is how a mount ends up two feet short of wall. A typical two-story house needs an extension ladder of 24 to 28 feet, and that ladder is still about 14 feet long fully collapsed; against a typical garage length of about 22 feet, one stored ladder consumes well over half the wall (s2). Measure the ladder collapsed, then measure the uninterrupted wall run — clear of door swings, shelving ends, and the arc of a car door — and only then choose horizontal, vertical, or ceiling placement. If the collapsed ladder is longer than any honest wall run, the ceiling stops being a compromise and becomes the plan.

Weight decides the hardware. Published Werner range figures put fiberglass step ladders at 16 lb for a 4-foot model up to 40 lb at 10 feet, aluminum step ladders at 11 to 31 lb over the same sizes, fiberglass extension ladders at 41 lb (16 ft) to 126 lb (40 ft), and aluminum extension ladders at 30 to 89 lb (s2). Large extension ladders typically run 50 to 75 lb (s2). That spread is the whole hardware decision: a mount that is generous for a 20 lb step ladder is undersized for a mid-range extension ladder, and no hook should be chosen until the ladder's actual weight — from its label or the manufacturer's table for its model — is sitting next to the hook's rating. Weigh the ladder on a bathroom scale if the label is gone; the number takes a minute and governs everything after it.

The support rules that keep a stored ladder straight

Three rules do most of the protective work, and they are the ones the average storage guide omits. First, supports contact the stiles — the side rails — never the rungs. Loading a ladder's weight onto its rungs in storage can cause micro-deformation over time, particularly on aluminium (s1). Hooks and rails should be positioned and padded to bear on the stiles, which are the structural members designed to carry load.

Second, the number of support points scales with length. Step ladders need at least two support points, positioned to distribute the weight evenly, one toward the top and one toward the bottom (s1). For extension ladders over 3 metres, the minimum rises to three support points on a horizontal wall rack, because a long ladder stored across only two points can develop a permanent bow along the stiles — a bend that then changes how the ladder sits and distributes load in use (s1). Werner's own storage instruction makes the same point from the other direction: support the ladder horizontally to prevent warping (s3). A bowed stile is not a cosmetic defect; it is the storage method becoming a safety defect on a delay.

Third, the storage environment is part of the mount. Ladders belong in a dry, covered location, away from extreme heat, moisture, and direct sunlight — Werner states the dry, secure, away-from-extremes rule directly, and excessive heat may weaken a ladder (s3, s4). The material decides which enemy matters most: aluminium does not rust but corrodes where its stiles sit on damp concrete, fibreglass degrades under sustained UV as its resin breaks down, and timber needs dry, ventilated storage against swelling and rot (s1). Every one of those failure modes is a floor-storage or outdoor-storage failure, which is why the guides converge on the same floor rule: flat on the floor is not a routine storage method — it adds trip hazards, hides one face from inspection, and parks the ladder in the dampest layer of the room (s1). Even a low rack that lifts the ladder a few centimetres off the concrete removes the damp contact entirely (s1).

Wall, ceiling, or vertical: choosing by ladder type

Step ladders are the easy case: wall hooks at two support points, bearing on the stiles, at a height that clears car doors yet stays low enough to lift the ladder down without overreaching — if retrieving the ladder requires standing on something, the mount is too high (s1). Extension ladders up to about 3 metres collapsed fit the same wall treatment with three supports once past that length threshold (s1). Long and heavy extension ladders belong on the wall rather than overhead: at 50 to 75 lb typical, a large ladder meets or exceeds what ceiling systems publish — the StoreYourBoard double ladder ceiling rack, for example, is rated at 150 lb for two ladders combined — while a pair of stud-mounted wall hooks rated 70 lb each carries the same ladder with margin to spare (s2). Ceiling storage earns its place for lighter ladders in garages where wall and floor are genuinely exhausted: it lifts the ladder clear of foot traffic entirely, provided the hardware's load rating is checked against the specific ladder's weight and the mounts land in joists, with straps to stop the ladder shifting (s1). Vertical storage suits short ladders in tight corners, but only secured — a ladder stored flat and folded shut, equally supported along its length so it does not warp, and fastened so it cannot tip (s2, s3). The unsecured lean against the wall fails every version of that test.

Storing a ladder properly, step by step

  1. Clean the ladder before it goes away: mild soap and water, rinse, and let it dry completely, so stored contamination cannot mask damage or make rungs slippery later (s3).
  2. Inspect it on the way in, using the manufacturer's checklist: rungs for damage or looseness, side rails for cracks, dents, or splinters, rung locks, rope, and pulley for smooth operation, feet for wear and intact traction, and bolts and rivets for anything loose or missing (s3, s4). A ladder that fails inspection does not get stored — it leaves service.
  3. Measure the ladder collapsed and weigh it (or read the weight from its label), then measure the clear wall run or ceiling span it has to fit.
  4. Choose the mount from the decision framework above and verify the arithmetic: total ladder weight against the combined rating of the supports at the actual mounting surface — stud, not drywall, for anything heavy (s2).
  5. Install supports into studs or joists at the right count for the ladder's length — two points minimum, three for anything over 3 metres — positioned and padded to bear on the stiles, never the rungs (s1).
  6. Set the height so the stored ladder clears car doors and foot traffic but can be lifted down without overreaching, then hang the ladder flat and folded shut and secure it against shifting (s1, s2).
  7. Repeat the inspection every time the ladder comes down, before use — stiles, rungs, and treads straight, spreaders sound, feet intact, no fresh contamination — with a detailed inspection at least every six months for a ladder in regular use (s4, s1).

What success looks like: the ladder hangs flat and fully supported on its stiles at the right number of points for its length, its weight sits well inside the mounts' stud-anchored ratings, it clears the car door below it, and it comes down clean, straight, and inspection-ready — with no bow, no corrosion at the stile feet, and nothing to discover at the top of the first climb.

FAQ

Where is the best place to hang a ladder in a garage?

On a wall, on stud-mounted hooks or a rack that supports the stiles at two points minimum — three for ladders over 3 metres — high enough to clear car doors and low enough to retrieve without overreaching (s1). The ceiling is the fallback for lighter ladders when no wall run fits the collapsed length (s1, s2).

Can a ladder be stored vertically?

Yes, if it is folded shut and secured against tipping; Werner's guidance accepts horizontal storage or secure vertical fastening as the two sound options (s3). An unsecured lean is not vertical storage — it is a fall waiting for a bump.

Is it safe to store a ladder on the garage ceiling?

For lighter ladders, yes, with two conditions: the rack's rating must cover the specific ladder's weight — a published double ceiling rack tops out at 150 lb for two ladders — and the mounts must anchor into joists, with the ladder strapped so it cannot shift (s2, s1). Heavy extension ladders at 50–75 lb and up are better carried on stud-mounted wall hooks (s2).

General information only. Ladders are safety equipment: follow the manufacturer's storage and inspection instructions for your model, never use a ladder that fails inspection, and do not attempt repairs to damaged ladder components — the manufacturer directs those to qualified service (s3).
