How competitive players warm up: practice routines that transfer to a new game

Practice · Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

A warm-up is a short, fixed routine run at deliberately sub-maximal effort, in the minutes before real play, so the first real minutes are not spent cold. It is not training, not a workout, and not a place to set records. That definition sounds obvious until the published routines are compared — because the advice on how long it should take, and what it should contain, openly disagrees with itself.

Nearly all of that advice was also written for PC players: mouse and keyboard, first-person shooters, aim trainers. Most open-world players play on a controller, in third person. What follows keeps the parts of the documented routines that survive that change of hardware, rebuilds the parts that do not, and marks which is which.

What a warm-up is actually for

The clearest statement of purpose in the documented routines is a negative one: the goal is not to peak. One routine runs its blocks at 70 to 80 percent intensity for exactly that reason. A warm-up that ends with a personal best has usually overshot — it spent the sharpness it was meant to save.

Skipping has a cost at the other end. Warm-up guidance warns that starting cold leads to losing the early rounds of a match — the first real contest doubles as the warm-up, against opponents who did theirs beforehand. Between those two failure modes, peaking and skipping, sits the whole craft of the thing.

The dose: minutes and intensity, reconciled

Read together, the three stop conflicting. The overlap is roughly a quarter of an hour: long enough to load every aiming sub-skill, short enough to finish before fatigue starts charging interest. The practical rule that falls out of the overlap:

  • Default to 15 minutes. It sits inside every documented range.
  • Short on time? Ten minutes, structured, beats fifteen vague ones — the timed-block routine proves a short dose can still cover the ground.
  • Thirty minutes is a ceiling breach, not thoroughness. Past that point the documented risk is fatigue and frustration carried into real play.
  • Intensity stays at roughly three-quarter effort throughout. Sharp, not maximal.

The drill blocks, adapted for controller

The best-documented block order runs four aiming skills in sequence: a target-switching drill first, then tracking, then flicks — fast snaps between targets — then precision shots last. Timed, that order fills ten minutes as 0–2 minutes on the first block, 2–5 on tracking, 5–8 on flicks, and 8–10 on precision. The order transfers to a controller intact. The exercises inside the blocks change:

  1. Target switching (0–2 min). In a quiet area of a game already owned, pick two visible objects at different distances and move the aim point between them, settling fully on each before moving. Slow is fine — this block wakes the hands up.
  2. Tracking (2–5 min). Follow a moving target — a vehicle crossing the view, a character walking — keeping the aim point on it without firing. Smooth stick pressure is the whole exercise.
  3. Flicks (5–8 min). Snap the aim from centre to a new target at the edge of the view, stop dead on it, then return to centre. The stop matters more than the speed.
  4. Precision (8–10 min). Small, distant targets, fired on only when the aim has fully settled. Misses caused by rushing end the block early — drop back to tracking for the remaining time instead.
  5. Game transfer (final minutes). The documented routines end by practising the real skills the session will use, in the real game. Hold that rule on a controller too: the last block belongs in the game about to be played, not in a trainer.

One piece of the PC guidance does transfer unchanged, and one does not. Keeping the crosshair at head height, at the edge of the next angle — a technique the PC routines call slicing the pie — has a direct third-person cousin: keep the camera and aim point where a threat would appear, not pointed at the ground ahead. The mouse-specific machinery does not transfer: sensitivity measured in centimetres per full turn, and mouse DPI ranges, have no controller equivalent, and chasing them wastes the warm-up they were meant to improve.

Launch week vs ranked week: two routines

A warm-up built to maintain form in a known game answers a different question from a warm-up in a new game's first week. In ranked week, the game's feel is already known; the routine's job is consistency — the same fifteen minutes, repeated daily, unchanged for at least a week at a time.

Launch week inverts it. The feel is the unknown, so the warm-up moves inside the new game itself:

  1. Run the four blocks in the game's own quiet corners — an empty street for switching and tracking, an open view for flicks and precision.
  2. Keep the dose at fifteen minutes and the intensity low; the session that follows is the real teacher.
  3. Note one setting or habit per session that felt wrong, and change only that one thing before the next session.
  4. After a week of the same routine, keep what survived and rebuild the rest — the documented rule about repeating a warm-up for a week before changing it applies to the new routine too.

When to stop: fatigue signs and the reset

The dose evidence draws the outer line: routines stretching toward thirty minutes risk fatigue and frustration, and a frustrated warm-up taxes the session it precedes. Inside the routine, the stop signs are plainer. Grip tightening on the controller. Aim getting faster and sloppier at the same time. A drill repeated in anger rather than in sequence. Any one of them ends the warm-up early, without guilt — a short, calm warm-up beats a complete, ragged one.

Then take the reset the documented routines prescribe before starting: one to two minutes standing, breathing, visualising the first minutes of play. It reads like a ritual because it is one — the boundary between practising and playing, drawn on purpose.
