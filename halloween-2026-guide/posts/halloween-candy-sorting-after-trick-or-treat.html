Sorting Halloween candy after trick-or-treating: inspect, store, donate

Planning · 9 min read

The bags come through the door on Saturday night, October 31, 2026, and the sorting that happens in the next half hour decides everything that follows for weeks: what gets eaten safely, what keeps well, what can genuinely be given away, and what should never have stayed in the house. Most advice stops at “check the candy,” as if inspection were the whole job. It is only the first lane. This guide runs the full system in one sitting — inspect, sort into four lanes, store each type for as long as that type actually keeps, and donate only what a recipient is allowed to accept.

The four lanes: eat now, store, donate, discard

Every single item lands in exactly one lane, and the lanes are decided in this order, because each one is a gate for the next:

LaneWhat goes in itRule
DiscardAnything opened or unwrapped, damaged or unsafe-looking; homemade treats from the route; anything with a torn wrapper, discoloration, a strange appearance or tiny pinholesDecided first, by inspection only. No tasting, no negotiating — suspicious means out.
Hold asideChoking hazards for the youngest children — gum, peanuts, hard candies and small toys — plus anything whose label names an allergen in the house, or has no label to checkNot discarded, not shared. Stored separately, for older children or adults only, or removed if nobody can eat it safely.
DonateSealed, unopened, undamaged, properly labelled candy within its best-before date, beyond what the household will eatMust pass the acceptance test in the donation section below — sealed and unexpired is the entry ticket, not a courtesy.
Store / eat nowThe favourites that remain, sorted by typeKept only as long as that type’s storage window — the table below, not enthusiasm, sets the calendar.

Two official rules sit underneath the whole table. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s advice, as reproduced in hospital and public-health summaries, is that children should not eat treats until they have been inspected at home — which is why a snack or light meal before going out matters: it removes the temptation to eat uninspected candy on the route. Health Canada gives the same inspection duty to the adults at home: inspect all treats before children eat them. Sorting is therefore not the reward after the inspection; the sorting is the inspection, done item by item, in good light.

The sorting and inspection procedure

  1. Empty every bag onto a clean table, one child at a time. Keep each child’s haul separate — labels on bags matter when one haul contains an allergen another child can eat. Work in bright light, where wrapper damage and discoloration are actually visible. Success check: nothing is eaten during the sort, by anyone.
  2. Run the discard pass first. Remove anything opened or unwrapped, damaged or unsafe-looking, and homemade items collected on the route. Inspect commercially wrapped treats closely for the FDA’s tampering signs: tears in wrappers, discoloration, a strange appearance, or tiny pinholes — anything that looks suspicious is thrown away. Success check: every remaining item is commercially wrapped, sealed and normal-looking.
  3. Pull the hazards for the youngest children. From each young child’s pile, remove gum, peanuts, hard candies and small toys. Health Canada’s line is specific: do not give small, hard or chewy candies to children under the age of 3, because they are a choking risk. Check toys and novelty items for small parts and button batteries, and keep those away from young children too. Success check: a child under 3 could be handed anything left in their pile without a second look.
  4. Read the labels for allergies. For any child with a food allergy, check the label on every item to confirm the allergen is not among the ingredients — and keep home-baked goods out entirely, because there is no label to check. Items with missing or unclear ingredient information go to the hold-aside lane. Success check: every item in an allergic child’s keep pile has been read, not assumed.
  5. Let each child pick the favourites. This is the American Dental Association’s practical suggestion in action: a family picks a few favourites and donates the rest. Favourites go to the store lane; the sealed surplus goes to the donate lane while it is still sealed, labelled and in date. Success check: the keep pile is a deliberate selection, small enough to finish inside its storage windows.
  6. Sort the keepers by type and container them. Chocolate together, hard candy together, gummies and soft candy together — because each type has a different window in the table below. Move candy out of the open collection bowl into sealed containers, in a cool, dark and dry place. Success check: every container is labelled with its type, so its window can be checked at a glance in the weeks ahead.
  7. Box the donations the same night. Sealed surplus, kept dry and unopened, goes into a donation box while the decision is fresh — donations sorted on November 1 are donations that actually happen. Success check: by bedtime, the house holds only the keep lane and one labelled donation box.

The storage table: how long each type actually keeps

“Store it somewhere cool” is not a shelf life. The windows below come from National Confectioners Association guidance reported by a Virginia Tech food-science expert and by Hackensack Meridian Health. They are quality windows for sealed candy stored properly — cool, dark and dry — not permission to ignore a best-before date printed on a package, which always wins.

Candy typeQuality windowNotes
Dark chocolate1 to 2 yearsThe longest keeper: kept wrapped in foil in a cool, dark, dry place. A pale surface coating called bloom can appear over time; it is not harmful.
Milk and white chocolate8 to 10 months, maximumChocolate fats oxidize when exposed to oxygen, causing staleness and off-flavours; higher cocoa content lasts longer, which is why milk and white run shorter than dark.
Hard candyUp to 1 yearAt room temperature or in a cool, dry location. Still a hold-aside item for children under 3, however long it keeps.
Gummies6 to 9 monthsCounted from the date of purchase in the reported guidance.
Caramels and jelly beans (soft candy)6 to 9 months opened; up to 1 year unopenedOpening the package starts the shorter clock.
Candy corn3 to 6 months opened; 9 months unopenedThe shortest window in the table — eat it, donate it sealed, or let it go first.

Two habits make those windows real instead of theoretical. First, eat candy with meals, or right afterward: the ADA’s reasoning is that the mouth makes extra saliva while eating, which helps clear sugar and acid, while frequent snacking spreads the exposure across the whole day. A planned treat time — after dinner, from the labelled container — also ends the nightly negotiation the sorting night was meant to prevent. Second, give the whole stash an end date when it goes into the cupboard. One clinician quoted in the Hackensack Meridian guidance suggests tossing whatever Halloween candy remains by the time Easter comes, so excess candy is never around longer than about six months regardless of type. That is a stricter rule than the table’s longest windows, and for a mixed family stash it is the simpler one to keep.

Donating what leaves the house

Donation fails in one predictable way: a bag of mixed, opened, undated candy arrives somewhere that cannot accept it. Canadian food banks state the acceptance test plainly. The Compass food bank in Mississauga, Ontario, for example, requires food donations to be sealed, unopened and non-expired, within best-before dates, properly labelled and in undamaged packaging — and homemade food is not accepted. Run the donate lane against exactly that test before choosing a destination: sealed, unopened, in date, labelled, undamaged. Anything failing one item on that list is not a donation; it is the discard lane wearing a costume.

For candy that passes, two routes appear consistently in current guidance. Some dental offices run candy buyback events after Halloween — ask the office directly, because events are local and annual details change. Troopathon’s Halloween Candy Buyback, which sends candy in care packages, has suggested collecting candy from November 1 to 7 and says it can be sent any time before the end of the year; its own guidance is to check the website for current details before shipping anything. Local food banks and community organizations are the third route, subject to the sealed-and-unexpired test above — call or check the donation page first rather than arriving with a mixed bag. Whichever route is chosen, the work was already done on sorting night: the donation box holds only items that were sealed at the door, inspected at the table, and set aside on purpose.

FAQ

Does candy really need inspecting if the neighbourhood is familiar? Yes — and the inspection is mostly not about strangers. The FDA and Health Canada checks catch damaged packaging, choking hazards for small children, and allergens with no label, which exist on every route. Inspect all treats before children eat them; throw away anything opened, unwrapped, damaged or homemade.

How long can the keep pile honestly last? By type, not by hope: dark chocolate one to two years stored cool, dark and dry; milk and white chocolate eight to ten months at most; hard candy up to a year; gummies and soft candy six to nine months; opened candy corn three to six months. When in doubt, the six-month Easter rule is the clean cutoff.

Can opened candy be donated? No. Food-bank acceptance rules require donations sealed, unopened, non-expired and properly labelled, and homemade food is not accepted. Opened surplus belongs in the household keep lane, inside its window — or in the discard lane.

What about the candy nobody picked as a favourite? That is the system working. The ADA’s suggestion is to pick a few favourites and donate the rest — sealed, in date, and boxed on sorting night, while every option is still open.
