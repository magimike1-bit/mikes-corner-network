Halloween night safety: the final checks to do before you head out tonight

Safety · 7 min read · Published October 31, 2026

Do these checks tonight, in this order: walk the porch and path while there is still light, fix visibility and agree the route and return time at dusk, hold the door rules steady while you are out, and inspect every treat at home before anyone eats. That is the whole piece. Everything below is those four passes, timed for Saturday, October 31, 2026 — a final-check run for tonight, not a repeat of the general safety guide.

Late afternoon, while it is still light: the porch and walkway pass

The cheapest safety work of the night happens before the first costume goes on, because hazards are easier to see in daylight than by porch light. Clear leaves, hoses, toys and other tripping hazards from walkways, and sweep wet leaves off pathways, steps and porches — wet leaves are slippery under costume shoes. Check that stairs, decks, porches and handrails are secure, and keep decorations away from stairs and walking paths.

Then do the lighting half of the pass. Turn on exterior and pathway lighting and replace any burned-out bulb now, while a replacement is still a two-minute job. Health Canada’s decorating guidance makes the same two moves: keep walkways clear of obstacles, and turn on outdoor lighting to improve visibility and welcome visitors. Use only outdoor-rated electrical decorations and extension cords outdoors, plugged into GFCI-protected receptacles, with cords kept out of driveways and walkways. Swap real candles in pumpkins for battery-operated lights, and check decorative lights for damaged wires and loose connections — only lights carrying a certification mark such as CSA, cUL or cETL belong outside.

At dusk: visibility, route and the return time

Visibility is a costume check, not a shopping trip. Health Canada advises costumes that fit well — long or loose costumes may lead to trips and falls — in bright colours, or with reflective tape or a vest added to be more visible at night. The CDC’s SAFE HALLOWEEN tips land in the same place: fasten reflective tape to costumes and bags, hold a flashlight to help you see and be seen, and wear well-fitting masks, costumes and shoes to avoid blocked vision, trips and falls. Face paint instead of a mask is the fix when a mask does not fit properly, because a poor fit can block vision and make breathing difficult.

That table follows Health Canada’s supervision split directly: young children always supervised, older children in groups with a planned route and a set return time. Agreeing the return time out loud, before anyone leaves, is the step that prevents the late-night negotiation on a doorstep an hour later.

While you are out: the door rules that do not change

  1. Only visit homes with the exterior lights on. The porch light is the street’s signal in both directions: yours on welcomes visitors to your door, and a dark house on the route is a house to pass, every time.
  2. Stay in well-lit areas, on sidewalks or paths. Where there is no sidewalk, walk on the far edge of the road facing traffic.
  3. Cross at intersections and designated crosswalks, looking both ways first — and never cross between parked cars, because they block the view of oncoming drivers.
  4. Walk, do not run, flashlight in hand. Running in a costume that already narrows vision is how the ordinary trip happens.
  5. Never enter a stranger’s home or car, even when invited. Treats change hands on the porch.
  6. Keep clear of lit candles and luminaries. Candle-lit pumpkins belong away from doorsteps and walkways, and no costume goes near an open flame to get a closer look.
What success looks like: every child can be picked out by a driver at a crosswalk, the group is on the agreed streets, the return time was set before departure — and the treat bags come home unopened.

Home again: the candy check before anyone eats

The last pass happens at the kitchen table, and it is not optional or postponable to morning. Health Canada’s instruction is to inspect all treats before children eat them, and the FDA guidance reported this week says the same in more detail: wait until children get home before inspecting, and check wrappers for tears, pinholes, discoloration or other signs of damage or tampering. A meal or snack before trick-or-treating is what makes the wait bearable — it discourages sampling along the way — and hands get washed before anyone eats.

Throw away anything opened or unwrapped, damaged or unsafe-looking, or homemade. For very young children, pull out the choking hazards before the bag goes any further: Health Canada names small, hard or chewy candies for children under 3 years, and the FDA list for small children adds gum, peanuts and small toys. Check toys and novelty items for small parts and button batteries, and read labels where a food allergy is in the house. Anything suspicious goes in the bin, not back in the bag for a second opinion tomorrow.

Four passes, one evening. Porch in daylight, visibility at dusk, door rules on the street, candy on the table at home. Run them in that order tonight and the safety work is done before it can be forgotten in the excitement of the first doorbell.
