How much Halloween candy to buy in the final days: estimating by foot traffic and past years

Budget · 6 min read · Published October 29, 2026

The answer fits in one line: expected trick-or-treaters, times two fun-size pieces each, plus about ten per cent for breathing room. Fifty kids means roughly 110 pieces; a hundred means roughly 220. That formula — the Farmers’ Almanac’s current version of the standard advice — has not changed. What changes in the final two days before Saturday, October 31, 2026 is the quality of the number you feed into it. In September the visitor count is a guess. On October 29 it is a reconstruction, because last year actually happened and this year’s signals are standing in your street, visible from the porch.

Reconstruct last year from the bags

Most households never counted their visitors — but they did buy bags, and some of them remember the leftovers. That is enough. The Almanac’s worked hint: if you started last year with 200 pieces, handed out two at a time, and had roughly 40 left at the end, you probably saw around 80 trick-or-treaters. Run your own version of the same subtraction:

  1. Pieces you started with. Bags bought, times the piece count printed on each bag — variety bags give an approximate count, which is all this math needs.
  2. Minus pieces left over when the porch light went off. Be honest about the bowl that went to the office.
  3. Divide by pieces per child — two, for most houses. The answer is last year’s visitor count, close enough to plan on.

No memory of last year at all? Ask the neighbours — this week, not next. A few nearby neighbours reporting what a normal Halloween looks like on your block is more useful than any general figure, and October 29 is when they remember last year most clearly. As a cross-check, one candy guide’s traffic bands put a quiet street at roughly 10–25 visitors, a typical suburban neighbourhood at 25–75, and a known trick-or-treating destination at 75–200 or more.

The final-days signal check

Last year’s count is the base. These are the adjustments — every one of them checkable today, none of them available in September.

The day itselfHalloween 2026 falls on Saturday, October 31 — confirmed in the Almanac’s Halloween guideUp. A Friday or Saturday Halloween tends to draw higher turnout than a weeknight, because families have more flexibility.
The weather outlookThe forecast for Saturday evening, two days outA cold or rainy Halloween keeps trick-or-treaters closer to home and can cut traffic significantly; a clear, mild evening brings out more kids, walking further.

Apply the signals as a nudge, not a doubling. Two signals pointing up might justify planning one traffic band higher; a grim forecast pulls the Saturday bump back down. The ten per cent cushion in the formula absorbs the small misses. It was never sized to absorb a guess that ignored the week you are actually in.

Stage the buy: core, reserve, pacing

  1. Buy the core now. Expected visitors times two pieces, plus the ten per cent cushion, bought by the piece count on the bag — not by how the cart looks. A bag holding about 70 pieces means three bags for a 220 target; a bag holding about 110 means two.
  2. Hold one bag back, sealed. Do not put everything in the bowl at once. Start the night with the core; keep one bag in reserve. If the sidewalk is packed, the backup exists. If traffic is light, the reserve is still an unopened bag at the end of the night — the cheapest possible form of insurance, because nothing about it has been spent into an open bowl.
  3. Pace the handout, not the panic. Two pieces per visitor while the street is busy. If a mountain of candy remains when the knocks slow to an occasional one, that is the moment to become the house handing out three or four. The reverse order — fistfuls early, rationing at the end — is the one version of the night nobody enjoys.
  4. End the night with the light. When the candy is done, the porch light goes off. Families out on the route only visit homes with the exterior lights on — Health Canada’s wording — so a dark porch is the understood signal that a house has finished. Running out with the light off is a normal ending, not a failure.

A worked example, assumptions labelled

Assume — and these are assumptions to replace with your own numbers, not findings — a house that reconstructs last year at about 80 visitors. The signals this week: Saturday (up), a decent forecast (neutral to up), a modest display (neutral), a street much like last year (neutral), handing out in person (neutral). Plan on 90 visitors, a nudge up rather than a leap. Ninety times two pieces is 180; plus ten per cent is 198 — call it 200 pieces, staged as roughly 160 in the bowl and one sealed bag of about 40 in reserve. If Saturday underdelivers, the household is holding one unopened bag and a modest bowl surplus. If it overdelivers, the reserve opens and the late-night handout stays at two. Handing out full-size bars instead? The math collapses to one bar per expected visitor, plus a few extras.

When the extra bag is not worth it

There is no rule that a perfect Halloween ends with an empty bowl — and no rule that it ends with a month of mini bars in the pantry either. The honest version is a decision, made today: if this household likes the candy, a modest surplus bought on purpose is part of the plan, so buy varieties nobody will mind meeting again on November 1. If leftovers are a genuine cost here — money spent to be thrown at a break room in December — then hold the estimate tight, keep the reserve sealed, and resist the giant emergency bag at the checkout. What the final-days version of this decision never does is buy fear in bulk: two days out, the evidence is as good as it is going to get, and the count — not the anxiety — should set the size of the pile.
