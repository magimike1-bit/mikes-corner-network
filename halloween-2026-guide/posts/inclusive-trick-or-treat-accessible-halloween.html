Inclusive trick-or-treating: making your home accessible for every child

Inclusion · 8 min read

As many as 400,000 children across Canada with disabilities face barriers that can sideline them from trick-or-treating — and the barrier is often as simple as the stairs to a front door. That is the finding behind Treat Accessibly, the Canadian movement founded in 2017 by Rich Padulo and his family after they realized a neighbour who used a wheelchair could not easily reach their home on Halloween. More than 100,000 Canadian households now display its orange lawn sign. The fixes are small, free or nearly free, and mostly finished before Halloween night. This guide turns them into a host-side setup: where to put your treat station, what each barrier actually needs, and the order to do it in. (Food allergy specifically — the teal pumpkin system — has its own guide on this site; here it is one row of a bigger table.)

Choose your station location: a decision framework

The single highest-impact change is moving the treats to the child, instead of asking the child to come to the treats. Treat Accessibly’s core advice: set up the trick-or-treating station at a step-free spot — the end of the driveway, the garage, the lawn near the sidewalk, or a building lobby. Pick yours with three questions:

QuestionIf yesIf no
Can every child reach your front door without stairs, inclines, or curving narrow paths?The door can stay the station — still clear and light the path.Move the station to the driveway end, garage, or curbside.
Do you have a driveway or garage that is not too steep?Station there; park the vehicle on the street or in the garage to keep access open.Use the lawn near the sidewalk — or trunk-or-treat from your vehicle.
Do you live in an apartment or condo?Create a communal trick-or-treat spot in the lobby, where the route is already step-free.Not applicable — the house options above apply.

Whichever location wins, the path to it must be clear of obstacles and well-lit, and the station itself — table, chairs, treat bowl — sits where a child using a wheelchair, walker, or other mobility device can roll straight up to it and reach the bowl without navigating stairs.

The barrier-by-barrier comparison

"Accessible" is not one fix. Treat Accessibly organizes its tips by barrier, and each barrier has a different, specific remedy:

BarrierWhat blocks the childThe fix at your home
MobilityStairs, inclines, curving or uneven walkways, obstacles on the pathStep-free station at driveway, garage, lawn near sidewalk, or lobby; path cleared and well-lit; vehicle moved out of the way
SensoryStrobe and flashing lights, high-pitched or sudden loud noises, jump scares, loud musicSteady (not flashing) lighting; lower or remove sudden sounds; greet calmly; no jump-scare decorations at the station
Neurological & developmentalUnpredictable interactions, pressure to perform the ritualKeep it simple, predictable, and friendly; smile and be patient; never require a child to speak; take cues from parents when a child does not respond
CommunicationExpectation of a spoken "trick or treat" or "thank you"Do not expect verbal responses — some children are non-verbal or communicate differently; offer the treat the same way regardless
Allergy & healthOnly food on offer; some children cannot eat orally at allOffer non-edible options — stickers, pencils, glow sticks, crayons, small Halloween toys — kept as a genuine alternative, not an afterthought
PetsDogs at the door can frighten some childrenKeep pets safely away from the front of the house; a bonus of an outdoor station is no doorbell ringing, which also lowers stress for pets inside

The setup procedure: two weeks out to Halloween night

  1. About two weeks before (by Sat, Oct 17, 2026): get the sign. Treat Accessibly’s free Accessible Trick-or-Treating lawn sign can go out up to two weeks before Halloween — and at minimum a week before — because families and caregivers plan their routes in advance. A sign that appears on October 31 is a welcome gesture; a sign that appears on October 20 changes where families actually go. Signs are available through treataccessibly.com and at local Pet Valu stores. Success check: the sign is visible from the sidewalk by October 24 at the latest.
  2. One week before: choose and test the station. Run the location framework above, then physically test the winner: walk the route from the sidewalk at dusk, note every obstacle, step, and dark patch, and fix them. Clear the driveway and pathways, and decide where the vehicle goes on the night (street or garage). Success check: an unobstructed, well-lit, step-free path from sidewalk to treat bowl.
  3. Two days before: audit your decorations. Remove or disable strobe lights and anything that makes high-pitched, sudden loud noises near the station. Swap flashing effects for steady lighting. Keep scary elements away from the station itself — atmosphere belongs in the yard, not at the point where a child must stand. Success check: standing at the station for two minutes produces no flash, bang, or jump.
  4. Halloween afternoon: stock two bowls. One with regular treats, one with non-food treats (stickers, pencils, glow sticks, small toys) for children with severe food allergies or who are not orally fed. Offer both neutrally — "candy or a treat?" — and let the child or parent choose. Plan the pet’s indoor spot away from the front of the house. Success check: both bowls are within reach of a seated child, and the non-food bowl is not an empty gesture.
  5. On the night: greet without barriers. Greet calmly, with a smile, at the station. If a child does not say "trick or treat," does not respond, or communicates differently, hand over the treat exactly as you would for any other child, and look to the parents for cues rather than prompting the child. Consider starting earlier, before dark, so more families can participate in natural daylight. Success check: every child who reaches your station leaves with a treat, and none had to climb, shout, or explain anything to get it.
What success looks like overall: a family that planned its route around your sign finds exactly what the sign promised — a step-free, calm, well-lit station where their child is treated like every other trick-or-treater on the street.

Beyond your own driveway

Households are not the only route to an inclusive Halloween. Treat Accessibly runs free Halloween Villages in some communities, and municipalities hold sensory-friendly events of their own: the City of Surrey’s, for example, offers a calm, low-sensory environment with no loud noises, strobe lights, or jump scares, quiet spaces for breaks, and a wheelchair- and stroller-accessible venue, free of charge. Children’s Health Foundation hosts an accessible trick-or-treat event with partner organizations in London, Ontario. For a child whose barriers a street route cannot fully remove, an event like these is not a lesser Halloween — it is the same stations, crafts, and costumes at a pace the child sets. And for hosts, the same principles scale: a school, mall, or apartment lobby running an accessible station is doing the driveway move at building size.

FAQ

Is this the same as the teal pumpkin? No. A teal pumpkin signals non-food treats for food allergies — one barrier. Treat Accessibly is the wider frame: mobility, sensory, communication, and developmental access, signalled by its orange lawn sign. Many homes do both.

What does it cost? The lawn sign is free, the station is a table you already own, and the main investment is an hour of setup. Non-food treats are the only purchase, and a bag of stickers or pencils covers the children who need them.

My front door is up a full flight of stairs. Is the driveway really enough? Yes — that is precisely the situation the program was built for. Treats at the foot of the driveway, garage, or curbside remove the barrier entirely; the child never has to navigate the stairs at all.

What if a child does not say anything? Treat them the same. Some children are non-verbal or communicate differently; York Region’s guidance is to be patient, avoid expecting verbal responses, and never make the treat conditional on the ritual phrase.
