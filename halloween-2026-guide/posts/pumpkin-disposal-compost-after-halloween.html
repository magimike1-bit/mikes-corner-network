Pumpkin disposal and composting after Halloween: the November 1 guide

After Halloween · 8 min read

Canada produced 73,603 tonnes of pumpkins in 2022, and most of them were never food — they were decorations with a two-week lifespan and a November 1 problem. The scale matters because the destination matters: organic waste buried in a landfill produces methane as it decomposes, and Environment and Climate Change Canada notes Canadian landfills accounted for 17 per cent of the country’s methane emissions in 2023. Composting the same pumpkin avoids that methane and returns a nutrient-rich soil amendment instead. The catch is that "just compost it" hides real differences — by condition, and by city. This guide routes every pumpkin by what state it is actually in, then covers the one popular idea the conservation sources say to skip.

Decide by condition, not by habit

Before the routing table, one warning about local rules, because Canadian cities genuinely disagree. Port Coquitlam, BC tells residents to put pumpkins in the green cart with food scraps. Vaughan, Ontario routes pumpkins, cornstalks, and straw to leaf and yard waste instead, collected weekly until the week of December 5. Greater Sudbury splits the difference: intact pumpkins go beside the Green Cart; broken pumpkins go in certified compostable bags inside it. So the table below decides the kind of destination; your city’s waste page or 311 line decides the exact bin. Checking takes two minutes and prevents the whole load being rejected at the curb.

The November 1 prep procedure

  1. Strip every non-pumpkin part. Remove candles, tea light holders (metal or plastic), battery lights, pins, and any glued decorations. Port Coquitlam’s rule is explicit: nothing but pumpkin goes in the green cart. Success check: what remains is 100% pumpkin.
  2. Sort by condition using the table. Whole and sound goes to the kitchen or donation pile; carved and clean goes to compost; painted or glittered goes to garbage unless the decorated section can be cut away cleanly; mouldy goes straight to municipal organics. Success check: every pumpkin on the property has exactly one destination.
  3. Save the seeds worth saving. From uncarved pumpkins, seeds can be roasted as a snack; Ontario Parks staff also dry seeds to plant next year. Scoop them before anything goes to compost. Success check: seeds are washed and drying, or already seasoned and in the oven.
  4. Chop for the backyard route. For a home composter, chop pumpkin into thumb-sized pieces — Comox Valley’s guidance for backyard and worm bins — because small pieces break down far faster than a whole shell. Burying pieces in the garden also works; remove the seeds first if you do not want volunteer pumpkins next spring. Success check: pieces are thumb-sized, mixed into the pile or buried, not sitting on top.
  5. Set it out on your city’s terms. Green cart, leaf-and-yard bag, or beside the cart — whichever your municipality specifies — on the correct collection day. Vaughan’s leaf and yard waste, for instance, must be in Kraft paper bags or marked open-top rigid containers; plastic bags are not permitted. Success check: the pumpkin is at the curb in the right container, on the right day, with nothing non-compostable in it.
What success looks like: by the first full week of November, no pumpkin remains on the porch, none went to landfill that did not have to, and the painted one — the only true garbage in the pile — is the exception that proves the routing worked. Port Coquitlam’s math is the encouragement: if just one in four households composted their pumpkins, thousands of pounds of waste would be diverted.

Why "leave it for the animals" is bad advice

Every fall, social posts suggest tossing old pumpkins into a forest or field "for the wildlife." Conservation sources say do not — for reasons that go beyond tidiness. Ontario Parks is direct: provincial parks are not places for composting pumpkins, and a tossed pumpkin’s flesh decomposes but its seeds do not — they lie dormant, sprout in spring, and spread non-native pumpkin plants through protected areas, further scattered by animals storing seeds for winter. Those plants take space and resources from native plants and shrink the natural food supply. Pumpkin is also not natural food for park animals, and animals can get their heads or antlers stuck in a pumpkin while feeding — a frightening, potentially life-threatening trap. Colorado Parks and Wildlife adds the backyard version of the same problem: pumpkins left out attract bears loading calories before denning season, creating preventable human-bear conflicts; feeding deer can draw predators into residential areas; and artificial feeding crowds deer together, spreading disease. The kind move and the correct move are the same move: compost it, donate it to a farm program that actually wants it, or bin it — never dump it.

Do not forget the wrappers

One line for the rest of the November 1 sweep: candy wrappers are not the same everywhere either. Vaughan states plainly that candy wrappers go in the garbage there — none are recyclable in its program — while Port Coquitlam directs plastic wrappers to a participating Recycle BC depot with other soft plastics. Check the local rule before tossing them in the blue bin on instinct; a wrong guess contaminates a recycling load either way.

FAQ

Can a carved pumpkin be eaten? No — once carved and left outdoors it is a compost candidate, not food. Ontario Parks’ test is simple: only cook with a pumpkin that has not gone rotten, which in practice means uncarved. The seeds from carving day are the edible salvage.

How much difference does one pumpkin make? Individually, little; collectively, the Winnipeg Pumpkin Drop collected 15,558 lbs in a single day in 2024, sending over 2,425 lbs to an animal sanctuary for feed and the rest toward compost instead of landfill.

Can I bury a whole pumpkin in the garden? It will break down, but chopping works faster and burying pieces — with seeds removed — avoids a patch of surprise pumpkins next summer. That seed warning comes straight from Ontario Parks’ experience with dumped pumpkins.

What if my city has no organics program? Use the backyard route (chopped, in a composter or buried), a community drop-off or smash event, or a farm donation program. Landfill garbage is the last resort — and still the right call for a painted or glittered pumpkin if no other route accepts it.
