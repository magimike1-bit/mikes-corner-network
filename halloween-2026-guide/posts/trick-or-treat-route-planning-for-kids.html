Planning a trick-or-treat route: timing, distance, and a warm-up plan for kids

Family · 6 min read · Published October 29, 2026

Ten to fifteen houses. That is the reasonable target for a school-age child of about four to nine, per current parent guidance — with a planned break inside it. Toddlers manage about five houses. Young children generally find six to ten plenty. Those three numbers are the whole route plan in embryo: count the doors first, and the distance, the timing and the shape of the evening all follow from the count. Most route advice starts with a map. Start with a number instead.

Loop or line: pick the route shape

Two shapes cover almost every family route, and they fail in different ways — which is what makes the choice a real one.

The loopThe out-and-back line
ShapeA circuit that ends back at your house or carOne street out, the same street back
Why it worksYou never walk tired kids the long way home; every door is new groundSimple to explain and to preview; the return leg passes familiar, already-visited houses
The halfway pointThe far corner of the loop — name it out loud before leavingThe turnaround house — the door where the group turns for home, agreed in advance
Best forSchool-age children working toward the ten-to-fifteen door rangeYounger children, shorter counts, and any route being walked for the first time
Watch out forA loop sized by enthusiasm at the door, not by the door count set at the tableCounting doors on the way out only, then doubling the walk on the way back

Whichever shape wins, the ground rules for choosing the streets themselves are Health Canada’s: a safe route runs through populated, well-lit areas, on sidewalks or paths, visiting only homes with the exterior lights on. A short, familiar loop is easier to manage than a sprawling neighbourhood tour — the route your children already know beats a famous street across town, because familiar ground is what the warm-up below rehearses.

Timing: work backwards from home-by

Set the end before the start. Health Canada’s planning advice for older children is a planned route plus a set return home time — and the same discipline scales down to every age. Pick the home-by time first, then place the halfway point at the halfway clock time, not the halfway street: when the clock reaches the middle of the outing, wherever the group has got to is the turnaround. The door count is a target, not a contract.

Age moves the whole window earlier. Toddlers start before dark — out while it is still light, and home before the street gets busy. School-age children go with an adult within sight at all times, and their ten to fifteen doors come with a planned break, which belongs at the halfway point, on a lit corner, with the group together. Older children, about ten and up, travel in a group of at least three on a known route with a return time, a charged phone, and a check-in text at the halfway point. Dress for the second hour, not the first: Health Canada advises paying attention to the weather and bringing warmer clothes if the evening will cool down as it goes.

The Saturday warm-up plan

Halloween falls on Saturday, October 31, 2026 — which hands this year’s route something a weekday Halloween never has: a whole daylight day in front of it. The warm-up is a sequence, run in this order, and every step is borrowed from advice that usually sits scattered across five different pages.

  1. The route preview, in daylight. Walk or drive the planned route during the day. Note which houses look likely to take part, where the best-lit streets are, and where the halfway point actually falls. Success check: an adult can name the turnaround corner without checking a phone.
  2. The costume test walk. Costume on, one short walk around the block. A hem that drags is a trip hazard on stairs and doorsteps, and Health Canada warns that long or loose costumes may lead to trips and falls — a problem found at 11 am is a hem fixed by noon. Add reflective tape to the front, back and sides of the costume, and hand every child a flashlight.
  3. The door rehearsal. A family member plays the neighbour: approach the door and knock, say “trick or treat,” wait for the answer, say “thank you,” return to the sidewalk. Repeat a few times. A child who communicates by gesture, picture card or device rehearses exactly the same sequence in their own way — the routine is the point, not the script.
  4. The meeting spot, agreed and practised. Pick one spot — the corner house, the car — and practise the stay-put rule: if you can’t find me, go to the meeting spot and stay there. For older children heading out in a group, program the emergency contacts (a parent and one backup adult) and confirm location sharing works before anyone walks out the door.
  5. Fuel, then doors. An early, easy meal before the route — hunger is the most preventable mid-route collapse, and a fed child grazes less on uninspected candy along the way. Pack the extra layer and, for ambitious routes, a backup bag.
What success looks like: by the time the group leaves, the costume has already been walked in, the route has already been seen in daylight, the door routine has already been done at least once, and every child can say where the meeting spot is. The evening contains no firsts except the candy.

On the route: the rules that travel with you

Walking, not running, is the rule for the whole evening. Stay on sidewalks or paths; where there is no sidewalk, walk facing traffic and close to the edge. Stop at every curb and look left, right, and left again; cross at corners and crosswalks, never between parked cars. Young children stay supervised by an adult the whole way, and nobody — at any age — enters a stranger’s home or car for a treat. The halfway break is also the count check: doors done, doors left, energy left. If the count says twelve and the group says tired, the turnaround rule wins, because it was set at the kitchen table by people who were not yet excited.

The route card, filled in before costumes go on

Five lines, written down or said out loud as a set: door count ____ (five for a toddler, six to ten for a young child, ten to fifteen for school-age); shape ____ (loop or line); halfway point ____ (a named corner or house); home-by ____ (a clock time, agreed before leaving); meeting spot ____ (one place, known to everyone). Home again, the last step happens at the kitchen table, not the porch: inspect all treats before children eat them, and throw away anything opened or unwrapped, damaged or unsafe-looking, or homemade — with small, hard or chewy candies kept away from children under 3, who face a choking risk from them. A route that ends with the inspection is a route that is actually finished.
