Trick-or-treat safety guide for Canadian families (2026)

Safety · 7 min read

Halloween night concentrates everything that worries a parent into about three hours: dark streets, excited children, and traffic. Manitoba Public Insurance recorded eight pedestrian collisions on October 31 over the past four years — serious enough that the province put 86,580 flashing safety lights into elementary schools in 2025. Most Halloween pedestrian injuries happen between 6:00 and 9:00 p.m., when it's fully dark. Nearly all of it is preventable with visibility, age-matched supervision, and a planned route.

The age-by-age supervision framework

Canadian injury prevention specialists note that many children don't have the skills to cross roads safely until ages 9–11 — so supervision should step down gradually. The Canadian Paediatric Society's age bands:

The step most parents skip is the explicit return time. Set it before anyone leaves, and make sure every child knows what to do if separated from the group. The Canadian Red Cross also suggests noting where the Block Parent houses are on your route as safe fallback spots.

Route planning that keeps kids off the road

Most trick-or-treat injuries involve traffic, so treat the route as a safety device:

  1. Map one side of the street first. Plan the route so kids visit every house on one side before crossing once to the other side — no criss-crossing mid-block, ever.
  2. Pick the crossing points in advance. Cross only at intersections or crosswalks, never between parked cars. If the neighbourhood has no sidewalks, the rule is walk facing traffic on the left-hand edge.
  3. Choose porch-light houses only. A lit porch means the household is expecting trick-or-treaters — and a lit walkway means fewer trip hazards. Teach kids to skip dark houses without being told each time.
  4. Set the curfew and the check-in rule. Agree the return time before leaving (see the table above), and for older kids, the check-in interval. A watch works where a phone would be a distraction.
What success looks like: the group stays on sidewalks, crosses the street only as planned, and everyone's home on time. If you're shouting "stay together" more than twice, the group is too big — shorten the route.

Costume safety beyond the checklist

The Canadian Red Cross and the RCMP agree on the big three: bright and reflective beats dark every time; face paint beats masks, because masks restrict vision and movement (patch-test for sensitivities first); and short beats long — flowing fabric is a tripping hazard and, with cords or sashes, a strangulation risk. Two Canadian-specific notes: design the costume to work over winter clothing — Toronto Halloweens typically run 5–10°C, and a costume that can't fit over a coat gets abandoned by 7 p.m. — and keep everything away from open flames. LED tea lights (about $10 for a 24-pack) remove the jack-o'-lantern candle risk entirely.

Candy check and allergy planning

When the night's over, the candy gets inspected before anyone eats a piece: packaging intact, nothing opened, nothing homemade from unfamiliar houses. The RCMP adds a Canadian caution: after a 2022 incident of a child ingesting an edible cannabis product, check for THC/cannabis labelling and discard anything suspicious. Small, hard candies are a choking hazard for younger kids; set those aside.

For allergies, Food Allergy Canada's guidance is practical: carry the epi-pen and make sure others in the group know where it is, hold off on eating anything until labels are read at home, and tell neighbours ahead of time. Households can help with non-food treats — stickers and pencils work — and a teal lightbulb on the porch signals an allergy-aware house.

Drivers: slow below the posted limit in residential areas from 5:30 to 9:00 p.m., never assume a child sees your vehicle, and be prepared to stop — children dart between parked cars.

Saturday hours: when trick-or-treating starts in a weekend year

In 2026 Halloween falls on a Saturday, and that shifts the evening earlier. On a school night, most neighbourhoods see the first doorbells around 5:30 or 6:00; on a Saturday, families with younger kids head out in the late afternoon, as soon as costumes are on and supper is done. By the time it is fully dark, the streets have already been busy for an hour or more. Adjust the safety plan to match: put the reflective tape and glow sticks on before anyone leaves, even if it still looks light out — dusk is exactly when drivers struggle to see small pedestrians. If you are driving anywhere on Saturday afternoon or evening, treat residential streets as active from mid-afternoon, not just after dark. And set the return time with the earlier start in mind: a group that heads out at 5:00 can be home, candy checked, and winding down earlier than a weekday-year crew.

FAQ

What's the single most effective safety purchase? Reflective tape at $8–12 — the cheapest option in the table, and it works with headlights, which is what drivers use to see your child.

Can my 10-year-old go with friends? At 9–11, indirect supervision is the guideline: a small group, a planned familiar route, a charged phone, and a firm return time. Under 9, an adult or responsible older child should be present for every street crossing.

Should I worry about tampered candy? Reports of tampering are rare, but the inspection habit is cheap: check every wrapper, discard opened or suspicious items, and watch for cannabis labelling.
