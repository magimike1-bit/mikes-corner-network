External SSD vs External HDD for Backup: Speed, Shock, and Cost

Backup drives · Published 2026-10-27 · Last updated 2026-10-27

US$65 against about US$140. Those are the cited prices, from a 2026 GuideSpot analysis checked on 25 October 2026, for two 2TB portable drives: a Seagate Portable external HDD at approximately US$65, and a SanDisk Extreme portable SSD at about US$140. The SSD costs a little more than twice as much for the same capacity. What that extra money buys — and the one backup job where it buys the wrong thing — is the whole comparison.

These are cited figures from that analysis, not prices re-verified at a retailer for this page, so treat them as a dated snapshot: product, price, and check date together. Prices move; the ratio and the reasoning are the durable parts.

Speed, worked end to end: timing a 1TB backup

Interface peaks sell drives. Sustained rates time backups. The sourced sustained ranges are: external HDD at 80–160 MB/s, a SATA external SSD at 400–550 MB/s, and an NVMe external SSD over USB 3.2 Gen 2 at 900–1,000 MB/s.

Divide 1TB by those rates and the backup window changes shape:

Drive typeSustained rateTime for 1TB, by simple division
External HDD80–160 MB/sAbout 105–210 minutes
SATA external SSD400–550 MB/sAbout 30–42 minutes
NVMe external SSD, USB 3.2 Gen 2900–1,000 MB/sAbout 17–19 minutes

That arithmetic assumes a steady stream of large files. A real library is mixed, and for many small files the SSD advantage can be 10 to 20 times, because of random-access speed. So the table understates the gap for a documents-and-photos backup and states it fairly for a video archive.

Why it matters is behavioural, not technical. A backup that takes three hours gets postponed. A backup that finishes in twenty minutes gets run.

Cost per terabyte, and the cost of the pair

The same 2026 analysis puts external HDDs at US$20–30 per terabyte for 2–5TB models, and external SSDs at US$60–90 per terabyte. Other published tables disagree — one lists HDD at about US$10–15 per terabyte and SSD at about US$40–80 — which is exactly why this page anchors on two named drives with a date instead of averaging conflicting tables.

From the named examples: the 2TB SSD at about US$140 works out to about US$70 per terabyte, inside its range. The two-drive pattern covered at the end — one of each, 2TB apiece — costs about US$205 in total using those cited figures (US$65 + US$140). That combined figure, not either drive alone, is the honest comparison for a reader whose data matters most: roughly three times the HDD-only cost, for a fast working copy plus a long-term copy.

How each drive dies, and the travel question

The failure patterns differ in shape. HDD failure is listed as gradual, SSD failure as sudden. A gradual failure gives warning signs and time to copy; a sudden one gives neither, which is why an SSD backup should never be the only copy of anything.

On shock, a caution about evidence: the sources behind this comparison publish no drop rating for either named drive. Do not buy on a shock number that is not on a spec sheet you can check. What can be planned around, from the sourced material, is the failure pattern above and where the drive will live — a drive that travels gets handled, packed, and unpacked far more often than a drive that stays on a desk, so it needs a second copy sooner, whichever type it is.

Wear is the other fear, and it is mostly misplaced for this job. A typical consumer SSD rated at 300 TBW can last well over a decade under normal use, and the limiting factor is usually electronic failure, not NAND wear. Backup duty — large writes, then long rests — is not the workload that spends a 300 TBW budget quickly.

The drawer problem: unpowered retention

Here the HDD wins a round that speed cannot answer. One published comparison table lists data retention at 5–10+ years for HDD against 1–3 years for SSD, and recommends HDD for long-term backup on that basis.

Separate two situations that most comparisons merge. An SSD in regular powered use is covered by the endurance picture above: rated wear lasts well over a decade in normal use. An SSD written once and left unpowered in a drawer is a different case, and it is precisely the backup case — the drive whose whole job is to sit untouched until a bad day. For that job, the retention figures favour the HDD, and a drawer SSD should be powered and checked on a schedule rather than trusted silently for years.

Neither figure is a guarantee in either direction. Both say the same practical thing: a long-term copy belongs on the medium whose storage behaviour matches long, unpowered rests, and every backup drive needs periodic checks regardless of type.

The two-drive pattern, and who needs it

The source recommendation matches the evidence assembled here: SSD for active data plus HDD for long-term backup is the best strategy of the options compared. Assigned by job, the pair looks like this:

  • External SSD: the working backup — frequent, fast runs that actually happen, and the copy that travels.
  • External HDD: the long-term copy — cheaper per terabyte, better suited to unpowered storage, and the second copy if the SSD fails suddenly.

Who can skip the pair? A reader with one small library, backed up often to a single drive that stays powered and checked, can start with one drive and add the second later. Who should not skip it: anyone whose only copy would be a drawer SSD left for years, or a single drive of either type holding data they cannot replace.

Start from the job, then. Frequent backups favour the SSD’s minutes. Archive storage favours the HDD’s retention and price. Data that matters most earns both, at about US$205 on the dated figures above.
