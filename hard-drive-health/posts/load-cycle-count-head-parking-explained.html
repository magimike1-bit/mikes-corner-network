Load Cycle Count Explained: When Head Parking Is Normal and When It Is Eating Your Hard Drive

Drive monitoring · Published 2026-11-19 · Last updated 2026-11-19

Is 200,000 load cycles a lot? On one drive that figure is two thirds of its published life spent. On another it is one third. The number on your SMART report cannot answer the question by itself, because the limit it should be compared against is printed in your drive model’s own data sheet, not in a universal table, and because a total accumulated gently over six years means something entirely different from the same total accumulated in six months. This guide works through both corrections: the rating first, then the rate.

What one load cycle actually is

A spinning hard drive reads and writes with heads that float just above the platters. When the drive idles or powers down, it parks those heads off the data surface. WD’s desktop specifications describe the mechanism as ramp load technology: the heads rest on a ramp rather than on the platter, and one load cycle is one trip from that ramp onto the platters and back again. Every spin up, every wake from idle, and every aggressive power saving park in between adds to the count.

The counter lives in SMART attribute 193, named Load_Cycle_Count in smartmontools output. The smartmontools documentation shows the table format: each attribute carries a normalized VALUE that counts down, a THRESH it must not fall to, a TYPE, and a RAW_VALUE, and for attribute 193 the raw value is the actual number of cycles. Two cautions from the same documentation matter here. Attribute 193 is typed Old_age, meaning it tracks a design lifetime being used up, not a prediction of imminent failure. And attribute meanings and raw formats are vendor specific and not set by any standard, which is why the next section starts with the manufacturer’s document for your exact model rather than with a number remembered from a forum.

The rating is printed per model, not per technology

Read the table as a warning against borrowed limits. Two surveillance drives of the same capacity, sold for the same job, carry ratings that differ by a factor of two: 300,000 for the WD Purple model listed, 600,000 for the Seagate SkyHawk model listed. The Purple figure works out to roughly 274 cycles a day spread across its three year warranty period, and the SkyHawk figure to roughly 548 a day across the same span. A desktop WD Blue sits at 300,000, while WD’s NAS line doubles it. The ratings also carry a footnote worth respecting: WD’s data sheets define the figure as a controlled unload at ambient conditions, a laboratory version of parking, not a promise about any individual drive’s behaviour in a hot enclosure.

So the first check on any drive is clerical, not technical. Find the exact model number on the SMART information screen, look up that model’s data sheet, and write its rating next to the raw count. A count of 280,000 is an end of life conversation on a 300,000 rated drive and unremarkable on a 600,000 rated one.

The rate is the verdict: two readings and one division

A healthy drive parks when it is genuinely idle and stays parked. A drive with aggressive power management parks, gets woken seconds later by routine system activity, and parks again, hundreds of times a day, while doing almost no work. The total cannot separate those two histories. The rate can.

The reference case is old and still instructive. The Ubuntu bug record on excessive load/unload cycling documented drives parking more than once every three minutes while idle, with the count climbing visibly over a few minutes of watching. The fix criterion proposed in that record was fewer than about 15 load cycles per hour, except during heavy use on battery, a rate that stretches even a 300,000 cycle rating across years. The same record notes the mechanism plainly: aggressive defaults park the heads after a short idle period while the operating system keeps touching the disk, so the heads park and reload almost immediately, over and over.

Work your own rate out with the readings you already have. Attribute 193’s raw value divided by attribute 9’s power on hours gives the lifetime average cycles per hour. Averages hide recent changes, so take a second reading a week later as well: the increase in the count, divided by the days between readings, is your current cycles per day. One widely used rule of thumb from Linux drive guides flags a rise of more than 25 cycles per day as too many for a drive that should mostly sit idle. Treat that figure as a prompt to investigate rather than a verdict, and judge it against your pattern of use: a laptop drive that sleeps and wakes with the lid will legitimately cycle far more often than a NAS drive that spins around the clock.

Read your count and rate, step by step

  1. Get the full attribute table. On Linux or macOS with smartmontools installed, run sudo smartctl -a /dev/sda, replacing the device with your drive. On Windows, the same table is shown by a SMART reader; look for attribute 193 by name.
  2. Record three numbers. Attribute 193’s RAW_VALUE (the count), attribute 9’s raw value (power on hours), and today’s date. Note the normalized VALUE of 193 as well, but do the arithmetic on the raw count.
  3. Find your model’s rating. Take the model number from the top of the same smartctl output and look up its data sheet figure, using the table above as a starting point if your model is listed.
  4. Do the two divisions. Count divided by rating gives the share of the parking budget spent. Count divided by power on hours gives the lifetime rate per hour.
  5. Take the second reading. Repeat step 1 after seven days of normal use and divide the increase by seven. That is your current cycles per day, the number that decides whether anything needs changing.

What success looks like: you end with three figures on one line: the count as a share of your model’s own rating, the lifetime rate, and the current daily rate. A drive at 40 percent of its rating with a current rate under a few cycles per hour needs nothing but a note to recheck in six months. A drive adding hundreds of cycles a day has a power management problem that the next section addresses, whatever its total says.

Slow the parking down, carefully

On Linux, the documented lever is the drive’s Advanced Power Management level, set with hdparm. The APM scale runs from 0 to 255: lower values park aggressively, 254 is the least aggressive setting that still permits the drive to manage power, and 255 usually turns APM off entirely where the drive supports it. The Slackware SMART guide’s longstanding prescription is hdparm -B 254 /dev/sda, placed in a boot script. That placement is not a detail: a hdparm setting applied by hand lasts only until the next reboot or power cycle on most systems, so a fix that is not written into a startup script or a power management configuration is a fix that quietly expires.

Three limits belong with the advice. First, verify the result rather than trusting the command: recheck the count after a week and confirm the daily rate actually fell. Second, some WD drives run a separate vendor idle timer, marketed as IntelliPark, that parks the heads on its own schedule and ignores the APM level; Linux drive guides note that those models need the vendor timer adjusted with its own tool before the count will settle. Third, weigh the trade honestly. Parking exists to save power and to protect the heads from shock while a laptop moves. A desktop or NAS drive that never travels gains little from aggressive parking, while a laptop drive set to never park trades a small amount of battery life and shock protection for its cycle budget. The setting should follow where the drive lives.

On Windows and macOS there is no equivalent single switch documented by the operating system vendors for ATA APM levels. The practical levers there are the system sleep and disk idle timers: lengthening the idle time before the disk sleeps reduces how often a wake, park, wake cycle can repeat, at the same battery cost described above.

When the count, not the rate, is the problem

Two situations call for replacement planning rather than settings. The first is arithmetic: the count is approaching the model’s published rating, say past 80 percent of it, even at a gentle rate. The drive has simply used the budget it was built with, and the correct response is an orderly migration to a new drive while the old one still works, with backups verified before the swap. The second is company the count keeps: attribute 193 rising alongside new reallocated or pending sectors, read errors, or a normalized value that has fallen to its threshold is a different picture from parking wear alone, and the error evidence outranks the cycle count in deciding urgency.

The reverse also holds. A mid life count, a low current rate, and a clean error record mean the parking system is doing its job, and the right action is to write the three figures down and check them again in six months. Load Cycle Count frightens people because it is a big number next to the word count. Compared against the right rating and read as a rate, it becomes what it should have been all along: a maintenance gauge, not an alarm.

— Dex Carter, Tech Writer
