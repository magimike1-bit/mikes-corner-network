Additional living expenses in Canada: what counts, what does not, and how the limit runs out

Coverage · 7 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Oct 31, 2026 · Last updated Oct 31, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how additional living expenses coverage works in Canadian home, condo and tenant policies. It is not insurance or financial advice and it does not promise that any expense will be paid. Limits and wording vary by insurer and province, and your own policy document is the final word for your situation.

The trap first: ALE does not pay your whole cost of living

Most people meet this coverage on the worst week of their year, and they meet it with a wrong picture in mind: the home is unlivable, so the insurer pays for living somewhere else. Half of that picture is right. The Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC) describes this part of a policy — typically called Coverage D, Additional Living Expenses, or ALE — as covering the cost of alternate accommodations and living expenses for people whose home has become unliveable after an insured loss. Then comes the condition that decides almost every dispute about it.

ALE covers the increase over your normal expenses, not the expenses themselves. IBC's own example is laundry: if your temporary accommodation has no laundry facilities and you have to use a laundromat, those laundromat costs may qualify, because you would not have paid them at home. Your normal grocery bill, by the same logic, does not become claimable just because you are eating it somewhere else. You were going to buy groceries anyway.

That one rule — increase only — is the whole guide. Everything below is applying it: what triggers the coverage, which lines of a displaced household's spending are increases, and the three separate ways the money can run out while you are still counting on it.

The three triggers, and how each one ends

ALE is not one situation. IBC lists three categories of ALE claim, and they start and stop on different conditions:

TriggerWhat has happenedHow this trigger ends
Your own home is damagedAn insured peril — IBC gives fire and related damage as examples — makes your home unlivableTypically after a reasonable time to repair or rebuild, or when the policy limit is reached
A civil authority orders a mass evacuationYou are prohibited from returning home by an official order, whether or not your own home is damagedCoverage starts on the evacuation date and typically expires after a specified number of days; when the order is lifted, this coverage ends
Damage to neighbouring premisesYour home may be undamaged, but damage nearby means you cannot return to itInsurers review duration case by case; confirm your limit with your insurance representative

Two details in that table catch people out. First, leaving early on a warning is not the same as leaving under an order: IBC notes that once an evacuation order is lifted and the authority has given the all clear, a person whose home is not damaged and who chooses not to return is not eligible for further ALE. Second, in a flood situation IBC ties this coverage to having bought the optional water coverages in the first place — the underlying loss still has to be an insured one. A home made unlivable by something the policy does not cover does not become covered because the family had to move out.

What counts as an increase, line by line

PolicyMe's consumer explainer lists the expense categories ALE can reimburse, and they all share the same shape: a reasonable, necessary cost that sits above routine spending. Temporary housing — a hotel bill or a short- or long-term rental. Food costs that rise because the temporary place has no kitchen, such as restaurant meals or delivery. Transportation that costs more because the temporary home is farther from work. Pet boarding when pets cannot stay in the temporary accommodation. Laundry. In longer repairs, even the cost of pausing and reconnecting utilities can appear on the list.

The excluded side of the ledger is just as consistent. Routine expenses you already had — groceries at their normal level, rent, mortgage payments — are not reimbursed, because they are not increases. Luxuries beyond your usual standard of living are not covered. Repair costs are not ALE at all; they belong to the dwelling part of the policy. And costs beyond the policy limit are the household's, which is why the limit section below matters before the spending starts, not after.

One practical point from the same explainer: payment can run two ways. An insurer may pay a provider such as a hotel directly, or reimburse the policyholder for costs already paid. IBC adds that reimbursement timing varies by insurer, and that in some instances an insurer may provide an advance on ALE — worth asking about in the first call, when cash flow is tightest.

Worked ledger: one month displaced, assumptions labelled

The figures below are invented for the arithmetic and labelled as assumptions. They are not prices, quotes, or predictions. The point is the shape of the calculation: for each line, subtract what the household would have spent anyway. Only the difference is an ALE amount.

Say a fire makes a house unlivable for 30 days. The household's mortgage and normal bills continue. The displaced month looks like this:

LineDisplaced monthNormal monthIncrease (the ALE line)
Hotel, 30 nights at an assumed $180 a night$5,400$0$5,400
Food: restaurant and delivery, no kitchen$1,500$700 in groceries$800
Laundromat$120$0$120
Extra fuel for a longer commute$240$150$90
Mortgage payment$1,900$1,900$0
Total$6,410

Read the food line twice, because it is where claims shrink. The claimable amount is not the $1,500 spent eating out; it is the $800 above the grocery spending it replaced. Read the mortgage line the same way: the payment continues, unchanged, and precisely because it is unchanged it contributes nothing. A household that budgets its displacement around the full $9,160 of displaced-month spending will feel short-changed by a $6,410 claim. A household that budgets around the increase will not.

How the limit runs out: money, time, or repairs — whichever is first

ALE ends on the first of three finish lines. The home is repaired and the household can move back. The dollar limit is exhausted. Or, on policies that carry one, a time limit runs out.

The dollar limit is usually expressed as a percentage of the dwelling coverage. PolicyMe puts the typical range at 20 to 30 percent of the dwelling limit, while noting it may instead appear as a set dollar amount on the policy. On a $400,000 dwelling limit, that typical range is $80,000 to $120,000 of ALE room — a large number against a two-week hotel stay, and a much smaller one against a rebuild that stretches past a year of rent.

The time limit is the quieter finish line. Some policies stipulate a maximum ALE period, with 12 or 24 months given as examples. A household can therefore run out of coverage with money technically left in the limit, because the calendar closed first. Neither figure is discoverable from a brochure: both sit in your own policy, and the time to read them is at renewal, not in a hotel lobby.

Keeping the claim payable: a receipt procedure

IBC's instruction is short — keep all receipts and invoices for additional living expenses following your loss. Turned into a procedure a tired person can actually follow, it looks like this:

  1. Ask the ALE questions on the first call. What is the limit, is there a time cap, and will the insurer pay any provider directly or advance funds? Write the answers down with the date.
  2. Start one folder, paper or digital, on day one. Every hotel bill, meal receipt, laundry receipt, boarding invoice and fuel receipt goes into it, in date order.
  3. Record the normal amount beside each line. A note of what groceries, fuel or laundry normally cost each month is what turns a pile of receipts into the increase-only calculation above.
  4. Track the running total against the limit weekly. Displacement spending feels small nightly and large monthly; the ledger from the worked example, kept live, shows the limit approaching before it arrives.
  5. Confirm before extending. Before renewing a rental month or upgrading accommodation, check the remaining limit and time with the adjuster, so a cost beyond the limit is a chosen cost, not a surprise.

Success looks like this: at any point in the displacement, the household can produce, for every dollar claimed, a receipt, the normal-cost figure it is measured against, and the remaining limit. That is the whole claim, organised.

Decision framework: checking your own Coverage D before you need it

Five checks, done at renewal, turn ALE from a surprise into a known number:

  1. Find the limit and its form. Percentage of dwelling coverage, or a set dollar amount? Do the multiplication and write down the actual dollars.
  2. Find the time cap, if any. A limit with a 12-month clock behaves very differently from the same limit without one.
  3. Price your own likely increase, roughly. Local hotel or rental costs for a month, plus the food increase if the temporary place had no kitchen. Compare that monthly burn rate with the limit.
  4. Count your offsets honestly. Family nearby who could host, and pets who would need boarding, move the real cost a long way in either direction.
  5. Ask about changing the limit before renewal, not after a loss. Whether a higher or lower limit is available, and what it costs, is a renewal conversation; after an insured loss, the limit already in force is the one that applies.

The short version

  • ALE — Coverage D — pays the increase over your normal living costs when an insured loss makes your home unliveable. It does not pay the normal costs themselves.
  • Three triggers: damage to your own home, a civil-authority mass evacuation, and prohibited access because of damage to neighbouring premises. Each ends on its own conditions.
  • On the worked ledger, a $9,160 displaced month produced $6,410 of increase — the food line claims the difference over normal groceries, and the unchanged mortgage claims nothing.
  • Coverage ends at the first finish line: repairs complete, the dollar limit (typically 20–30% of the dwelling limit) exhausted, or a policy time cap such as 12 or 24 months.
  • Keep every receipt and invoice, record the normal cost beside each line, and track the running total against the limit from week one.
