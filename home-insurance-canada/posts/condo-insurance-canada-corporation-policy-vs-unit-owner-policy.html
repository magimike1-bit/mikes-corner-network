Condo insurance in Canada: the corporation policy vs your unit owner policy

Renters & condos · 8 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Nov 3, 2026 · Last updated Nov 3, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how a condo corporation policy and a unit owner policy divide responsibility in Canada. It is not insurance, legal or financial advice. Condo law differs by province, every building's documents differ, and your own policy plus your corporation's documents are the final word for your unit.

The building is insured. That is not the same as your condo being insured.

Most condo buyers hear one reassuring sentence during the purchase: the corporation carries insurance on the building. The sentence is true and still leaves the buyer underinsured, because the corporation policy was never designed to cover the whole of any one unit. It covers the building, the common elements and a defined slice of each unit. Everything outside that slice belongs to a second policy, the unit owner policy, which nobody sells you automatically.

The two policies sit side by side like this.

Who buys it?The condo corporation, paid from common expensesYou, the unit owner
What property does it cover?Common elements, the building, and each unit only up to its standard unit definitionYour contents, your improvements above the standard unit, and your share of certain corporation costs
Your belongings?Never. The Condominium Authority of Ontario is blunt that corporation insurance covers the standard unit, not personal belongingsYes, this is the contents cover
Your liability?The corporation carries its own liability cover for common areas and its directorsYour personal liability lives here
If you cannot live in the unit after a covered loss?Not the corporation policy's jobLoss of use cover, one of the items CMHC recommends a unit owner policy include

Neither policy is the deluxe version of the other. They cover different property, and the seam between them is where condo claims go wrong.

The standard unit line, and what sits above it

The seam has a name: the standard unit. In Ontario, the Condominium Authority explains that the condo's governing documents define the standard unit to clarify which portion of the unit the corporation must repair and insure. Generally, standard units include walls, doors and plumbing. Anything outside the definition is an improvement, and improvements are the owner's to insure and repair. The Authority adds that the non-standard elements tend to include appliances and flooring.

Alberta uses a sibling concept, the standard insurable unit description. CondoLaw Alberta lists what such a description typically contains: floor, wall and ceiling coverings, electrical and plumbing fixtures, cabinets and counter tops, and non-chattel appliances. An Alberta owner can request the description from the corporation, and the corporation has 10 days to provide it.

Read that boundary as money. After a fire or a major leak, the corporation policy rebuilds your unit back to the standard version. The upgraded hardwood, the renovated kitchen, the better bathroom fixtures: the difference between standard and actual is improvement value, and it only gets paid if your own policy carries enough improvements cover. A unit renovated twice since construction can hold tens of thousands of dollars of improvement value that the owner has never totalled. Total it, in writing, before renewal. That total, not a guess, is the improvements limit to ask for.

One more boundary hides in the master policy itself. Western Financial Group notes that a corporation policy is one of two types: named perils, which covers only the losses listed, or all risks, which covers anything not specifically excluded. Two identical units in different buildings can sit under different peril lists, which is one more reason the corporation documents, not a neighbour's experience, set your homework.

Three different bills that all get called an assessment

Condo owners hear one word, assessment, for three different events. They are not interchangeable, and only two of them are insurable in the usual way.

The billWhat triggers itWhich cover responds
Deductible chargeback or deductible assessmentA loss starts in, or is caused from, your unit and the corporation claims on its policy. The corporation passes its deductible to you under the provincial rule or a by-lawDeductible assessment cover inside your unit owner policy. CMHC's recommended list names deductible chargebacks first
Loss assessmentDamage to the building exceeds the corporation policy limits, or a shared liability cost lands on all owners. Your share arrives as an assessment on every unitLoss assessment cover. Western notes these assessments can be large and due quickly
Special assessment for repairsThe building needs work the reserve fund cannot cover, such as a major repair. No insured loss needs to have happenedUsually your own savings. This is a maintenance cost shared by owners, not an insurance event, so do not count on loss assessment cover without checking the exact wording

Western makes one further point worth separating out: a corporation may split its deductible between all owners after a common area loss, and in some cases an owner who is liable for the damage pays the entire deductible. Your exposure is therefore not limited to losses that begin inside your own four walls.

CMHC's recommended checklist for a unit owner policy, quoted in a Rates.ca guide, pulls the whole picture together: deductible chargebacks, personal property and contents, unit improvements, loss of use, loss assessment, and unit contingency, which addresses structural inadequacies in your unit that the corporation policy does not cover. The same guide notes the Insurance Bureau of Canada recommendation that owners who sit on the condo board carry directors and officers liability coverage.

The Alberta deductible numbers, worked

Alberta states its chargeback rule with unusual clarity, and the worked examples on the CondoLaw Alberta site show why the deductible amount matters more than the deductible concept.

If damage for a claim originates in or from an owner's unit, the corporation can claim its insurance deductible back from that owner, up to a maximum of $50,000. The owner is described as absolutely liable on demand, meaning liability applies whether or not negligence is proven. Run the two published examples:

  • Corporation deductible of $15,000: the owner of the unit where the damage originated owes the full $15,000.
  • Corporation deductible of $75,000: the same owner owes $50,000, the statutory cap, not the full deductible.

The exceptions matter as much as the rule. The same source lists cases where the owner is not liable: damage from a construction defect in the unit, damage from an act or omission of the corporation or its board, officers, employees or agents, and damage from normal structural deterioration of common property the owner was not responsible for maintaining.

Ontario reaches a related result by a different route. The Condominium Authority of Ontario explains that corporations may have by-laws extending the circumstances in which owners pay the corporation deductible, giving the example of an owner whose negligence causes a major flood affecting many units. The lesson travels even where the statute does not: in every province, some document in your building can move the corporation deductible onto your kitchen table. Your deductible assessment limit needs to be set against the deductible your corporation actually carries today, and that number changes. CondoLaw Alberta notes that when coverage changes, an Alberta corporation must give owners written notice and a copy of the updated insurance certificate within 30 days of receiving it.

The three-document check

This check takes one email to the property manager and one sitting with your own policy. It replaces guessing with the building's actual numbers.

  1. Get the corporation insurance certificate. CondoLaw Alberta advises owners to ask the board, the manager or the corporation's insurance broker for the Certificate of Insurance, which outlines current deductible amounts. Note each deductible separately if the certificate lists more than one, since a water damage deductible and a general deductible may differ.
  2. Read the standard unit definition. In Ontario it sits in the governing documents; in Alberta, request the standard insurable unit description, which the corporation must provide within 10 days. Mark everything in your unit that falls outside it.
  3. Total your improvements. Price the flooring, cabinets, counters, fixtures and appliances above the standard version at replacement cost. Compare the total with the improvements limit on your own policy and raise the limit if the total is higher.
  4. Match the deductible assessment limit to the certificate. Your limit should at least equal the corporation deductible that could be charged back to you. In Alberta, the exposure is capped at $50,000; in a by-law province, work from the certificate figure and any deductible by-law the manager can share.
  5. Check the remaining CMHC items. Contents, loss of use, loss assessment and unit contingency, each with a limit you can explain. Then set a diary note: when the corporation sends an insurance change notice, repeat steps 1 and 4.

Success looks like this: one page holding the corporation deductible, your deductible assessment limit beside it, your improvement total beside your improvements limit, and no pair where the second number is smaller than the first.

When the owner policy is not worth padding

Penny's closing test is a saving test, not a sales test. If your improvements total is small because the unit is close to its standard build, a large improvements limit buys back a gap that barely exists. If your building carries a modest deductible and your deductible assessment limit already matches it, raising that limit further protects against a chargeback your province may cap anyway. Spend the premium where a document shows a real gap: an unmatched deductible, an undertotalled renovation, or a missing loss assessment limit in a building whose corporation policy limits look thin. Everything else is padding.

One overlap is not waste, though. Where the corporation and the owner both insure the same loss, CondoLaw Alberta explains that the corporation insurance is treated as first loss and the owner insurance as excess. The two policies coordinating after a loss is normal. The expensive outcome is the opposite one, each policy pointing at a gap the other was assumed to fill.

General information only, not insurance, legal or financial advice. Standard unit definitions, deductible chargeback rules and assessment powers vary by province and by building; the Alberta $50,000 cap and the Ontario by-law route described here are provincial examples, not national rules. Talk to a licensed insurance professional about your unit, and treat your corporation's documents and your own policy as the final word.
