Earthquake insurance in Canada: who actually needs it

Coverage · 6 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Oct 28, 2026 · Last updated Oct 28, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how earthquake cover works as an add-on to a Canadian home policy. It is not insurance advice, it does not recommend any insurer or product, and it is not written by a broker or agent. Insurers set their own rules, and those rules differ — confirm anything that matters with your own insurer or broker, and treat your policy documents as the final word.

$40,000 first: the deductible is the product you are buying

$40,000 is not a premium. It is the amount one worked example puts in a homeowner's own pocket before earthquake cover pays anything: a 5% deductible on an $800,000 dwelling coverage limit, from a British Columbia broker guide. That single arithmetic fact reframes the whole question. Earthquake insurance in Canada is not part of a standard home policy — it is an added endorsement — and its deductibles are a percentage, not the flat few hundred dollars most people picture from a regular claim. Before asking what the endorsement costs per year, a household needs to know what it would still have to pay itself on the day it is used.

Read the two deductible rows together. A percentage sounds small until the base is named. On any quote, the first question is therefore not “what percent?” but “percent of what — and what does that equal in dollars on my limit?” A quote that cannot be turned into a dollar figure has not yet answered the question that matters.

Where Canada actually shakes: three risk bands

The Insurance Bureau of Canada risk picture, as reported by ThinkInsure, sorts the country into three bands. British Columbia is the high-risk band, with a 30% chance of a damaging earthquake in any 50-year period. The St. Lawrence and Ottawa Valley corridor — the Ottawa to Montreal to Quebec City line — is the moderate band, at 5–15% over 50 years. The rest of Canada sits in the low band, at under 1% over the same period.

Those bands explain the price map. The Square One annual estimates for a $300,000 house and contents, via Ratehub (checked Oct 25, 2026), were: Toronto $15, Winnipeg $25, Montreal $178, Halifax $213, Edmonton $284, Vancouver $332, and Richmond $515. The estimates are one insurer's figures for one house value, not a market average — treat them as a dated price check that shows the shape of the pricing, not as a quote for any other home. Even with that limit, the shape is instructive: the cheapest estimate in the list is in the low band, and the two most expensive are both in BC's high band, a short drive apart.

One exclusion belongs in this section because it surprises people after coastal storms: earthquake insurance does not cover tidal waves or tsunamis, per Ratehub's summary. A coastal household weighing the endorsement for water-driven damage is pricing the wrong peril if that is the loss it has in mind.

The strata trap: someone else's deductible, assessed to you

Condo and strata owners face a second exposure that the usual “do you need it” framing misses entirely. The BC guide reports that strata deductibles can be $250,000 to $500,000 or more, and that a strata deductible can be passed back to unit owners. In plain terms: the building's own policy can carry a deductible so large that, after a loss, the amount is assessed across the owners — an owner can owe a share of a deductible they never chose, on a policy they do not hold.

That changes what a strata owner needs to check. The question is not only “does my unit policy have earthquake cover?” It is also whether the unit policy responds to a deductible assessment from the strata's policy, and what the strata's current deductible actually is. A household that has never seen that number is guessing about its largest earthquake exposure, whatever its own endorsement says.

Tenants sit at the opposite end of the scale. The same BC guide prices tenant earthquake coverage at $4 to $13 per month. A tenant has no dwelling to rebuild — the exposure is contents and displacement — and the monthly figure in the source reflects that narrower job. Renters in the high band should still read their own policy wording, but the decision is a small monthly one, not a five-figure deductible one.

Worked math: the ten-year cost against the one-day cost

A total-cost view keeps both sides of the ledger in the same frame. Take the Vancouver estimate as a labelled worked example — assumptions stated, arithmetic redoable:

  • Premium side: $332 a year (Square One estimate via Ratehub for a $300,000 house and contents, checked Oct 25, 2026) held flat for ten years comes to $3,320. Premiums change at renewal, so this is an illustration at today's estimate, not a forecast.
  • Claim side: using the BC guide's worked example, a 5% deductible on an $800,000 dwelling limit is $40,000 out of pocket before the endorsement responds.
  • The comparison: a decade of premiums at that estimate is roughly one-twelfth of one claim's deductible in that example. The endorsement is therefore not “cheap cover” or “expensive cover” in the abstract — it is a decision about whether the household can absorb the uninsured loss above a deductible it can name in dollars.

Run the same arithmetic in the low band and the answer often flips. At the Toronto estimate of $15 a year for the same $300,000 house and contents, ten years is $150 — a trivial premium — but the underlying risk band is under 1% in 50 years, and the deductible question still applies if a claim ever arose. A low premium does not remove the need to know the deductible; it just makes the premium side of the decision easy.

A decision framework, by who you are

  1. BC Lower Mainland or Vancouver Island homeowner: you are in the 30%-in-50-years band. Get the deductible in dollars on your own dwelling limit before deciding. If that dollar figure is one you could not pay after a damaging quake, the endorsement is addressing a real exposure.
  2. Ottawa–Montreal–Quebec City corridor homeowner: the moderate band, at 5–15% in 50 years. The Montreal estimate ($178 a year for the $300,000 example) sits between the extremes — the deductible-in-dollars check decides here too.
  3. BC strata or condo owner: check the strata deductible assessment exposure first. The $250,000 to $500,000-plus strata deductible range is the exposure most likely to be missing from your own planning.
  4. Tenant in a higher-risk area: weigh the $4 to $13 per month figure in the source against what your contents would cost to replace, and confirm what your own tenant policy says.
  5. Homeowner in a low-risk region — the Prairies, most of Ontario outside the corridor, and Atlantic Canada on the IBC bands as reported: the honest skip case. With a risk band under 1% in 50 years, many households will reasonably decline the endorsement and put the money against risks their standard policy treats differently. Skipping it knowingly, with the band named, is a different decision from never having checked.

What success looks like: before renewing, you can state three numbers without guessing — your risk band, a premium estimate dated from a real quote, and your deductible converted to dollars on your own limit. If any one of the three is missing, the decision is not yet made, whatever the premium quote says.

The short version

  • Earthquake cover is an endorsement, not part of a standard home policy, and its deductible is a percentage — sources give typically 2–20%, with a BC guide stating 2–10% of the dwelling limit (5% on $800,000 = $40,000).
  • IBC risk bands as reported: BC high (30% chance of a damaging quake in 50 years), St. Lawrence/Ottawa Valley moderate (5–15%), rest of Canada low (under 1%).
  • Dated estimates for a $300,000 house and contents (Square One via Ratehub, checked Oct 25, 2026): Toronto $15, Vancouver $332, Richmond $515 a year.
  • BC strata deductibles can be $250,000 to $500,000 or more and can be passed back to unit owners; tenant earthquake cover is priced at $4–$13 a month in the source checked.
  • Earthquake insurance does not cover tidal waves or tsunamis.
