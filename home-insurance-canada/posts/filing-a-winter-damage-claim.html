Filing a winter damage claim in Canada, step by step

Claims · 7 min read

A burst pipe at 2 a.m. is not the moment to learn how claims work. This guide covers the procedure every claims guide lists — and the two decisions they all skip: whether to file at all, and what you're allowed to fix before the adjuster arrives.

Before the steps: should you even file?

Not every loss deserves a claim. Run the decision framework first:

  1. Is the repair cost clearly above your deductible? If the fix costs less than your deductible, there's nothing to claim — the policy pays the excess over the deductible, which here is zero or negative. Pay it yourself and move on.
  2. Is it barely above? This is the judgment call. Filing a claim for a loss just over your deductible can cost more over time — through a higher premium or the loss of a claims-free discount — than simply paying it. One broker puts it plainly: weigh the payout against the possible premium increase before you file.
  3. Is it obviously a covered peril? If you're unsure whether the policy covers this type of damage at all, review your documents before calling — there's no point starting a claim for an excluded peril.

File when the damage is clearly covered and clearly exceeds the deductible by enough to matter. For everything else, the math usually says pay out of pocket.

Step 1: Make it safe and stop the damage

Once everyone is safe, take reasonable steps to prevent the damage from getting worse — shut off the water for an active leak, turn off power to a flooded area, move undamaged belongings out of the way. If you think emergency repairs may be needed, contact your insurer immediately so they can advise you. Insurers expect mitigation; they do not expect heroics.

Step 2: Document everything before anything moves

This is the step that decides claims. Before cleanup, before repairs, before the contractor:

  • Photograph and video the damage from multiple angles, including the source (the burst pipe, the failed fitting).
  • Write down what happened, when you discovered it, and what you've done so far — while it's fresh.
  • List damaged items with approximate purchase dates and values.
  • Keep every receipt from this point forward: emergency plumber, hotel, supplies. Reimbursement starts with paper.

A well-documented claim moves faster. An undocumented one invites questions.

Step 3: Notify your insurer the right way

Contact your insurer as soon as possible — within 24 hours is the standard advice. Most insurers let you file online, through an app, by phone, or through your broker; the digital route is often fastest because it walks you through the required information and accepts document uploads immediately. When you report, have the essentials ready: what happened, when it happened, what's damaged, whether the situation is urgent, and your policy number. Be specific — precision now reduces back-and-forth later.

Step 4: The proof of loss and the paper trail

Your insurer will likely ask you to complete a proof-of-loss form: a formal list of everything damaged or stolen with a value for each item. You typically have around 30 days to complete it. Be accurate and honest — discrepancies between the form and the evidence can reduce or sink the payout. From here on, keep a log of every interaction: names, dates, what was said. Claims that drag on (property claims can take 60–90 days) are won on records.

Step 5: Work with the adjuster — and what to do when you disagree

A claims adjuster will be assigned to assess the damage, usually within a few days. They'll inspect, ask for your documentation, and arrange for repair estimates. Two things most guides bury: first, you can get your own independent repair estimates — they're useful leverage. Second, the settlement offer is negotiable. If the number doesn't reflect the damage, push back with your documentation and estimates until you reach an agreement you're willing to sign. Only sign the release when you're satisfied; the payout follows the signature.

The repair boundary: what you can fix now vs. what waits

Temporary protection (tarps, drying)YesPrevents the damage from growing
Permanent repairs and replacementsWait for adjuster approvalStarting permanent work early can jeopardize the claim

This is the boundary the other guides never draw. Emergency mitigation: do it, document it, get reimbursed. Permanent repair: wait. Confusing the two is how good claims go bad.

The one habit that matters most: document first, call second, repair third — in that order, every time. Homeowners who prepare before disaster strikes, document thoroughly when it does, and follow the process carefully are the ones whose claims don't end in denial.

The short version

  • Run the file-or-pay math before you file: barely over the deductible usually means pay it yourself.
  • Document everything before anything moves; keep every receipt from minute one.
  • Notify within 24 hours; complete the proof of loss honestly and on time.
  • Emergency mitigation now, permanent repairs after the adjuster approves.
  • Settlement offers are negotiable — bring your own estimates.
