Home insurance after a major claim in Canada: what changes at renewal and what to check

Claims · 8 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Nov 13, 2026 · Last updated Nov 13, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, what a home insurance claim can change at renewal in Canada. It is not insurance or financial advice. Renewal decisions turn on your insurer, your province, and your policy wording, and a licensed insurance professional who can see your file is the right source for advice on your situation.

The number you were quoted does not exist

Start with the trap, because it sits inside almost every search result on this topic. There is no standard percentage that a Canadian home premium rises after a claim. MyChoice, reviewing the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada and Insurance Bureau of Canada guides for its post-claim explainer, states it plainly: there is no nationwide percentage or standard timeframe for rate increases following a claim in Canada, and a premium may simply change when the policy comes up for renewal.

The conflicting numbers online are real numbers. They are just measuring different things. BrokerLink publishes a table putting the average increase at 10 to 25 percent per claim, varying by claim type and insurer. Its claim-type rows run from water damage at 15 to 30 percent and liability or bodily injury at 20 to 35 percent, down to fire and theft at 10 to 20 percent each. MyChoice ran a different measurement across its own quote platform in 2026 and found premiums about 32.5 percent higher for similar homes with one claim than for homes with no reported claims. MyChoice itself warns that this is not a study tracking the same homes before and after a claim, so it cannot tell one household what its renewal will do.

Read those figures as a range of reported outcomes, never as your forecast. Only your insurer can price your renewal, and the useful work at renewal time is not guessing the percentage. It is finding out which of five separate things your claim actually changed.

Five things a claim can change at renewal, only one of them price

A claim is one event. The renewal letter is where its consequences separate. Based on the FCAC and IBC material MyChoice reviewed, a claim can produce any combination of these outcomes:

Renewal outcomeWhat it looks like on paperWhere to check
Higher premiumThe total rises beyond the territory-wide increase your neighbours also gotTotal premium, old declarations page against new
Claims-free discount removedThe price rises because a discount line disappears, even if the base rate barely movedDiscounts section of both declarations pages
Deductible changedA higher deductible, sometimes a special deductible for the peril you claimedDeductible fields for each coverage section
Eligibility narrowedThe insurer will renew you but would not quote you as a new customer, which weakens your shopping positionAsk directly; it will not be printed on the letter
Non-renewalThe insurer declines to renew, and you must place the risk elsewhere with a recent claim on recordThe renewal letter itself, well before the expiry date

Two of the five leave the headline premium almost untouched while still costing you money or options. A removed discount is a price rise wearing a different label. A changed deductible is a cost deferred to your next claim. This is why comparing only the two totals, last year against this year, misleads: the totals can match while the policy underneath them got worse.

Frequency matters separately from size. LowestRates, summarising broker experience with current pricing algorithms, notes that insurers weigh the type and cost of a claim alongside location and claims history, and that multiple claims in a short period can make coverage harder to get at all. One large claim and three small ones are different problems. The second pattern is the one that threatens eligibility.

How long the claim follows you, and what forgiveness actually does

BrokerLink puts the lifespan of a claim on your record at about five to seven years before it is erased. That window explains the arithmetic of small claims: a modest payout can be priced against you at several renewals, while a major loss, the kind insurance exists for, is absorbed once and fades on the same schedule.

Claims forgiveness, also sold as a claims protector, softens the first event only. BrokerLink describes it as the provider waiving the premium increase after a first claim, with a second claim not waived. MyChoice adds the limit readers miss: forgiveness does not mean rates will not change. It may protect a discount or stop a defined first claim from triggering an increase, while rebuilding costs, territory rates, and your dwelling limit keep moving the total anyway. LowestRates describes claims protector coverage the same way, as an add-on that prevents a hike after a claim, not as a freeze on the whole bill.

So a forgiven claim and a rising renewal can both be true. Before assuming the forgiveness failed, separate the claim effect from everything else, which is exactly what the comparison below does.

The declarations comparison: old page against new page, line by line

MyChoice's renewal advice is concrete: compare the new and old declarations pages, including limits, deductibles, endorsements, and discounts. Run it as a procedure, not a glance.

  1. Totals first, then stop trusting them. Note both premiums, then set the totals aside until the fields explain them.
  2. Dwelling limit. If the rebuild value rose, part of your increase is arithmetic, not punishment. A higher limit costs more in a claim-free year too.
  3. Discounts. Find the claims-free discount on the old page. If it is gone or smaller on the new page, price that loss separately.
  4. Deductibles. Compare every deductible field, including any new peril-specific deductible for water or sewer backup.
  5. Endorsements and limits. Check that no coverage you had was quietly reduced, capped, or removed at renewal.
  6. Ask the remainder question. Whatever increase is left after those four fields is the part to put to your broker in one sentence: which part of this change is tied to my claim?

Success looks like this: every dollar of the difference is assigned to a field, a discount, or an unanswered question you have actually asked. A renewal you cannot decompose is a renewal you cannot negotiate or shop intelligently.

A worked renewal: separating the claim effect from everything else

Labelled assumptions, Penny style. Take a household paying 1,400 dollars a year that filed one water damage claim. The renewal arrives at 1,680 dollars, up 280 dollars, or 20 percent, inside BrokerLink's published 15 to 30 percent water damage range. The declarations comparison finds the dwelling limit was also raised for rebuilding costs, which the broker prices at 60 dollars of the increase, and the claims-free discount shrank, worth 90 dollars. That leaves 130 dollars, about 9 percent of the original premium, as the unexplained remainder to challenge or shop against.

The same 280 dollars, undecomposed, reads as a 20 percent claim penalty and invites a panicked switch. Decomposed, it is three smaller facts, one of which buys more coverage. Over the five to seven years a claim stays on record, even the true claim portion usually costs less than a major uninsured loss would have. The claim was not the mistake. Filing small, repeatable losses is the pattern the record punishes.

Stay or shop: a decision framework for the first renewal after a claim

Work through four questions in order. First, did anything besides price change? A lost discount or raised deductible can make a rival quote with a similar total the better policy. Second, is your eligibility intact? If your insurer will renew but others are quoting reluctantly, staying one more year while the claim ages can be worth more than a small saving now. Third, what does the remainder cost over the record window? A modest, shrinking surcharge is a different decision from a steep one with five years to run. Fourth, if you shop, shop the whole record honestly. The claim follows you to every insurer, so a quote built on an incomplete history is a quote that can be corrected later, at a worse moment.

None of this argues against claiming. Insurance that is never used for a major loss is the most expensive version of it. The discipline is narrower: know which of the five outcomes your claim produced, price each one separately, and make the stay-or-switch call on the decomposed numbers rather than on the scariest percentage a search result offered you.

General information only, not insurance advice. Post-claim renewal outcomes, discount rules, forgiveness terms, and record windows vary by insurer and province. Talk to a licensed insurance professional about your specific situation, and treat your own policy document as the final word.
