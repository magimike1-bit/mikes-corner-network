Home insurance claim denied or underpaid in Canada: the dispute process, step by step

Claims · 9 min read

General information only. This guide describes, in general terms, the complaint and dispute routes available to Canadian home insurance policyholders. It is not insurance or legal advice, and it cannot predict the outcome of any claim. Processes and deadlines vary by insurer and province; for a dispute of any size, a licensed insurance professional or a lawyer is the right source of advice on your specific situation.

First, name your dispute: denied or underpaid?

Disputes feel identical from the kitchen table — the insurer said no, or said less than the damage costs — but they are two different arguments, and Canadian insurance law gives them partly different tools. A denial is a coverage argument: the insurer says the loss is not covered at all, because of an exclusion, a condition, or its reading of the policy wording. An underpayment is a valuation argument: coverage is not really in dispute, but the two sides disagree about the value of what was lost or what the repairs should cost.

The distinction matters because the valuation argument has a dedicated mechanism — the statutory appraisal process described below — that exists specifically for disagreements about value and amount of loss, and because the complaint routes handle the two arguments differently. Before escalating anything, be able to finish this sentence precisely: "The insurer's position is X; the position being asked for instead is Y; the difference is Z dollars / this specific clause." Every step that follows works better with that sentence written down.

The escalation procedure

  1. Get the decision in writing, with the clause named. Ask the insurer to state its decision and the exact policy wording it relies on — the exclusion, condition, or valuation it applied. Assemble the file behind your position: the policy, photographs, repair quotes and estimates, receipts, and every email. The federal Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) gives the same starting advice for any insurance complaint: have the policy number, the agent or broker's name, the key facts, the resolution you want, and copies of related documents ready before the process begins.
  2. Ask the adjuster's level first, then open the formal complaint. A valuation disagreement sometimes resolves with a supervisor once a second quote or a missed line item is on the table. If it does not, move to the insurer's formal complaint-handling process — every insurance company in Canada has one, and it is published on the company's website. State the concern and the outcome you expect clearly, provide the documentation, and allow time for the investigation. FCAC's standing tip applies at every step: take notes — conversation dates, employee names, and what was discussed.
  3. Obtain the final position letter. At the end of the internal process, ask for a letter that clearly states the insurer's final decision on the complaint. This document is not a formality: it is the key that unlocks the independent review in the next step, because the ombudsman service cannot take the file without it.
  4. Take the file to the General Insurance OmbudService (GIO). GIO is the independent dispute resolution service for home, auto, and business insurance in Canada, and its services are free to consumers. Its Investigation Officers can only review a case once the policyholder has received a final position letter, and the insurer must be a GIO subscriber company — most Canadian home insurers are. GIO generally handles complaints about claims, interpretation of policy coverage, and policy processing and handling.
  5. Know what GIO cannot do. GIO's published scope excludes the cost of insurance and premium rates, the availability of insurance, dispute procedures that are set by law or by regulators, and matters that are or have been before the courts. A complaint aimed at one of those targets belongs with the regulator or a lawyer instead — which is why naming the dispute correctly in step one matters.
  6. Keep the court deadline in view the whole time. Complaint processes do not stop the clock on legal rights. The statutory conditions in provincial insurance law set a hard deadline for suing on the contract — in the British Columbia statutory conditions, an action is barred unless it is commenced within one year after the loss occurs, and other provinces set their own periods. Treat that deadline as the outer wall of the whole process and get legal advice well before it is anywhere close.

What success looks like: a file where every position is in writing, the final position letter is in hand, the independent route matching your dispute type is open, and no deadline — the insurer's, GIO's, or the court's — can expire unnoticed.

The parallel track: your provincial regulator

Most guides present the provincial regulator as the last resort after everything else fails. FCAC's guidance says something different and more useful: if you are not satisfied, you can file a complaint with your province or territory's insurance regulator — and you can contact the regulator separately at any point in the complaint process. The regulator's concern is conduct: whether the insurer, agent, or broker followed the laws and rules of the province where it does business, covering both the products sold and the conduct of the people selling them. Ontario's regulator is the Financial Services Regulatory Authority (FSRA); British Columbia's is the BC Financial Services Authority; Alberta's is the Superintendent of Insurance; Manitoba's is the Financial Institutions Regulation Branch. A regulator complaint will not usually re-price your claim — that is the ombudsman and appraisal territory — but a conduct problem is the regulator's business from day one, in parallel with everything else.

Quebec residents have a different external door: for both general and life insurance complaints, FCAC directs Quebec policyholders to the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) rather than the ombudsman services.

The underpayment shortcut most people miss: statutory appraisal

If coverage is accepted and the fight is purely about how much, provincial insurance legislation provides a mechanism built for exactly that fight. Under the appraisal provisions — in British Columbia, section 9 of the Insurance Act — where the insurer and the insured disagree on the value of the property insured, the value of property saved, or the amount of the loss, those questions are to be determined by an appraisal unless the two sides resolve the disagreement themselves. The same rule appears in the statutory conditions printed into policies: disagreements over value, the nature and extent of required repairs or replacements, or the amount of loss must be determined through the Act's dispute resolution process, independently of all other questions — including whether coverage itself is disputed.

Two practical details matter. First, the right has to be triggered properly: there is no right to the process until a specific demand is made in writing and the proof of loss has been delivered to the insurer. Second, the insurer has a duty to tell you the route exists: in British Columbia, the insurer must notify the insured of the availability of the appraisal process within 21 days after becoming aware of a value disagreement. Appraisal answers the valuation question only — it does not decide whether an exclusion applies — which is why an underpayment with a coverage argument attached may need the complaint route and the appraisal route at the same time, each deciding its own question.

Decision framework: which route fits your dispute?

  1. "They say it isn't covered." A wording fight. Internal complaint first, in writing, aimed at the specific clause — then GIO with the final position letter, and a regulator complaint in parallel if the handling itself was part of the problem.
  2. "They agree it's covered, but the number is too low." A valuation fight. Press the adjuster with documented quotes first; if the gap holds, the statutory appraisal process was built for this — demand it in writing once your proof of loss is delivered.
  3. "They won't respond, won't explain, or keep moving the goalposts." A conduct problem. The regulator can be contacted at any point — this is the case where parallel beats sequential.
  4. "The amount at stake is large, or the deadline is approaching." Get a lawyer involved while the free routes run. The complaint processes are free and worth using, but none of them extends the court deadline.

FAQ

Is the GIO process really free? Yes — GIO states that all of its services are free to consumers. It is an independent service, not a department of any insurer, and it covers home, auto, and business insurance complaints against its subscriber companies.

Can I skip the insurer's complaint process and go straight to GIO? No. GIO's Investigation Officers can only review a case once you have received a final position letter from the insurer — the internal process has to run to its end first. The regulator, by contrast, can be contacted at any point.

What if my insurer is not a GIO member? Then the external ladder runs through your provincial or territorial Superintendent of Insurance / regulator instead, as the standard escalation guidance describes. Ask the insurer's complaints office, in writing, which external body reviews its unresolved complaints.

Does filing a complaint pause the deadline for suing? Do not assume so. The statutory conditions set a hard commencement deadline for actions on the contract, and complaint handling runs on its own track. If a dispute is heading toward that deadline unresolved, that is a question for a lawyer, urgently — not a detail to discover afterward.

The short version

  • Name the dispute first: a denial is a coverage argument, an underpayment is a valuation argument, and the tools differ.
  • Escalate in writing: adjuster, then the insurer's formal complaint process, and insist on the final position letter at the end.
  • The final position letter unlocks GIO — the free, independent review for claims and coverage-interpretation disputes with member insurers.
  • The provincial regulator is a parallel track, contactable at any point — not a last resort.
  • For pure amount-of-loss fights, the statutory appraisal process exists specifically to settle the number; it requires a written demand after proof of loss.
  • Watch the clocks: 60 days for payment after proof of loss, and a hard statutory deadline for court that complaints do not pause.
