Running a home business: the home insurance coverage gap

Coverage gaps · 8 min read

The spare room became an office, then a studio, then a small warehouse. At no point in that progression did the home insurance policy change — and that is exactly the problem. A standard Canadian home policy is priced and worded for a dwelling and the people who live in it. Business or commercial activities sit on the exclusion lists of Canadian home policies: business-related liability and damage to property connected with a home-based business are not usually covered by a standard homeowner policy. The gap is rarely dramatic at the start. It grows quietly, one client visit, one shelf of inventory, and one paid invoice at a time, until the first claim discovers it.

Disclosure comes first — because the home policy itself is at stake

Before shopping for anything, there is a phone call to make. The Insurance Bureau of Canada puts the obligation plainly: reporting material changes to your property during the policy period is your responsibility, and you should contact your insurance representative before you set up any home-based business — its own example is a daycare — just as you would before renovating, installing a pool, or renting out part of the property. Starting a business from home is a material change because it changes the risk the insurer agreed to cover: more visitors, different equipment, different liability.

That framing matters more than any product comparison. The question is not only “is my business covered?” but “is my home still covered on the terms I think it is?” An insurer that learns about an undisclosed business only when a claim arrives is an insurer with grounds to dispute the claim and revisit the policy. Disclosing first costs a conversation. It also produces the useful outcome: the insurer or broker can say, in writing, which of the routes below fits — an endorsement on the home policy, a standalone commercial policy, or the home policy continuing unchanged because separate business coverage is in place.

What a standard policy leaves out

Broker guidance on home business coverage in Canada lists the situations a homeowner should not expect a standard policy to respond to, and the list is broader than most people guess:

  • A customer or client brings a claim against you and your home-based business.
  • A product you sell or manufacture causes bodily injury or property damage to someone else — product liability can attach whether or not you manufactured the product yourself.
  • Business inventory or business equipment is damaged or stolen.
  • A piece of business equipment causes a fire or another insured peril.
  • The business is forced to close temporarily after an incident, and the income stops with it.
  • A customer, client, or delivery visitor is injured while at your home for business purposes.
  • You damage someone else’s property in the course of doing business.

Notice what unifies the list: none of it requires the business to be large. A single client who slips on an icy walkway on the way to a home appointment is a business-purpose visit, not a social one, and that distinction is the whole ballgame for the liability section of a home policy.

Four different risks, four different coverages

“Business insurance” is not one thing, and buying the wrong component is its own kind of gap. Each risk below needs its own policy or endorsement:

RiskCoverage that respondsHome-based example
Someone is physically hurt, or their property is damagedCommercial general liability (CGL)A client trips over a cable in your studio and breaks a wrist; defence costs, settlements, and medical expenses are the CGL conversation
A client loses money because of your mistake or adviceProfessional liability (errors & omissions)A consultant’s error, a missed deliverable, or a service not provided as promised leads to a negligence claim — CGL does not respond to pure financial loss from bad work
Your business things are damaged or stolenCommercial property / business contentsInventory, stock, tools, and electronics kept at home, against perils such as fire, theft, wind, and vandalism
An insured event closes the business temporarilyBusiness interruptionLost revenue and extra expenses while repairs proceed after a covered loss; some forms also address wages that must still be paid
Client data is breached, or specialized gear breaks downCyber / equipment breakdown add-onsNotification and legal costs after a data incident; mechanical or electrical breakdown of equipment that commercial property alone would not cover

Two adjacent items belong on the same checklist even though they are easy to forget: a vehicle used to transport materials, visit clients, or make deliveries generally needs commercial auto treatment rather than a personal auto policy, and once anyone is employed by the business, employee-injury coverage through the provincial workers’ compensation system and employers’ liability enter the picture.

Endorsement or commercial policy? A decision framework

Insurers draw this line with eligibility rules, and one Ontario insurer’s published criteria for a home-business extension of a home policy shows the shape of the boundary: the business is owned and operated by the homeowner or a resident family member, operates solely from the home, is a sole proprietorship, takes in no more than $250,000 in annual gross revenue, has no employees other than family members, the home is the primary residence, and no commercial or rented space is used elsewhere. The same source describes a typical endorsement as possibly including $10,000–$50,000 for business property, third-party liability, and business interruption. Treat those numbers as one insurer’s example, not a market standard — but the structure generalizes well:

  • Desk-only, no visitors, modest equipment: a disclosed home-business endorsement is often the proportionate route. The main exposures are equipment and, for advice-based work, professional liability — which may still be a separate purchase.
  • Clients or customers come to the home: liability exposure is now physical. Confirm that business-purpose visitor injury is covered, at a limit that makes sense, before the first appointment — not after.
  • Inventory, products, or food: stock value and product liability push toward commercial property and product liability wording that a light endorsement may cap too low.
  • Employees, a second location, rented space, or revenue past an extension’s ceiling: the business has outgrown the endorsement model. The same Ontario source flags exactly these — employees, large inventory, higher-liability services, cross-border operations — as triggers for full commercial coverage.
  • Clients pay for advice or expertise: ask about professional liability by name. It is the component most often assumed and most often absent.

Premium differences between these routes are driven by ordinary underwriting factors — revenue, number of employees, industry, the services and products offered, experience, claims history, and the limits and deductibles chosen — so the honest answer to “what will it cost?” is a quote based on your actual operation, not a number from a guide.

Questions to ask, in order

  1. “I am starting (or already running) this business from home — what do I need to disclose, and can you confirm my home policy continues in force?”
  2. “Which of my risks does an endorsement cover: business property, visitor liability, product liability, interruption?”
  3. “Is professional liability included, excluded, or a separate policy for my kind of work?”
  4. “What are the eligibility limits of the endorsement — revenue, employees, visitors, space — and what happens when I cross one?”
  5. “If I outgrow the endorsement, what does the standalone commercial version look like with the same insurer or broker?”

The short version

  • Disclose the business before anything else — an undisclosed material change puts the home policy itself at risk.
  • A standard policy should not be expected to cover business inventory, equipment, visitor injuries, product claims, or lost business income.
  • CGL, professional liability, business property, and interruption are separate components; buying one does not buy the others.
  • Endorsements suit small, home-only, no-employee operations; employees, premises elsewhere, and product or advice risk point to a commercial policy.

FAQ

Does working from home as an employee create the same gap? The gap described here is about running a business. An employee working at a kitchen table is a different risk profile from a business with clients, inventory, and invoices — but the disclosure principle still applies if anything about the home’s use has materially changed, and the broker question costs nothing.

My business is tiny and entirely online. Do I still need to call? Yes — the call is the point. Small and online reduces some exposures (no visitors, little inventory) and leaves others untouched (advice errors, client data, equipment). The insurer’s answer for a tiny operation may well be a modest endorsement; it just needs to be their answer, on the record.

Can an endorsement cover everything? Sometimes, for the smallest operations — but read the components, not the label. In particular, confirm whether professional liability is actually inside it if clients pay for your judgment.
