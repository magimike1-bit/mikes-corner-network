What your mortgage lender requires from your home insurance

Mortgages · 8 min read

Home insurance in Canada is not mandatory by law. The Insurance Bureau of Canada says so directly — and then adds the sentence that governs most purchases anyway: before lending you money to buy a house, most banks and mortgage lenders will require you to purchase home insurance coverage, name them as a mortgage lender on your policy, and provide proof of that purchase. So the requirement is contractual, not statutory. A mortgage-free owner can legally go uninsured. A borrower cannot, because the home is the lender’s collateral, and an uninsured total loss would leave a loan with nothing behind it. Understanding exactly what the lender wants — document, wording, timing, amount — turns a common closing-week scramble into a two-week-early errand.

First, three products with confusingly similar names

Part of the stress around closing comes from three different products all wearing the word “mortgage.” Only the first one is this article’s subject:

ProductWhat it protectsRequired?
Home (property) insuranceThe building (and usually your contents and liability) against fire and other insured perils; claim money for a building loss is payable with the lender’s interest recognizedRequired by the lender as a condition of the mortgage — not required by law
Mortgage default insurance (CMHC and others)The lender, if you default and the sale of the property does not cover the balance owingMandatory when the down payment is under 20%; premiums in the sources checked range from 0.6% to 4.5% of the mortgage
Mortgage life / creditor insurancePays the lender the outstanding balance if you dieOptional. A lender cannot make buying it a condition of approval, and declining it does not affect your approval or your interest rate

When a lender’s checklist says “insurance,” read which row it means. The closing cannot proceed without row one (and row two, if it applies). Row three is a sales offer that can be accepted or declined on its own merits, at any point, without endangering the mortgage.

What the proof document must show

Buyers are often surprised that the full policy is not the closing document. The Canadian Bar Association’s Mortgage Instructions Toolkit — guidance written for the lawyers who close these transactions — notes that lawyers rarely receive a full insurance policy in advance of closing. What they receive, and rely on, is a certificate or binder from the insurer, broker, or borrower, which may show that coverage in favour of the lender takes effect on closing, subject to payment of the premium. If an existing policy’s declaration pages do not name the proposed lender, a certificate of insurance is needed before closing showing the lender as mortgagee/loss payee. Five details on that document do the real work:

  • The lender’s exact legal name and mailing address — the loss payee designation must match the lender’s records exactly, and the loan number should be included once it is available. A generic proof-of-insurance letter without the loss payee designation does not satisfy the requirement.
  • The lender named as mortgagee / first loss payee. Sample lender instructions in the CBA toolkit call for the Insurance Bureau of Canada standard mortgagee clause with the mortgagee as first loss payee — the clause that protects the lender’s interest even in circumstances where the borrower’s own claim might be in dispute.
  • Coverage type and amount. The same sample instructions call for fire and extended coverage, or all-risks coverage, for not less than the amount of the mortgage or the full replacement cost value of the buildings and improvements, whichever is less. Note the logic: the lender’s floor is its loan; your own interest usually argues for full replacement cost regardless, and the replacement-cost figure itself comes from the insurer or broker’s valuation, not from the lawyer.
  • The effective date matches the possession/closing date. Coverage must be active when you take ownership; a binder dated the week after closing is a closing problem, full stop.
  • Premium status. Some lenders also want confirmation that the initial premium has actually been paid, not merely quoted.

The same requirement re-appears outside purchases: refinances and home equity loans carry it too, because a new lender’s interest has to be added to the policy each time. Tell your insurer whenever the lender changes, not just when the house does.

A closing timeline that avoids the scramble

  1. At offer / mortgage commitment: note the lender’s exact legal name and address for service from the commitment documents — this spelling goes on the binder, character for character.
  2. At least two weeks before closing: arrange the policy. Buyer guidance recommends this buffer in case the application raises property-specific questions — age of roof, wiring, heating, or prior claims can all slow a quote on an older home, and none of them are two-days-before-closing surprises you want.
  3. Before closing day: send the binder or certificate to your lawyer and confirm it shows the lender as loss payee, the correct effective date, and a coverage amount that meets the lender’s instruction.
  4. On closing day: if proof of an active policy is missing, funds may not be released — the closing is delayed, with possible penalties and a very uncomfortable chain of buyers and sellers behind you.

Escrow basics, the Canadian version

Most online explanations of “escrow” describe the American default: one monthly payment split into principal, interest, taxes, and insurance (PITI), with the lender holding the tax and insurance portions and paying both bills. Canadian mortgages do not automatically work that way, and assuming they do causes two classic errors — thinking your insurance premium is being paid when it is not, and being surprised by a property tax bill you thought was handled.

The familiar Canadian arrangement is narrower. In some cases — most commonly for first-time homebuyers — a Canadian lender collects property taxes alongside the mortgage payment, holds those funds in an escrow-style account until the taxes are due, and pays the municipality on the borrower’s behalf. That is a tax collection arrangement, described in your mortgage documents; it is not automatic, it is not universal, and it says nothing about your insurance. Home insurance premiums are normally arranged between you and your insurer — monthly or annual billing, your choice within the insurer’s options — and keeping the policy in force remains your job for the life of the mortgage. The practical rule: read the commitment for a tax portion, ask the lender directly whether taxes are being collected, and never infer anything about insurance from the answer.

If coverage lapses after closing

The lender’s interest does not end at closing, and neither does the requirement. Maintaining the policy, with the lender’s interest on it, is a standing condition of the mortgage for as long as the loan is outstanding. A lapse is therefore not only a personal risk — an uninsured fire would leave you owing the full mortgage on a destroyed home — it is also a breach of the deal you signed. Renewal is the moment to be careful: changing insurers is perfectly fine, but the new policy must carry the lender’s name from its first day, and the binder for the new policy should reach the lender rather than assuming the paperwork follows you automatically. Shopping at renewal and keeping a lender properly named are fully compatible; letting a policy die quietly between insurers is the failure mode.

The short version

  • Home insurance is required by your mortgage contract, not by Canadian law — mortgage-free owners face no legal requirement.
  • The closing document is a binder or certificate naming the lender as mortgagee/loss payee, with its exact legal name, an effective date matching closing, and coverage meeting the lender’s instruction.
  • Do not confuse property insurance with mortgage default insurance (mandatory under 20% down) or mortgage life insurance (optional, and a lender cannot require it).
  • Canadian “escrow” usually means property-tax collection only. Your insurance premium is normally yours to arrange and keep current.

FAQ

Can I choose any insurer I like? The lender’s requirement is about coverage and its named interest, not about a particular company. You shop for the policy; the lender specifies what the binder must show. If a property is hard to insure — older wiring or heating, for example — start earlier, because insurer questions are the main source of delay.

What if I am buying a condo? The IBC formulation covers this directly: lenders require proof of home or condo insurance purchase. The condo corporation’s master policy covers the building structure; your unit owner’s policy covers your unit, contents, and liability, and the lender’s interest is named on yours.

Does the requirement end when my mortgage gets small? No. It runs with the loan, not the balance. It ends when the mortgage is discharged — at which point insurance becomes your choice again, and almost certainly still a wise one.
