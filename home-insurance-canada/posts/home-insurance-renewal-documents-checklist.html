Home insurance renewal documents checklist: the line-by-line review

Renewals · 9 min read

Home insurance renews annually in Canada, and the default behaviour — open the envelope, see the new number, file it — costs homeowners real money every year. Renewal season is the one moment when you hold the current policy, the new terms, and a full year of changes to your home in the same place. Insurers know most people will not read it: extreme weather losses hit a record $8.5 billion across Canada in 2024, and premiums and terms are moving accordingly. This is a document checklist, not advice about which insurer to choose: gather the papers, compare the right lines, and make the update decisions before the renewal date. This is general information, not financial advice — policy wording varies by insurer and province, and a licensed professional should weigh in on coverage decisions.

Step 1: locate both declarations pages

The declarations page is the summary of your policy — who is insured, the coverage limits, deductibles, endorsements and premium. You need two of them side by side: the current policy's and the renewal's. Broker guidance for renewals starts here because everything else is a comparison against these numbers. Line-by-line comparison is the method; scanning the premium and stopping is the mistake. The renewal envelope typically arrives 30–60 days before the renewal date — treat its arrival as the start of the checklist, not as a bill to pay.

Step 2: confirm the basics (the five-minute fixes)

Errors in the basic fields cause delayed claims and wrong premiums. Verify: the named insured, the property address, contact details, the mortgage lender or lien holder (if the mortgage was paid off, removing the lender can affect the premium), and that the renewal date matches what you expect. These are also the fields a new insurer will ask about first — year built, square footage, exterior siding, heating system, roof material and age, electrical panel type, water-heater age — so confirming them once serves both the renewal decision and any shopping around.

Step 3: evaluate the dwelling limit against rebuilding costs

Market value is not the coverage number; rebuilding cost is. The dwelling limit covers materials, labour and permits to rebuild after a loss, not what the house would sell for — and construction costs keep moving (Statistics Canada recorded 3.7% year-over-year residential construction cost growth in late 2024). The renewal review question is whether the limit still reflects local rebuilding costs after a year of inflation and any renovations: a finished basement, a new roof, an EV charger, solar panels — any of these change the rebuild figure and must be disclosed, because undeclared renovations are the classic underinsurance trap. Ask whether inflation-guard protection is included and whether it actually adjusted the limit. If the number has not moved while construction costs have, the policy has quietly shrunk.

Step 4: audit deductibles, exclusions and sublimits

Step 5: update the household facts and re-price the discounts

The renewal is also the moment the insurer learns what changed. Renovations and major purchases update the contents coverage; a security system or monitoring upgrade may unlock a discount the policy is not applying; bundling with auto may be newly available or newly worth it. Household changes — a home office, a tenant, a short-term rental, a dog — can affect eligibility or require endorsements, and undisclosed changes are the material-misrepresentation risk that voids claims. The broker checklist for a quote runs: mortgage details, home inventory, home value and details, previous claims history, alarm certificates and contents appraisals, and the existing policy for apples-to-apples comparison. Collect these once and they serve every comparison you run.

Step 6: the switching protocol (if you switch)

  1. Gather current documents first — renewal policy, alarm certificates, appraisals, claims history.
  2. Compare apples to apples — same dwelling limit basis, same deductibles, same endorsements — before comparing premiums.
  3. Start at least 30 days before renewal so there is no coverage gap; a lapse of even a few days leaves the home exposed.
  4. Do not cancel the old policy until the new one is active, and confirm the cancellation in writing with the end date.
  5. File the new documents where next year's checklist can find them.
What success looks like: both declarations pages compared line by line, renovations and household changes disclosed, discounts verified as applied, the deductible number consciously chosen — and the decision (stay or switch) made 30 days out, not the night before.

What to do with the answers: the decision meeting with yourself

The checklist produces four possible findings, and each has a correct response. Finding one: nothing material changed. Same limits, same deductibles, no new exclusions, premium movement explained by the rate environment. The correct response is to file both declarations pages, set next year's reminder 45 days before the renewal date, and stop — a clean renewal is a finished task, not an invitation to shop out of habit. Finding two: the dwelling limit lagged. Construction costs moved, renovations happened, and the limit did not. The correct response is a rebuild review with the insurer before the renewal takes effect, because an understated limit discovered after a loss is discovered too late, and co-insurance or guaranteed-replacement terms can turn a small understatement into a proportionally reduced payout. Finding three: terms quietly worsened. A deductible changed form, a roof moved to actual cash value, a sublimit shrank. The correct response is to price the old terms elsewhere — this is the comparison the document set exists for — because a renewal that reprices the same coverage is a quote; a renewal that redefines the coverage is a different product wearing the old product's name. Finding four: life changed and the policy did not. A tenant, a home business, a finished basement, a paid-off mortgage. The correct response is disclosure first and shopping second: an undisclosed material change is a claims risk that no premium saving justifies. Notice what the four findings share: none of them can be spotted from the premium line alone. The premium is the last number on the declarations page, and the renewal process works only if it is also the last number you look at.

One storage habit closes the loop. Save the renewal documents somewhere easy to find — the broker checklist's final item, and the one that makes next year's comparison a ten-minute job instead of an archaeology project. A single folder per policy year, holding the declarations page, the endorsement list, the inventory and any appraisals, is the entire system. The households that renew well are not the ones with insurance expertise; they are the ones whose paperwork is findable.

FAQ

Is it worth switching insurers every year? Shopping around can save money, but compare identical coverage first — a cheaper premium with weaker water endorsements or a higher deductible is not the same product. Continuity also matters for some claims histories; the renewal checklist is about making the stay-or-switch decision on facts, not inertia.

What causes the premium to rise even with no claims? Rate filings driven by regional loss trends (the record 2024 weather losses feed into everyone's pricing), rebuilding-cost inflation adjusting your dwelling limit, and risk-factor updates on your file. Ask the insurer to name the driver — it is a legitimate question.

Should I raise my deductible to lower the premium? A higher deductible usually lowers the premium, and the right number is one you could pay tomorrow without stress. It is a trade-off to decide deliberately at renewal, not a setting to inherit forever.
