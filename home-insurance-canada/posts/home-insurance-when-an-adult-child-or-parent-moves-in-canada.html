Home insurance when an adult child or parent moves in: the multigenerational household check

Coverage · 7 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Nov 3, 2026 · Last updated Nov 3, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how a Canadian home policy responds when a relative moves in permanently. It is not insurance or financial advice and it does not recommend a specific policy structure for any household. Occupancy rules and policy wording vary by insurer and province, and your own policy document is the final word.

Does one policy cover everyone under the roof?

Short answer: not automatically, and the conditions are knowable before moving day. A standard home policy is built around one family unit with shared finances living in the home. Insurely, a Canadian insurance platform, puts the gap plainly: the policy likely covers the policyholder's belongings and immediate dependents, but probably does not extend full protection to adult children with separate incomes or to parents with their own property. The belongings sitting in the spare room may be the least insured property in the house, owned by the person who assumes the house policy has it handled.

The scale of the arrangement is no longer unusual. Statistics Canada defines multigenerational households as three or more generations of the same family living together, and Insurely reports the 2021 figures: 2.4 million Canadians lived in multigenerational households, across 441,750 private households, up from 406,645 in 2016, with Ontario accounting for 50.2 percent of the people in such households. Insurers underwrite this pattern every day. What they penalize is not the arrangement, it is the surprise.

First, the saving: three household shapes, three insurance answers

The cheapest correct structure depends on two facts about the move: whether rent is paid, and whether the newcomer is a second family unit or joins the existing one. BrokerLink and Acera, both Canadian brokerages, describe the same dividing lines.

Relative moves in, no rent, shared householdKeep one policy and list the newcomer on it as an insuredBrokerLink: a returning adult child who pays no rent should be covered under the parents' policy as long as they are listed on it; Acera gives the same first step for extended family, list everyone living in the household as an insured so their belongings are covered
Relative pays rent, or signs a rental agreementDisclose the rental to the homeowner's insurer, and the renting relative buys their own tenant policyBoth brokerages describe renting part of a home as a commercial use the insurer must be told about; BrokerLink adds that once the adult child is an official renter, the parents' policy likely stops covering their property, and that a tenant policy covers the renter's belongings, liability and additional living expenses
A second family unit, or a separate suite with its own kitchen and entranceExpect the insurer to restructure the cover, possibly reclassifying the homeInsurely: a second family unit moving in can lead the insurer to reclassify the home as owner-occupied with a rented suite or as rental property, with different premiums and terms, and cover for two families typically costs more

One arrangement crosses all three rows: disclosure. Insurely states that anyone sharing a home, or renting part of it, must inform the insurance company whether rent is charged or the family member lives there rent free. The insurer needs to know who lives in the property and how the space is used. A short call before the move costs nothing. The same disclosure made after a fire costs a claim argument.

A useful edge case sits at the other end of the age range. BrokerLink notes that a full-time student who is still dependent and temporarily away at a recognized school is likely covered for personal belongings under the parents' policy, typically up to 10 percent of the policy limit, though a cap may apply. Temporary absence for school is a standard extension; a permanent move-in by an independent adult is a household change. Treating the second like the first is how belongings end up uninsured.

The contents limit was set for one household

Even where one policy is the right structure, its numbers were set for the old household. The contents limit was chosen to replace one family's furniture, electronics, clothing and kitchen gear. A parent arriving with a lifetime of belongings, or an adult child arriving with a full apartment's worth, pushes the true replacement total well past the limit printed on the policy, and a total loss pays to the limit, not to the true total.

BrokerLink's advice for the no-rent case is to consider increasing the contents portion so everyone's belongings are protected, and to consider raising liability cover to account for more adults in the house. Acera turns the contents point into a method: make a home inventory list together, with rough estimates of the value of everything owned, and choose policy limits from that list. Insurely gives the same method a route through the house: walk room by room, list the major items for every family unit, estimate replacement cost, and add the totals together rather than basing the estimate on a single family.

Liability deserves the same arithmetic, in the opposite direction. More occupants mean more visitors, more activity and more chances of an injury on the property. Insurely recommends personal liability starting at $1 million as a minimum and suggests $2 million for multigenerational homes. It adds a caution most families never hear: many policies contain family or household member exclusions, so if a resident relative sues another resident relative, cover may be excluded. That exclusion is worth one direct question on the disclosure call, because it cannot be fixed after the fact.

Additional living expenses need a matching check. If a covered loss makes the home uninhabitable, the coverage pays for temporary living costs, but Insurely notes that policies may specify which insured persons qualify. A household where the second family unit is not eligible has insured the hotel bill for only half the people standing on the lawn.

What else moves in: suites, pets, work and cars

A move-in rarely brings only a person. The extras that arrive with them each touch a policy somewhere, and gathering them into one disclosure list is cheaper than discovering them one claim at a time.

  • A suite or second kitchen. Insurely: adding a secondary suite, basement apartment or in-law suite, usually with a separate kitchen, bathroom and entrance, changes the property classification and must be disclosed, and the dwelling limit should reflect the full replacement cost including the suite. Renovations come first in the sequence: tell the insurer before work begins, not after.
  • A pet or a trampoline. Acera lists both as factors that may require coverage adjustments or higher rates when extended family moves in, while noting that in other cases the move changes nothing at all.
  • Working from home. Acera names it alongside pets as a possible adjustment trigger. A relative running a business from the spare room is a separate coverage question from a relative sleeping in it.
  • A car in the driveway. Acera's car insurance point: anyone who will drive a vehicle owned by someone else in the household should be added as an occasional driver on that vehicle's policy, and an unlisted driver having an accident in the vehicle could see the claim denied. A parent who moves in and can drive belongs on the list if they will use the household car. One who does not drive does not need adding.
  • Valuables. Acera notes that expensive items such as jewellery, artwork, collections or sports equipment may need additional coverage beyond the standard contents terms, whoever in the house owns them.

The pre-move procedure: five steps before moving day

  1. Decide the household shape honestly. Rent or no rent, one shared household or a second family unit, suite or no suite. The table above converts those answers into the structure to ask about.
  2. Call the insurer before the move, in Insurely's timing. Its guidance is to update the policy before a parent, in-law or adult child moves in permanently, as soon as the move is known, because damage that occurs before the insurer is told about new occupants or structural changes can lead to a denied claim. Material changes to occupancy must be disclosed.
  3. List every permanent resident as an insured on the homeowner's policy where one policy is the chosen structure, per both brokerages, and confirm in the same call how belongings, liability and additional living expenses treat each listed person.
  4. Run the joint inventory and reset the limits. Room by room, both households, replacement values. Set the contents limit from the combined total, revisit the liability limit against the larger household, and flag valuables that need additional coverage.
  5. Close the side doors. If rent will be paid, arrange the relative's own tenant policy to start on moving day. If a household car will be shared, add the occasional driver. If a renovation creates the space, notify the insurer before work starts and again when it is complete so the replacement value is updated, as Acera advises.

Success looks like this: on moving day, every adult in the house can answer two questions without guessing. Whose policy covers my belongings? And what happens to all of us if this house cannot be lived in next month?

When keeping one policy is the wrong kind of saving

One policy with everyone listed is the cheapest structure, and for the shared, no-rent household it is also the correct one. It stops being a saving when the facts have quietly crossed into row two or three of the table: rent changing hands under a handshake, a lockable suite with its own kitchen, a second family keeping separate finances in the same house. At that point the single policy is not frugal, it is misdescribed, and the premium difference the family avoided is small next to the claim the misdescription puts at risk. Pay for the structure that matches the household you actually run. That is the whole rule, and the move-in call is where it gets applied.
