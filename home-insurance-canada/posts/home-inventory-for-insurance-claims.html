Creating a home inventory to document your belongings for claims

Claims · 8 min read

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how a home inventory supports an insurance claim in Canada. It is not insurance advice and it does not recommend any product, app, or amount of coverage. Policy wording varies by insurer and province, and your own policy document is the final word for your situation.

Why an inventory is a claims document, not a chore list

A home inventory feels like housekeeping until the day it becomes evidence. After a fire, a theft, or serious water damage, the claim turns on a deceptively simple question: what exactly did you lose, and what was it worth? The Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC) advises policyholders, once it is safe, to make a complete list of damaged, destroyed, or stolen items and to support that list with proofs of purchase, receipts, owner's manuals, and warranties where possible. Building that list after a loss, from memory, in a damaged home, is the hardest possible version of the task. Building it beforehand, room by room, is the same work done under calm conditions — and IBC specifically recommends creating a record of everything you own and updating it periodically, noting that photos or videos of belongings can help.

An inventory does a second job before any loss happens. Because a policy is priced in part according to the amount of coverage required to replace your personal belongings, a finished inventory gives you a grounded total to discuss with your insurance representative when you review whether your contents limit still resembles what you actually own. Without that total, the limit on your declarations page is just a number somebody picked years ago.

Choose your method first

Most guides say "make a list and take photos" and stop there. The method matters, because each format proves different things at claim time and fails in different ways. This comparison is the step the other guides skip:

The practical answer for most households is a combination: a written list as the working record, with photos or a narrated video as the proof layer behind it. IBC publishes a home inventory checklist designed for exactly this purpose, and whether you use that checklist, a spreadsheet, or an app, the fields in the next sections are what make the record useful.

The room-by-room procedure, step by step

  1. Pick one room and finish it before moving on. A single completed room is worth more than a half-finished whole house. Start with the room holding the most value per square metre — usually the main living area or the primary bedroom.
  2. Photograph or film the room before touching anything. Take wide shots from each corner, then closer shots of shelves, surfaces, and storage. Open every drawer, closet, and cabinet on camera; the contents of closed storage are precisely what memory drops after a loss.
  3. Record each significant item as its own row. Use the field list in the next section. For groups of similar low-value items, one row with a count is enough — the goal is a credible record, not a museum catalogue.
  4. Capture serial numbers and labels where they exist. Electronics, appliances, and tools usually carry a model and serial label. Photograph the label itself so the details stay legible even if the item is later destroyed.
  5. Attach the paper trail. Where you have them, note receipts, warranties, and manuals against the item. IBC's claim guidance treats these documents as supporting proof, and they are far easier to gather now than to reconstruct later.
  6. Subtotal the room before you leave it. A per-room subtotal makes gaps visible immediately — a room that feels under-recorded usually is — and it feeds the whole-home total you will check against your policy later.
  7. Repeat room by room, including the unglamorous spaces. Garage, basement, storage locker, and shed belong in the inventory too. Tools, seasonal equipment, and stored belongings are easy to forget and slow to replace.
  8. Back the finished inventory up off-site the same day. See the storage section below — an inventory that burns with the house helps no one.

What success looks like: a stranger could open your inventory, walk into any room, and match rows to objects — and you could hand the file to an adjuster and answer "what did you lose, and what was it worth?" without relying on memory.

What to record for each item

IBC's after-loss guidance asks for descriptions, estimated values, and purchase dates where possible, backed by photos or video and supporting documents such as receipts, warranties, and owner's manuals. Working backwards from that, a useful inventory row carries: a plain description of the item (what it is, and its brand where known); the model and serial number for electronics, appliances, and tools; the approximate purchase date; what you paid or a reasonable estimated value; the room it lives in; and a photo. Perfection is not the standard — an approximate date and an honest estimate are far better than a blank row — but a row with no description, no value, and no photo will not do much work in a claim.

One valuation point deserves care. Policies settle belongings in different ways: some on a replacement-cost basis and some on a depreciated, actual-cash-value basis, and IBC notes, for example, that where a home is not rebuilt on its original site a policy would typically respond on an actual cash value basis for the home itself. Record what you paid and when, and let the policy's own settlement basis do its work — do not inflate values to "be safe," because a discrepancy between your record and the evidence helps no one.

The rooms and categories people forget

Inventories fail by omission more than by error. The categories most often missed are the ones spread thin across the whole home: clothing counted by general category rather than piece by piece; kitchen small appliances and cookware; books, games, and media; tools and outdoor equipment in the garage; seasonal items in storage; and the contents of desks and hobby spaces. A narrated video pass that opens every drawer and closet is the cheapest insurance against these gaps, because the camera records what a written list, drafted from memory at the kitchen table, leaves out.

Store it somewhere the loss cannot reach

An inventory stored only on a laptop inside the home it describes has an obvious flaw. Keep at least one copy outside the home: cloud storage, a copy with a trusted person, or another off-site location you can reach when you cannot get into the house. The same logic applies to the supporting documents — photograph receipts and warranties into the same off-site set. IBC's after-storm guidance makes the related point for the claim itself: gather supporting documents such as receipts, warranties, manuals, or previous photos of the items, and hold onto damaged belongings unless they pose a health risk, because the insurer may need to inspect them.

Close the loop: totals, limits, and sublimits

This is the decision step that turns an inventory from a record into a check on your coverage. Work through it in order:

  1. Total the inventory. Add the room subtotals into one whole-home figure.
  2. Compare it with your contents limit. Find the personal-property limit on your declarations page. If the inventory total is close to or above that limit, that is a conversation to have with your insurance representative — not a conclusion to draw on your own, because how limits apply varies by policy.
  3. Flag the special categories. Many policies apply lower internal limits — sublimits — to categories such as jewellery, bicycles, collectibles, and similar high-value items. Your inventory cannot tell you your sublimits, but it can tell you which categories you own enough of to ask about by name, and whether listing an item individually on the policy (often called scheduling) is a question worth raising.
  4. Note what is not yours or not at home. Belongings belonging to other people, items kept elsewhere, and anything connected to a business activity at home may be treated differently. Flag them in the inventory rather than assuming they are counted.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada publishes consumer guidance on home, condo, and tenant insurance — including how premiums are set and how claims work — and it is a sensible, neutral place to read before that conversation.

Keep it current without starting over

IBC advises updating the record periodically, and also reviewing and updating the inventory after any loss. In practice, three triggers keep an inventory honest without an annual marathon: add a row whenever a significant item arrives (the receipt is still in your hand, which makes this a two-minute job); delete or mark items that leave the home; and do one short review pass a year, walking the rooms with the list and correcting drift. A current inventory that is 90 percent complete will outperform a perfect one from six years ago every time.

FAQ

Does an inventory guarantee a claim will be paid? No. An inventory supports a claim by documenting what you owned; whether a loss is covered still depends on the policy wording, the cause of loss, and the policy's limits and conditions.

Is a video alone enough? A video is strong proof that items existed, but it cannot be searched or totalled, and values and serial numbers are rarely legible in it. Pairing video with a written list gives the claim both proof and a usable record.

What if there are no receipts for older items? Record the description, an approximate purchase date, and an honest estimated value, and photograph the item. IBC's guidance asks for purchase dates and values "where possible" — an incomplete record is still far stronger than none.

Do renters and condo owners need an inventory too? The same logic applies: tenant and condo policies cover belongings, and IBC's tenant guidance makes the same recommendation to create a record of what you own and update it periodically.

The short version

  • Build the list before a loss, room by room: photos or video as proof, a written record an adjuster can search and total.
  • Record description, model and serial numbers where they exist, approximate date, value, room, and a photo for each significant item.
  • Store at least one copy off-site — an inventory lost with the home helps no one.
  • Total the inventory and compare it with your contents limit; flag high-value categories and ask about sublimits by name.
  • Update on triggers — new purchases, departures, one short annual pass — not by starting over.
