How a home renovation changes your insurance in Canada

Renovations · 7 min read

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how a renovation interacts with a Canadian home insurance policy. It is not insurance advice, it does not recommend any insurer or product, and it is not written by a broker or agent. Insurers set their own rules, and those rules differ — confirm anything that matters with your own insurer or broker, and treat your policy documents as the final word.

The misconception: your policy does not automatically follow your renovation

Many homeowners assume the policy quietly updates itself: the house gets a new room, the coverage stretches to fit, and the premium sorts itself out at renewal. Nothing in the sources supports that picture. Centris, the Quebec real estate organisation, warns that an unreported added room or garage may simply not be insured when a claim arrives — the new space exists in the house, but not in the contract. YouSet, a Canadian insurance platform, states the sharper version of the same risk: undisclosed renovations can mean denied claims, or policy cancellation for misrepresentation. A renovation changes the house the insurer agreed to cover. Until the insurer hears about it, the policy still describes the old house.

The useful way to handle that gap is to stop treating notification as one chore and see it as three separate moments — before the work, during the work, and after it — because a different risk sits in each one.

Before: when notification is mandatory and when it can wait

The sources divide renovations into two notification classes, and the dividing line is the scale of the job:

Type of workWhen to tell the insurerSource basis
Major projects — work needing a permit, or work done by contractorsBefore the work beginsYouSet: notify before work begins for major projects needing a permit or contractors
Smaller updatesOnce the work is finishedYouSet: smaller updates can be reported once finished
Work that empties the house, or lifts the rebuild cost sharplyWhen construction starts — this is the case notification matters mostCentris: notification matters particularly if you vacate for an extended period, or the reconstruction value increases by at least $15,000

That $15,000 figure is worth treating as a household rule of thumb, with its limits stated plainly: it comes from one source, Centris, writing for a Quebec audience, and it describes when notification matters particularly — not a national legal threshold. Used honestly, it gives a renovating household a concrete test. If the finished job will cost the insurer at least $15,000 more to rebuild than the house they currently have on file, the phone call is not optional in any practical sense.

Centris also advises notifying your insurer when construction starts, and notes that premiums may rise during the work itself. That last detail surprises people: the premium change is not only an after-the-renovation event. The house under construction is a different risk from the house that was quoted, and the price can move while the walls are still open.

During: contractors, materials and the construction-site problem

The middle moment is the one renovation guides most often skip. Once work starts, part of the risk sits with your contractor — but only part of it, and the boundary is exact.

The Canadian Home Builders' Association's homeowner guide to renovation contracts sets the standard a contractor should meet: third-party liability insurance of at least $1,000,000, with many contractors carrying $2,000,000 or more (CHBA guide, checked October 25, 2026). Liability cover is what responds when the work injures someone or damages property beyond your house. A contractor who cannot show a certificate at or above that $1,000,000 minimum is asking the homeowner to carry a risk the homeowner did not price into the job.

The same guide draws a line many homeowners learn about too late. The contractor should be insured for theft of materials the contractor is responsible for — but the contractor's insurance will not cover materials provided by the homeowner. Buy the tiles, the fixtures or the flooring yourself and store them on site, and their theft is not the contractor's insurer's problem. Whether it is your own policy's problem is a question to ask your insurer before the materials arrive, not after they disappear.

Two further during-the-work exposures complete the picture. The CHBA guide notes that a major project can turn the home into a construction site, affecting the conditions of the homeowner's own policy — the policy was written for a dwelling, and a dwelling mid-rebuild is a different thing. And Centris flags the vacancy side: notification matters particularly if you vacate the home for an extended period during the work, because an empty house under renovation combines two changes an insurer wants to know about at once.

After: resetting the rebuild value so the new space is insured

When the work ends, the insurance job is a valuation job. The policy's job is to rebuild the house as it now stands; if the dwelling amount on file still describes the pre-renovation house, the new room or garage sits outside the number the claim will be calculated from. Centris's warning is the concrete version: an unreported added room or garage may not be insured at claim time.

YouSet describes what insurers want at this stage: project details, costs, and photos when the work is complete. That trio is the closing paperwork of the renovation, and it is cheap to assemble while the receipts and the contractor's final invoice are still on the kitchen table. It becomes archaeology a year later.

A short decision framework for the after stage:

  1. Did the rebuild cost change? Use the $15,000 test from the before stage. At or above it, the dwelling value on the policy needs a deliberate reset, confirmed with the insurer — not an assumption that renewal will catch it.
  2. Did the house gain space or a new structure? An added room or garage is the specific case Centris names as potentially uninsured if unreported. Name it to the insurer explicitly.
  3. Can you prove what was spent and what it looks like? Details, costs and photos are what YouSet says insurers ask for. If the file exists, this step is an email. If it does not, rebuild the file from invoices before they scatter.

Renovations that raise the stakes, and the honest limits of the premium-down list

The premium-up side is documented in plain terms: premiums may rise during the work (Centris), and a renovation that raises the reconstruction value raises the amount of house there is to insure. The premium-down side is where this guide has to be careful. None of the sources checked for this piece promises a discount for a particular upgrade, so no list of "renovations that lower your premium" is printed here — a household that wants that answer should ask its own insurer which completed upgrades it credits, and get the answer in writing.

What can be said without inventing numbers is where the real financial risk sits, and it is not the premium line. It is the claim line: an undisclosed renovation can mean a denied claim or a cancelled policy (YouSet), and an unreported room or garage may not be insured at all (Centris). Measured against those outcomes, a premium that rises because the house is worth more to rebuild is the system working as described — the expensive renovation mistake is the unreported one, not the repriced one.

FAQ

Do I need to tell my insurer about a small cosmetic update? YouSet's distinction gives the working answer: smaller updates can be reported once finished, while major projects needing a permit or contractors should be reported before the work begins. When a job sits near that boundary, the before-the-work call is the safer reading of the same rule.

How much contractor insurance is enough? The CHBA's homeowner guide sets the floor: at least $1,000,000 of third-party liability insurance, with many contractors carrying $2,000,000 or more. Ask to see the certificate before work starts.

Who covers materials I bought myself if they are stolen from the site? Not the contractor's insurer — the CHBA guide is explicit that a contractor's insurance will not cover materials the homeowner provides. Ask your own insurer how supplied materials are treated before they are delivered to the site.

We are moving out during the renovation. Does that change anything? Yes — Centris names vacating for an extended period as one of the situations where notification matters particularly. Tell the insurer the house will be empty, and for how long, before the move-out date.
