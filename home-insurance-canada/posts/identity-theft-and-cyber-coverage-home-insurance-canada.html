Identity theft and cyber coverage add-ons in Canadian home policies: what each one actually pays for

Coverage · 7 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Nov 3, 2026 · Last updated Nov 3, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how identity theft and personal cyber add-ons work in Canadian home insurance. It is not insurance or financial advice and it does not recommend buying either product. Endorsement wording, limits and prices vary by insurer and province, and your own policy document is the final word.

Two numbers first: $30 and $75 a year

Start with the price, because the price frames the whole decision. MyChoice, a Canadian insurance comparison site, publishes a comparison table built from its own data, checked for this guide on November 3, 2026. The median premium for an identity theft endorsement is $30 per year. The median premium for a personal cyber endorsement is $75 per year. The common coverage limit shown for both is $25,000.

Same limit, two and a half times the median price. That gap is the first clue that the two products are not the same product with different labels. The second clue is take-up: the same source reports that just over half of homeowners, 54 percent, carry identity theft coverage as an add-on, while only 1.5 percent choose personal cyber protection. Most buyers are purchasing the narrower product. Whether the narrower product is the right one depends on a boundary the sales material tends to soften.

The trap: restoration expenses are not your money back

An identity theft endorsement pays for the cleanup, not the theft. MyChoice lists what it pays: notary fees, correspondence costs, lost wages, credit card application fees, and professional or court fees spent restoring your identity. Then the same guide states the boundary plainly. The endorsement generally does not cover actual financial losses from fraud, such as a loan taken out in your name, business losses, or fraudulent charges that your credit card already covers.

Read that twice before valuing the product. If a fraudster spends on your card, the card system is usually the route that deals with those charges, and the endorsement was never the payer. If a fraudster opens a loan in your name, the endorsement does not repay the loan. What it buys is your time and your paperwork back: the wages for the days spent fixing records, the fees, and access to help. Ratehub makes the matching point about who benefits most, noting that the case for the endorsement strengthens for someone who would lose income taking time off to handle an incident.

The background risk is real, and it does not change the boundary. Ratehub, citing the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, reports 33,854 fraud reports processed, over 23,000 victims and $544 million in reported fraud losses as of September 30, 2025. A large national problem can still be a poor match for a narrow product, and a small premium can still be wasted money if the event you fear is the one the endorsement excludes.

Aviva Canada's own page for its household cyber endorsement shows what the broader product looks like from an insurer. Its listed components are online fraud cover, including identity theft and unauthorized bank or credit card use; computer attack cover paying to restore data and systems after malware; home systems attack cover for connected home devices; identity recovery with a case manager who helps restore identity and credit records; data breach cover; cyberbullying reimbursement, including counselling, legal expenses, relocation, tutoring and social media monitoring; cyber extortion assistance; and dark web monitoring, complimentary with registration. Aviva also states that household members covered under the home policy have access to the cyber coverages, and that coverage continues on vacation outside Canada. The same page carries the honest fine print: the products are subject to the terms, conditions, restrictions and exclusions in the final policy wording, and availability varies by province or territory.

MyChoice offers a plain way to choose between the rows. Identity theft coverage suits a homeowner whose worry is the logistics of identity recovery and who wants a base level of support. Personal cyber may suit a household with family members at risk of cyberbullying, regular online banking and shopping, a lot of personal information stored digitally, or smart home features. Notice that the second list is about how the household lives, not about how frightened it feels.

The free layer to check before paying anything

Two checks cost nothing and come before any purchase.

Check the policy you already hold. MyChoice notes that some home policies cover identity theft recovery costs as standard, even though most do not. Ratehub gives the concrete version: some insurers, such as the Co-operators, include free identity fraud coverage on all home, condo and tenant policies, up to $10,000. A household already carrying included cover who then buys a $30 endorsement may be paying to duplicate the layer it owns. One look at the endorsements page of the current policy settles it.

Do the free prevention steps either way. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre lists them: reject unsolicited requests for personal or financial information; guard your social insurance number and never provide it through an unsolicited contact; do not click suspicious links or attachments; disable automatic logins; use strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication where available; and check your credit reports for unauthorized accounts at least once a year through Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada. No endorsement substitutes for these, and the endorsement decision gets easier once they are done, because the remaining risk is the risk actually being priced.

If it happens anyway: the response order

Whether or not cover is in place, the official response sequence is the same, and it costs nothing to follow. Drawing on the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre guidance:

  1. Gather the evidence first. Keep the emails, messages, letters and statements that show what happened, including letters about credit applications you did not submit and unfamiliar transactions.
  2. Call your financial institution. Report unauthorized transactions or suspicious account activity, and follow its instructions on cards and account flags.
  3. Contact both credit bureaus. Report illicit activity on your credit report to Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada.
  4. Report to your local police and keep the file details for the institutions that ask for them.
  5. Report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre through its online reporting system or by phone at 1-888-495-8501.
  6. Notify the affected agencies and providers. Missing or compromised government identification goes to the issuing agency, re-routed or tampered mail goes to Canada Post, and lost access to email or social accounts goes to the relevant provider. If you hold an endorsement, MyChoice adds the practical step of contacting the insurer promptly so any support services start early.

Success looks like this: every institution that can freeze, flag or reverse something has been told within the first day, and you hold one written log of who was told, when, and what reference number came back.

When each add-on is worth it, and when it is not

The identity theft endorsement earns its $30 median when the household's real exposure is time: a worker who would lose wages across several days of calls, letters and office visits to restore records, in a household with no included cover already on the policy. It is not worth it as protection against stolen money, because stolen money is the thing it generally does not repay, and it is not worth it at all where equivalent cover is already included free.

The personal cyber endorsement earns its $75 median when the household's life is genuinely digital in the ways the broader cover names: smart home devices that could be attacked, children exposed to cyberbullying risk, heavy online banking and shopping, and personal records stored digitally whose restoration would mean paid help. For a household with few connected devices, strong passwords and multi-factor authentication already in place, and no cyberbullying exposure, the extra $45 a year over the identity theft median buys cover for events the household has largely engineered away.

Either way, buy the wording, not the label. Two products called cyber cover can differ in limits, exclusions and services, and the insurer's own warning stands: the final policy wording governs. Read the endorsement's exclusions list before paying, and re-check at renewal whether included cover has appeared on the base policy, because products in this corner of the market are still changing quickly.
