Oil tanks and oil heating: home insurance rules in Canada

Heating · 6 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Oct 29, 2026 · Last updated Oct 29, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how Canadian home insurers approach oil tanks and oil heating. It is not insurance advice, it does not recommend any insurer, product, or heating system, and it is not written by a broker, agent, or oil technician. Tank rules are set by each insurer's underwriting — confirm anything that matters with your own insurer or broker, and treat your policy documents as the final word.

Three words that are not the same word

Insured means a policy exists on the home. Insurable means an insurer is willing to keep offering that policy — and an ageing oil tank is one of the things that can change the answer at renewal. Covered means a specific loss would actually be paid. A home can be insured and insurable while a particular tank loss is not covered at all. Most of the confusion around oil heat comes from treating those three as one question. They are three, and a tank household needs an answer to each.

The comparison that follows puts the age lines side by side first, because that is where the three answers start to separate.

Read the table as a warning about certainty, not as a single rule. One source reports an IBC line that splits by location — 15 years outside, 25 inside. Another reports some insurers drawing the line at 20 years with no split. A third treats 25 years as the practical cutoff and names an inspection route back to insurability. These do not reconcile into one number, because there is no one number to find: the final call on a given tank is the individual insurer's underwriting decision for that home. Quoting any single age as a law of Canadian insurance would be wrong, and a renewal letter that cites a different age than a website did is not necessarily an error — it is a different decision-maker.

Why age matters at all is mechanical, not bureaucratic. Tanks corrode from the inside out: condensation settles at the bottom of the tank, so the first visible sign of failure can be the leak itself. A household cannot rely on spotting rust in time, which is why insurers lean on the calendar instead of the tank's appearance.

The hole in the standard policy: when the oil gets out

Suppose the tank passes the age test and the policy renews. The second question — covered — still has a gap in it. Per Pétrole Pagé, again citing the IBC, damage caused by an outdoor tank is not covered by most insurance policies. The tank that is easiest for an insurer to accept on age grounds, sitting outside the house, is the one whose damage the standard policy may leave with the homeowner.

The product that fills that gap has a specific name: an Escape of Fuel Oil endorsement. Per the same heating-oil source, that endorsement covers direct damage to the insured property and soil cleaning and decontamination, typically up to a maximum of $150,000. Two things about that figure deserve plain reading. It is a maximum the source describes as typical, not a guaranteed limit on any one policy. And the fact that a decontamination cap is stated in six figures at all tells a household what kind of bill the coverage is built for.

Set the other dated figure beside it. The isure older-home guide says a leak cleanup can cost as much as $20,000. Those two numbers — as much as $20,000 for a cleanup in one source, a decontamination maximum of $150,000 in another — do not average into a tidy estimate, and they should not be presented as one. They are different sources describing different scopes of mess, checked Oct 25, 2026. What they agree on is the shape of the risk: a spill can contaminate soil and groundwater and become a very expensive cleanup, and the cost lands on whoever the policy does not protect.

Scale helps explain why insurers care. The heating-oil source reports that about 12% of Quebec homes heat with oil, and that the average Quebec tank age is 28 years — older, that is, than every age line in the table above. A large stock of tanks past the common cutoffs is the background fact behind strict renewal letters.

Buried tanks: the least visible version of the same problem

The sources checked set out age lines for exterior and interior above-ground tanks; they do not set a separate buried-tank age line, and none is invented here. What carries over is the failure pattern: corrosion that starts inside, at the bottom, with the leak as the first sign. Underground, that pattern has no puddle on a basement floor to announce it — the soil and groundwater contamination the sources describe is the announcement.

For a buried tank, then, the practical questions for the insurer are the same three from the opening, asked explicitly rather than assumed: is the home insured with the tank disclosed, does the insurer consider it insurable at its age and type, and would an escape of fuel oil from it be covered, endorsed, or excluded? A buried tank that nobody has asked about since the house last changed hands is a gap in the household's own records before it is anything else. Getting the answers in writing, while there is no leak and no deadline, is the cheapest move available.

Three moves: replace, endorse, or convert

  1. Replace the tank. This addresses insurability directly: a newer tank answers the age objection, and on the isure account, a tank older than 25 years may need exactly this — a newer tank certified by a TSSA inspector — before insurance is available again. Ask the insurer, before spending, what age and certification it will accept, so the replacement is bought against a named standard rather than a hope.
  2. Add the Escape of Fuel Oil endorsement, if offered. This addresses coverage, not age: it is the route the heating-oil source describes for direct damage and soil decontamination, typically up to $150,000. It does not make an over-age tank younger, so it pairs with, rather than replaces, the insurability question.
  3. Convert away from oil. Removing the tank removes the tank questions with it — but conversion is a heating decision with its own costs and trade-offs, and no figure for it is offered here because the sources checked do not price one. Households weighing it should price the conversion itself, the removal, and the insurance position after conversion as three separate lines.

Each move fixes a different word from the opening section. Replacement speaks to insurable, the endorsement speaks to covered, and conversion retires both questions. Spending on one while the open question was another is the expensive mistake — a new endorsement on a tank the insurer is about to age out, or a new tank whose leaks the policy still does not cover.

Buying or selling with a tank: put it in the open

A tank changes hands with the house, so its paperwork should too. Before an offer firms up, a buyer can ask for the tank's age and location (interior, exterior, or buried), any inspection or certification records, and whether the seller's policy carries an Escape of Fuel Oil endorsement. A seller who can produce those answers is handing over a priced risk; a seller who cannot is handing over the three unanswered questions above, and the price conversation should reflect that.

What success looks like: the household — owner, buyer, or seller — can state the tank's age and location, name the age line its own insurer actually applies, and say whether escaped-oil damage is covered, endorsed, or excluded on its policy. Three facts, all obtainable with one phone call and one look at the tank's label. Until they are stated, every other number in the oil-heating budget is built on an assumption.

The short version

  • IBC guidance as reported: an exterior tank older than 15 years, or an interior tank older than 25 years, will not be insured — but some insurers refuse tanks over 20 years, so the deciding line is each insurer's own underwriting.
  • Tanks corrode from the inside out, so the first sign of failure can be the leak itself; a spill can contaminate soil and groundwater.
  • Damage caused by an outdoor tank is not covered by most policies, per the IBC as reported by a heating-oil source; an Escape of Fuel Oil endorsement covers direct damage and soil decontamination typically up to $150,000.
  • A leak cleanup can cost as much as $20,000 (isure, older-home guide) — a different scope from the $150,000 decontamination maximum, not an average of it.
  • About 12% of Quebec homes heat with oil and the average Quebec tank age is 28 years, past every age line in the sources.
