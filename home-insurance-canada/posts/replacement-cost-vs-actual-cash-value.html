Replacement cost vs actual cash value: how your policy actually settles a loss

Coverage · 9 min read

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, the two ways a Canadian home insurance policy can value what you lost. It is not insurance advice and it does not recommend a basis or a coverage amount. Policy wording varies by insurer and province, and your own policy document is the final word for your situation.

The two settlement bases, in plain language

Every home insurance claim eventually becomes a number, and that number depends on which of two settlement bases your policy applies. Replacement cost settles based on what it costs to repair or replace the damaged or destroyed property with a new equivalent — its value at the time of loss, without deducting depreciation. The Insurance Bureau of Canada's consumer guidance describes the standard home policy exactly this way: reimbursement is generally based on value at the time of loss, with no depreciation taken into account. One insurer's own wording puts it the same way: if you repair or replace the property, the claim is covered without any deduction for depreciation, so a five-year-old television lost in a fire is replaced with a new TV of like kind, quality, and usefulness.

Actual cash value (ACV) is replacement cost minus depreciation — an amount for the age, wear, and tear the item had already accumulated. The same insurer's FAQ describes the cash-settlement case: if you decide not to repair or replace, the insurer can pay the depreciated cost of the item, priced on its age and condition at the time of loss. A ten-year-old bicycle is the example given — the payment reflects what that bicycle had become worth, and it will not be enough to buy a new equivalent.

The single most useful thing to understand is that these are not two kinds of policy so much as two answers to two different questions: are you replacing the item, and what does your policy promise if you are not? That is why the same policy can contain both bases at once.

Which parts of a policy use which basis

Most guides define the two terms and stop. The part that matters at claim time is where each one applies inside a single policy:

Part of the policyUsual basisHow it settles
The dwelling (the house itself)Replacement costCost to rebuild or repair with materials similar to those damaged, on the same site — provided the home is actually rebuilt there; see the conditions below
Contents you replaceReplacement costNew equivalent of like kind and quality, no depreciation deduction, subject to your contents limit — usually paid in two steps (below)
Contents you do not replaceActual cash valueCash for the depreciated value — replacement cost minus depreciation for age and condition
Antiques and similar itemsNeither, by defaultSome belongings, such as antiques, cannot be settled at replacement cost at all; a predetermined agreed value can be arranged for valuable items instead

Two conditions do heavy lifting in that table. First, IBC's guidance is explicit that replacement-cost settlement comes with requirements — the item must actually be replaced, or the house rebuilt on the same piece of land. Decide not to replace, and the settlement drops to the depreciated basis even on a replacement-cost policy. Second, the dwelling figure is a rebuild figure, not a price tag: insurers describe it as the amount needed to completely rebuild or repair the home using similar materials, set using third-party data and appraiser or contractor input. It is deliberately distinct from market value — what the home would sell for — and it ignores the value of the land entirely.

How depreciation is actually applied

Depreciation is not a guess or a penalty. Insurers commonly work from depreciation tables that assign categories of property an expected useful life, and the deduction grows with the share of that life the item had used up. A published version of the formula makes the arithmetic plain:

ACV = RCV − (DPR × RCV × AGE) — where RCV is what the item costs to buy new today, DPR is the depreciation rate per year, and AGE is the item's age in years. An item with a ten-year expected life depreciates at roughly 10 percent a year under a straight-line table; at five years old, half its replacement cost has been deducted.

IBC's own consumer example shows the same arithmetic on one item. A refrigerator bought ten years ago for $800 has a current equivalent price of $1,000. Settled at replacement cost, the compensation is $1,000. Settled at actual cash value, depreciation of $40 a year over ten years — $400 — comes off, and the compensation is $600. Note what the example quietly demonstrates: the starting point is today's price of the equivalent item, not what was originally paid.

Worked example: one contents loss, both bases, step by step

The example below is illustrative only. The items, prices, ages, and assumed useful lives are invented for the arithmetic; real insurers use their own depreciation tables, and your policy's limits and deductible are your own. It exists to show the sequence of the math, not to predict any claim.

Say a fire destroys three items, and the policy carries a $1,000 deductible:

Sofa$2,4006 years / 12 years50% = $1,200$1,200

Totals$4,800$2,750

Now run the deductible and the payment sequence through both bases:

  1. Actual cash value settlement: $2,750 minus the $1,000 deductible = $1,750, paid as a cash settlement. Replacing everything new costs $4,800, so $3,050 of the rebuild comes out of pocket.
  2. Replacement cost settlement, first payment: most Canadian insurers pay replacement-cost claims in two stages — the first payment is the actual cash value amount. Here: $2,750 minus the $1,000 deductible = $1,750, the same cheque as above.
  3. Replacement cost settlement, second payment: once the items are actually replaced and the receipts submitted, the insurer pays the difference between the ACV amount and the full replacement cost: $4,800 minus $2,750 = $2,050. Total received across both payments: $3,800 — the full replacement cost minus the deductible, exactly once.

The lesson of the example is the timing, not just the totals. Under replacement cost, the policyholder fronts the difference between the first payment and the store price and recovers it afterward — and the second payment has a deadline, covered next.

The two-payment holdback, and its deadline

The two-stage structure above is standard practice, not an exception: insurers typically pay the depreciated amount first, then release the remaining replacement cost once the purchase has actually happened and a receipt proves it. The condition that catches people out is the window. The replacement generally has to happen within a set period — commonly stated as somewhere between 180 days and two years, depending on the policy — to collect the second payment. Miss the window, or decide not to replace after all, and the claim simply stays at the actual cash value amount. This is the mechanical reason behind IBC's condition that the item must absolutely be replaced to receive compensation without depreciation: the holdback is that condition, enforced through the payment schedule.

Replacement cost is also not all-or-nothing across a policy. It can apply to the dwelling, to contents, or to both, and a policy can carry it on one and not the other — one more reason the declarations page, not the brochure, is where the answer lives.

Standard, extended, and guaranteed replacement cost on the dwelling

For the house itself, three related promises get blurred together in marketing language, and they differ exactly where it hurts — at the dwelling limit. Standard replacement cost pays to rebuild with similar materials, up to the dwelling limit on the policy. If rebuilding costs more than that limit, the difference is the policyholder's problem. Guaranteed replacement cost is the stronger promise: the insurer pays the full rebuild cost even if it exceeds the stated limit. It is not automatic and not universal — insurers note plainly that not all customers qualify for it. Because the dwelling's replacement value is an estimate built from construction data, the practical risk sits with an estimate that has drifted below real rebuild costs after renovations or construction inflation; that is why insurers advise reviewing the figure regularly.

Decision framework: checking your own policy

You cannot change a settlement basis after a loss, but you can know yours before one. Work through these checks on your own declarations page and policy wording:

  1. Find the basis named for contents. Look for "replacement cost" or "actual cash value" wording in the personal property section — and check whether the replacement-cost promise is conditional on actually replacing, as it usually is.
  2. Find the basis named for the dwelling, separately. Contents and dwelling can differ. Note whether the dwelling wording says anything about guaranteed replacement cost, and do not assume it is present.
  3. Find the replacement window. The deadline for replacing items and submitting receipts to recover the holdback is a specific number of days in the wording. Put it in the calendar the day a claim opens, not the week it closes.
  4. Flag the items neither basis serves well. Antiques and similar belongings may not settle at replacement cost at all; if you own pieces whose value is the point, ask about agreed-value scheduling by name.
  5. Compare the dwelling figure with a rebuild estimate, not a listing price. Market value includes land and neighbourhood; the policy figure should track construction cost for a similar home on the same lot.

FAQ

Is replacement cost always better than actual cash value? It pays more at claim time, because no depreciation is deducted — but it is a promise with conditions attached: you must actually replace, within the policy's window, and premiums reflect the larger promise. Whether the trade suits a given household is a coverage decision for that household and its insurance representative, not a universal answer.

Why did a replacement-cost policy pay less than the new price first? Because the first payment is normally the actual cash value amount; the balance — the holdback — is released after the item is replaced and the receipt submitted. A first cheque below replacement cost is the design of the process, not by itself an error.

What happens if I take the cash and never replace? Then the settlement remains at actual cash value: replacement cost minus depreciation, minus your deductible. The option to settle on the depreciated basis is built into the same policies that offer replacement cost.

Does the deductible come off under both bases? Yes. The deductible is subtracted from the settlement whichever basis applies — in the worked example it comes off the ACV figure once, and the replacement-cost total across both payments is likewise the full cost minus the deductible once.

The short version

  • Replacement cost pays the new-equivalent price with no depreciation deduction; actual cash value subtracts depreciation for age, wear, and condition first.
  • One policy can hold both: contents you replace settle at replacement cost, contents you do not replace settle at actual cash value.
  • Depreciation runs off tables and a simple formula — ACV = replacement cost minus (rate × replacement cost × age) — applied per item, then the deductible comes off.
  • Replacement-cost claims usually pay in two steps: ACV first, the holdback after you replace and submit receipts, inside a deadline that commonly runs 180 days to two years.
  • On the dwelling, standard replacement cost stops at the limit; guaranteed replacement cost does not — and not everyone qualifies for it.
