Scheduling valuables on a Canadian home policy: special limits, floaters, and the category-total trap

Coverage · 7 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Oct 31, 2026 · Last updated Oct 31, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how special limits and scheduled coverage for valuables work in Canadian home insurance. It is not insurance or financial advice and it does not recommend scheduling any particular item. Limits, terms and prices vary by insurer and province, and your own policy document is the final word for your situation.

Start with two numbers: $15,000 and $3,000

Western Financial Group, a Canadian brokerage, uses a plain example to explain special limits. A household has $15,000 worth of jewellery stolen. The policy's special limit for jewellery is $3,000. The reimbursement is $3,000 — not $15,000, and not the household's total contents limit, however large that limit is.

That gap surprises people because the contents limit on the declarations page looks so comfortable. But inside that large limit sit smaller ones. A special limit — also called a standard limit — is the maximum a policy pays for a particular category of high-value belongings, such as jewellery. The category limit, not the headline contents limit, is the number that decides a valuables claim.

One insurer guide, Park Insurance, publishes the ranges it typically sees: jewellery, watches and furs often capped at $5,000 to $10,000 in total; artwork and collectibles typically $5,000 to $15,000; cash and precious metals usually limited to a few hundred dollars; firearms often $2,000 to $5,000. Treat those as one guide's published ranges, not as your policy's figures. Yours are printed in your own policy, in the personal property section — and finding them is step one of the audit at the end of this guide.

The trap: the limit is on the category, not the piece

Read the Western example again and notice its shape. The $15,000 is a total for the category. Nothing in it requires one spectacular ring. Three ordinary pieces clear it easily.

The split below is an illustrative breakdown of that same $15,000 total, labelled as an example. Only the total and the $3,000 limit come from the published example; the individual piece values are invented to show the arithmetic.

Park Insurance makes the same point from the other direction: even if individual pieces sit below the limit, a collection whose total exceeds the cap is the case for scheduling the most valuable items. A household that checks each piece separately — "nothing I own is worth more than the limit" — can pass its own check and still be $12,000 short on the only day the number matters. Add up categories, not pieces.

The categories worth totalling are the ones both guides name: jewellery and watches, furs, artwork and collectibles — Park also lists coins, stamps and sports cards among collectibles — plus bicycles and other sports equipment, and musical instruments. Cash sits at the far end of the scale: Western notes stolen cash is covered only to a small limit.

Three options, compared honestly

Once a category total sits above its special limit, there are three real responses, not one. Scheduling is the best known; it is not always the right one.

OptionWhat changesWhat it costsWho it suits
Leave it under the special limitNothing. The category claim is capped at the limit, and value above it is self-insuredNo extra premiumHouseholds whose category total is only slightly above the limit, or who could replace the pieces without hardship
Schedule the itemThe named item is insured up to its documented value, above the standard limit, usually with no deductible, and typically with broader coverAdditional premium, which Western notes depends on how much the item is worthA single piece, or the top pieces of a collection, whose loss would genuinely hurt
Ask about the limit itself, or a standalone policyThe category limit or the policy structure changes instead of listing items one by oneVaries; only your insurer can quote itCollections spread across many mid-value pieces, where scheduling each one is impractical

Western's own framing of the scheduling decision is financial, and it is the right test: scheduling makes financial sense when an item's value exceeds what the policy would normally pay for it. Below that point, the extra premium buys back a gap that is small enough to carry. Above it, the household is quietly self-insuring the difference whether it chose to or not.

What scheduling changes — and what it does not

To schedule an item is to list it individually on the policy — most insurers do this through a personal articles floater, also called a rider or endorsement. Western describes the mechanics: the policyholder pays additional premium to insure that particular valuable up to its value, above the standard limit. Park adds what the insurer needs before listing it: a detailed description of the item, an appraisal or receipt showing its value, and photographs. The scheduled item then carries its own coverage limit, set at that documented value.

The cover itself is typically broader than the unscheduled kind. Park describes scheduled coverage as protecting against theft at home or away from home, fire and smoke, accidental damage, and — for some items — mysterious disappearance, the loss with no clear event attached. The same source is clear about the edges: wear and tear and intentional damage remain excluded.

Two things scheduling does not change deserve equal billing. First, the deductible point cuts in the buyer's favour but only that far: there usually is no deductible on a scheduled item, per both guides. Second, and less advertised, Western notes that a claim on a scheduled item can still affect your home insurance history. Scheduling removes the limit problem. It does not remove the claims-history consequences of claiming, so the small-loss decision — claim or carry it — survives scheduling intact.

Documentation matters even for items never scheduled. Park notes that proof of value is required within the limits too: without receipts, appraisals or photos, supporting a payout can be difficult. The audit below therefore documents everything, not just the pieces that end up on a floater.

The one-sitting audit: five steps

This procedure needs one policy document, one phone, and about an hour. It produces the numbers the decision above actually requires.

  1. Pull your own special limits first. Find the personal property section of your policy and write down the limit for each category you own: jewellery, watches, furs, art, collectibles, instruments, bicycles and sports equipment, cash.
  2. Inventory by category, with values. List each piece under its category with its best documented value — receipt, appraisal, or a labelled estimate where no document exists. Photograph each piece as you go.
  3. Total each category and compare. Category total minus the special limit is the exposed amount. A negative or small figure means the leave-it option is doing its job; a large figure names the problem precisely.
  4. Gather the paperwork for the pieces you may schedule. For each candidate: a description, an appraisal or receipt, and photos. Western notes an appraisal typically produces a written report with a complete description and an explanation of how the estimate was determined, and that an insurer may ask for it.
  5. Ask for the scheduling premium in writing, then decide with the test. Item value above the normal payout, weighed against the quoted premium and the claims-history point above. Schedule, raise the question of the limit, or consciously carry the gap — each is a decision; drifting is not.

Then set the maintenance date. Appraisals age. Park advises updating them every 3 to 5 years, and gives the cost of skipping it: a ring scheduled ten years ago at $8,000 that is now worth $15,000 remains insured at the old figure. The scheduled value is a snapshot, and snapshots of valuable things go stale in one direction more often than the other.

Success looks like this: for every category, a total, the policy's limit beside it, the exposed difference, and a named decision — leave, schedule, or ask — for anything exposed. One page, kept with the policy.

FAQ

Is the special limit per item or per category? Per category, on the evidence of the published examples: Western's example applies one $3,000 limit to a $15,000 jewellery total, and Park advises scheduling when a collection total exceeds the cap even if each piece is below it. Check your own wording — but assume the category reading until your policy proves otherwise.

Does scheduling mean there is no deductible and no downside? Usually no deductible, per both guides — but Western notes a claim can still affect your home insurance history. The limit problem goes away; the decision about whether a small loss is worth claiming does not.

What paperwork does an insurer want for a scheduled item? A detailed description, an appraisal or receipt showing value, and photographs, per Park. For newer items a recent receipt may serve; for older or appreciating pieces, a professional appraisal — refreshed every 3 to 5 years — is the document that holds up.

Which belongings should be totalled first? The categories the guides name: jewellery and watches, furs, artwork, collectibles such as coins, stamps and sports cards, musical instruments, and bicycles and other sports equipment. Cash is covered only to a small limit, so it rarely repays scheduling arithmetic at all.
