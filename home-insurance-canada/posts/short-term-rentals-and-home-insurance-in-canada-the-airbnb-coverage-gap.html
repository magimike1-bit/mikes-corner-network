Short-term rentals and home insurance in Canada: the Airbnb coverage gap

Rentals · 7 min read

By Penny Hughes · Published Oct 31, 2026 · Last updated Oct 31, 2026

General information only. This guide explains, in general terms, how short-term rentals interact with a Canadian home insurance policy. It is not insurance advice, it does not recommend any insurer, platform, or product, and it is not written by a broker or agent. Insurers and platforms set their own rules, and those rules differ and change — confirm anything that matters with your own insurer or broker, and treat your policy documents and the platform's current terms as the final word.

The trap: a US$3 million number that is not your policy

The number most hosts rely on is the one in the platform's marketing: AirCover for Hosts includes US$3 million in host damage protection and US$1 million in host liability insurance, and Airbnb publishes those limits in US dollars. The trap is treating that package as a policy you hold. Airbnb's own help pages state the opposite, in plain words: AirCover protects you while you are hosting an Airbnb stay, and it is not a substitute for personal insurance. Airbnb also directs hosts to talk to their own insurer about how, or if, their personal policy overlaps with AirCover at all.

That one sentence reframes the whole question. The platform program sits on top of your insurance arrangements; it does not replace them. A host whose own policy was never told about the guests has a gap underneath the US$3 million figure — and the gap, not the figure, is what decides a bad night.

What your home policy does the night a guest checks in

Start with the policy already in the filing cabinet. Standard home insurance policies in Canada do not cover short-term rentals: insurers classify home sharing through platforms such as Airbnb and VRBO as a commercial activity, and a residential policy is not priced for commercial use. Some policies include limited home-sharing endorsements; most do not.

The consequence of hosting first and disclosing later is the severe part. If you host without the right coverage and something goes wrong, your insurer can deny your claim entirely. Broker reporting on the Canadian market puts it more bluntly still: without an endorsement and disclosure, a claim arising during a stay would be denied and the policy would likely be cancelled. A cancelled home policy is a problem that outlasts any single booking — it follows the household into every future application.

So the exposure is not "reduced coverage while guests are in the house." It is the whole policy, including losses that have nothing to do with the guest, resting on an occupancy the insurer never agreed to.

AirCover's real shape: what it pays, what it refuses

Scoped accurately, AirCover is genuinely useful — for the slice it covers. Per Airbnb's own description, the package includes guest identity verification, reservation screening, the US$3 million host damage protection, the US$1 million host liability insurance, and a 24-hour safety line. Host damage protection reimburses a host for certain damage a guest causes to the home or belongings when the guest does not pay for the repairs, and it covers the cost of specialised cleaning in certain cases, such as removing stains left by guests or their invitees, pet accidents, or smoke odour.

The refusals are where hosts actually live. Published breakdowns of the platform programs list what host protection does not include: intentional damage by guests, pet damage, many types of water damage, and theft of cash and valuables. Read that list against a real hosting year. A guest who deliberately wrecks a room, a dog that destroys a door frame, a slow leak discovered after checkout — each sits closer to the exclusion list than to the headline number.

Coverage written for the rental activity itself is shaped differently. Good short-term rental insurance, per the same breakdowns, covers guest property damage — accidental and intentional — theft by renters, liability, and lost rental income, and sometimes lock replacement and utility spikes. The distance between those two lists is the coverage gap in the title, itemised.

None of the three rows substitutes for another. The first row is the hazard, the second is a platform safety net with named holes, and the third is the only layer that is a contract between you and an insurer about rental use.

The endorsement market: three price shapes

Guides quote endorsement pricing as scattered fragments. Laid side by side — checked Oct 25, 2026, against Canadian broker and comparison-site reporting — the fragments form three distinct shapes:

  • A short block of days. Providers offer 31 to 90 days of short-term rental endorsement coverage for about $75. This is the shape aimed at the occasional host: a month or two of bookings across a year, bought as one block.
  • An annual add-on. Adding an endorsement can cost up to $800 a year, depending on the carrier. Same mechanism, longer commitment — the price of a household that hosts regularly but still lives in the home.
  • A cap, not a price. Some insurers cap whole-property rentals at 90 or 180 days per year. Renting a single room does not count toward that limit — a distinction that matters, because the host renting a spare room while living in the home is a different risk, in these insurers' framing, from the host handing over the whole property.

Two worked examples show how the shapes sort real hosts. A household planning roughly 45 booked nights across the summer sits inside the 31-to-90-day block — the about-$75 shape, if its insurer offers it. A household handing over the whole property for 200 nights is past even the 180-day cap shape: no endorsement fragment above fits it, and its conversation with an insurer is about a standalone rental or commercial policy, not an add-on. Neither example is a quote. Both are the arithmetic of matching nights hosted to the band an insurer will actually sell.

Decide by nights hosted

The decision framework is a count, done honestly, before the first listing goes live:

  • Count whole-property nights and single-room nights separately. The published caps treat them differently — single-room hosting, in the reporting above, does not consume the whole-property cap.
  • Under a short block of nights: ask your insurer, in writing, whether a home-sharing endorsement or a 31-to-90-day block covers your pattern — and accept that some policies include a limited version of this and most do not, so the answer may be no.
  • Regular hosting within an annual cap: price the annual endorsement (up to $800 a year in the reporting) against the rental income, as a cost of the activity, not an afterthought.
  • Past the caps, or a property you do not live in: the endorsement market is the wrong shelf. That is standalone rental-policy territory, priced as the commercial activity insurers already consider it to be.

When it is not worth it: a handful of nights at modest nightly rates can earn less than the endorsement, the cleaning, and the wear combined — and hosting undisclosed to save the endorsement cost risks the entire home policy for that handful of nights. The saving that survives contact with a real year is the disclosed one.

A licence is permission to host, not coverage

One confusion to clear before the checklist: municipal licensing, registration, strata or condo rules, and insurance are separate layers, and clearing one says nothing about the others. A host can hold every permission a city requires and still be undisclosed to their insurer. Check each layer on its own terms — your municipality's current rules for whether and how you may host, your strata or condo corporation's rules if they apply, and your insurer's answer about the rental use itself. The insurance layer is the only one that pays after a loss, and it is the one hosts most often assume someone else has handled.

The disclose-in-writing checklist, before your first booking

  1. Count your nights. Whole-property nights and single-room nights in separate columns, using the framework above. This count drives every conversation that follows.
  2. Disclose to your insurer in writing, before listing. Name the platform, the pattern of nights, and whether guests get the whole property or a room. Keep the insurer's reply with your policy documents — a remembered phone call is not a record.
  3. Buy the layer that matches the count. A day-band endorsement, an annual endorsement, or a standalone policy. Confirm the start date covers the first booked night, not the renewal after it.
  4. Read AirCover's current terms yourself. Check the exclusions against your property — pets, water, valuables — and decide what you will self-insure, remove from the property, or cover through your own policy.
  5. Clear the non-insurance layers separately. Municipal licence or registration and strata or condo permission, each confirmed on its own paperwork.

What success looks like: before the first guest arrives, you hold a written answer from your insurer naming the rental use and the endorsement or policy that covers it, a night count that fits inside that coverage, and a copy of the platform's current exclusions you have actually read. Anything less is a hope, not a position.

The short version

  • Airbnb states AirCover is not a substitute for personal insurance. It is a claims program — US$3M damage protection, US$1M liability, limits published in US dollars — layered over your own arrangements.
  • Standard Canadian home policies treat home sharing as a commercial activity. Hosting undisclosed can mean a denied claim and a likely cancelled policy.
  • Published AirCover exclusions include intentional damage, pet damage, many types of water damage, and theft of cash and valuables. Coverage written for rentals is the layer that addresses guest damage, theft by renters, liability, and lost rental income.
  • Endorsement pricing comes in shapes: about $75 for a 31-to-90-day block, up to $800 a year for an annual add-on, and whole-property caps of 90 or 180 days at some insurers — single-room hosting does not count toward those caps in the reporting.
  • Decide by nights hosted, disclose in writing before the first booking, and treat licences and strata rules as separate layers that never substitute for insurance.
