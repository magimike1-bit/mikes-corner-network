Entryway shelving and shoe storage, sized by the numbers

Small spaces · 7 min read

The entryway is the smallest storage problem in the house and the most visible one: every shoe, coat, bag, and set of keys in the household passes through a few square feet of floor, twice a day, often in a hurry. Entryway shelving fails when it is planned as furniture — a nice bench, a nice rack — instead of as a traffic system with dimensions. The published planning guidance is unusually specific about those dimensions, and this guide builds on it: seat height, cubby sizes by footwear type, bench width per person, and the tape-on-the-floor test that tells you whether a plan fits your actual doorway before you spend anything. It also covers the choice most entryway guides skip: which parts of the system should be open shelving, which should be a bench with cubbies, and which should be hooks — because each does a different job in the traffic flow.

The dimensions that decide whether it works

DTYStore's planning guide for mudroom benches with cubbies gives the full sizing set, and it is worth quoting as a set because the numbers interact. Seat height: 17 to 19 inches from floor to top, for comfortable sitting. Seat depth: 14 to 18 inches — adequate support without crowding the walkway. Cubby width: 8 to 12 inches per opening for adult footwear, wider for bulky items. Cubby height: 6 to 8 inches for low shoes, 10 to 12 inches for high-tops or ankle boots, and a tall section of 14 to 18 inches for boots. Cubby depth: 12 to 16 inches accommodates most shoes while keeping the bench compact. And overall length: allocate 18 to 24 inches of bench width per person if each person gets a cubby column. A four-person household therefore needs a bench in the 72-to-96-inch range to give everyone a column — or a honest decision about whose shoes live elsewhere, made on purpose instead of by overflow.

The same guide's final step is the one to actually do first: entryways vary, so mock up the footprint with painter's tape to confirm door swing, walkway clearance, and access to adjacent storage, and test the seat height with a chair at a similar elevation before committing. This Old House's mudroom cabinet build shows what those numbers become in a real project: its storage cabinet pairs a bench base roughly 48 inches wide with a tall upper section about 66 inches high, with shelves around 10 to 14 inches deep depending on the section — a worked example of the bench-plus-tower layout the dimensions above describe, sized for a compact entry rather than a full mudroom.

DTYStore's case for the bench-with-cubbies format is worth stating because it explains the layout: the seat offers a stable spot to tie shoes, the cubbies separate items by person or purpose, and the result is less floor mess, fewer misplaced items, and a calmer daily routine — and in an open-plan home the bench also acts as a visual anchor that defines the drop zone without building walls, while containing the dirt, water, and grit that would otherwise spread across the floor. Assigning cubbies by person, not by shoe type, is the detail that makes it self-maintaining: a person can see their own column is full, which is information; a shared shoe pile tells nobody anything.

Open shelves, cubbies, or a cabinet: choosing the form

  1. Open cubbies — the default for daily rotation. Shoes are visible, air circulates, and a wet pair dries instead of sealing in its own weather. Best where the entry is a working mudroom or a family door.
  2. Open flat shelves — better than cubbies for households whose footwear varies wildly in height, because a shelf has no fixed opening to defeat. Worse for assignment-by-person unless you label or basket by column.
  3. A closed cabinet or bench with doors — the right answer for a formal front entry guests use, where the system's job includes disappearing. It hides the same capacity; it just asks for the extra second of opening a door, twice a day, forever — be honest about whether your household will pay it.
  4. The bench-plus-tower — bench and cubbies below, hooks and a high shelf in a tall section beside or above, as in the This Old House build. Choose this when coats and bags are half the problem, which in most households they are.

Whichever form you choose, anchor anything tall to the wall studs — the This Old House build attaches its assembly to wall studs with 2-inch screws, and a loaded tower beside a door gets leaned on, climbed by toddlers, and caught by swinging bags. Freestanding tall units in an entryway are furniture accidents on a timer.

Install order: tape, test, then build or buy

  1. Tape the footprint on the floor, including the door's full swing, and live with the tape for two days of real comings and goings.
  2. Count the actual daily shoes — the pairs worn this week, per person — and size the cubby run to that count, not to the collection.
  3. Test the seat height with a chair at 17–19 inches and the tallest and smallest household members both.
  4. Place the tall boot section at the door end and the daily low-shoe cubbies under the seat, so wet boots cross the least floor.
  5. Fix tall pieces to studs, level the bench, and only then move shoes in — two pairs per person, with the overflow evicted to closets the same day, not "soon."

What success looks like: at the busiest departure of the week, every person can sit, find both of today's shoes in their own column, and leave through a doorway with no shoes on the floor — and the system still looks like that a month later, because the capacity was sized to the real rotation instead of the hopeful one.

FAQ

How deep does a shoe cubby really need to be? DTYStore's planning range is 12 to 16 inches, which covers most adult shoes; measure your household's largest daily pair sole-to-heel and size to it — a cubby an inch too shallow exiles exactly the pair worn most.

What if the entryway is too small for a bench? Drop the bench, keep the system: hooks for coats, one open shelf run at shoe height, and a boot tray for the wet pair. The three-jobs division above works without the seat; sitting is the most expendable job in a tight entry.

Should kids get lower hooks? Yes — a hook a child cannot reach is a coat on the floor with extra steps. Put one hook per child at their shoulder height on the tower's side, and let the high shelf hold the out-of-season overflow instead.
