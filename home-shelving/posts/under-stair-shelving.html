Under-stair shelving: zoning a sloped space so everything stays reachable

Small spaces · 7 min read

The space under a staircase is the only storage in the house shaped like a triangle, and it is usually treated like a rectangular cupboard — which is why it becomes the place where the vacuum, coats, shoes, and bags go to disappear. The slope is not the problem. The problem is planning the space as if every part of it were equally reachable, when the tall end and the low end are good for completely different things.

Most guides for this space are showroom pages for bespoke fitted furniture, and their measuring advice stops at a single floor width for a quote. Fitted units are a good option — but the planning underneath them matters either way, and a simple set of board shelves follows the same logic. This guide starts with the zoning, then covers measuring, then the choice between open shelves, pull-out drawers, and closed cupboards.

The shape decides: zone the slope first

Divide the under-stair triangle into three bands by how you can use them. The tall zone, near the open end where headroom is greatest, is the only part you can step into or reach across comfortably — it takes the everyday items: shoes in daily rotation, coats, the vacuum, bags. The middle zone works for shelving you reach into from the side: household supplies, cleaning products, board games, seasonal items used a few times a year. The low zone, where the slope meets the floor, fits nothing you need to reach by hand — anything stored there has to come out toward you, in a drawer, a box on castors, or a pull-out unit, or it will effectively be stored forever.

This zoning is what the fitted-storage designers are doing when they build separate sections for shoes, coats, cleaning products, and household essentials instead of one open void — the sections follow the shape of the staircase, and each one is matched to how often its contents are needed. Wider under-stair areas follow the same logic further: with enough width at the tall end, the space can hold a small desk or reading nook, because that is the one band with the headroom to sit or stand in.

Measure the triangle: a numbered procedure

  1. Measure the width of the under-stair space across the floor. This is the starting measurement the fitted companies ask for too — take it to the nearest centimetre, skirting board to skirting board.
  2. If your staircase turns at the bottom, measure the usable width only up to the edge of the stairs, and treat the turn wedge as low-zone storage at best.
  3. If the space already has a cupboard door you are keeping, exclude the door and its swing from the measurement, and check the swing clears whatever you plan to place in front of the opening.
  4. Measure the headroom at three points — open end, middle, and where the slope gets low — and write the three heights on a sketch of the triangle. These three numbers define your zones.
  5. Note anything that must stay accessible: utility meters, stopcocks, consumer units, and access panels. Fitted installers plan around most water, gas, and electricity meters rather than burying them, and your plan must do the same.
  6. List what will actually live in the space, tallest and bulkiest first, and assign each item a zone before choosing any shelving.

What success looks like: a one-page sketch with the floor width, three headroom heights, every meter and panel marked as keep-clear, and each stored item assigned to a zone — detailed enough that a shelf placed from it cannot block a meter or strand an everyday item in the low zone.

The published advice converges on a mix rather than a single format: open shelving for items that display well, such as books and decor, closed storage for clutter, and tall pull-out drawers for the awkward long items — umbrellas, scarves, hats — that fit nowhere else. Fitted ranges build the same mix in: pigeon-hole shelving for small items, pull-out hanging rails, boot racks, and double hanging space, all made to measure to the exact contours of the space. The decision framework is access, not appearance: if you can reach it from the side, shelve it; if you can only reach it by crawling, pull it out or do not store it there; if it should not be seen, close it.

The DIY board-shelf build, checked

For the tall and middle zones, plain board shelving is a proven low-cost build. A published DIY under-stair project used boards 12 inches wide for the shelves — or several narrower boards adding up to the same depth — cut 1/4 inch shorter than the width of the staircase, a small allowance that absorbs walls that are not perfectly straight. Supports were standard shelf brackets or simple wood strips screwed to the wall, with the shelves rested on top and screwed or glued in place; the builder used 1/2-inch plywood for both shelves and supports.

Three checks make that basic method reliable. First, cut to the shortest width you measured, not the average — the 1/4-inch allowance only helps if the board is not already too long at the narrow end. Second, fix the supports into studs or sound masonry wherever the span allows, because a shelf under stairs tends to collect heavier loads than planned: paint tins and toolboxes migrate there. Third, keep the lowest shelf high enough to slide a storage box under it, so the floor-level strip becomes low-zone box storage instead of dead space. Verified against the source build, those numbers — 12-inch boards, 1/4-inch allowance — are the spec; everything else is fitting them to your zones.

What never gets cut, blocked, or buried

Under-stair work has three hard limits. Structure: the staircase itself is not a shelving component. Stair stringers carry their loads through the depth of wood beneath the tread and riser notches, and stair-framing guidance warns that overcutting reduces the effective depth of the member — so never notch, trim, or drill a stringer to fit a shelf or a cupboard. If opening up the space means removing a wall and that wall might be load-bearing, that is a builder's job assessed separately, exactly as the fitted companies treat it, not a shelving weekend task.

Access: meters, stopcocks, and electrical panels stay reachable without moving stored items. A shelf that has to be emptied before the meter can be read will eventually be a shelf in front of a meter nobody reads. Escape and clearance: if the under-stair space opens off a hallway, keep the doorway swing and the passage clear — drawers that pull out into a narrow hall are useful only if someone can stand beside them while they are open.

FAQ

DIY shelves or fitted units? DIY board shelves suit the tall and middle zones and cost little; fitted units earn their price in the low and deep zones, where made-to-measure pull-outs recover space a fixed shelf cannot reach. Custom work gives the precise fit, at a cost that can be out of many budgets — zoning first tells you which zones actually need it.

What should go at the low end? Only things that come out toward you: drawers, wheeled boxes, or rarely used items in labelled boxes at the very back. Never everyday items, and never anything that must stay accessible, like a meter.

How do I measure if the stairs turn? Measure the straight run's floor width up to the edge of the stairs and plan the turn wedge separately as low-zone space.

Can the space hold a desk? Only the wide, tall end has the headroom for it — wider under-stair areas are used this way, but check your three headroom measurements before planning on it.

The short version

Zone the triangle before buying anything: daily items at the tall end, side-reach shelves in the middle, pull-outs or boxes in the low end. Measure the floor width, three headroom points, and every keep-clear meter or panel. Then choose the format by access — open, pull-out, or closed — and leave the staircase structure exactly as you found it.
