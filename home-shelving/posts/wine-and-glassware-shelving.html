Wine and glassware shelving: bottles flat, glasses hanging

Kitchen · 8 min read

55 to 60. That is the temperature range, in degrees Fahrenheit, that wine storage guidance gives for a constant storage temperature, and it is the first number to settle before a single bracket goes on a wall (s2). Above 70 degrees, wine ages two to three times faster than it does at the optimal temperature (s2). A wine and glassware wall is really two storage jobs sharing one patch of wall. Bottles want depth, darkness, and stillness. Glasses want a measured slot and a clear swing path. Plan them together, in that order, and the wall works. Plan them as two separate shopping trips and they fight over the same studs.

The job, the tools, and the honest limits

Difficulty is low to moderate. The work is measuring, levelling, and fixing rails and racks into sound material. Allow an hour to measure and mark, and an hour to mount and load, for a wall holding roughly a case of bottles and a dozen glasses. The hire a pro line is narrow here: a tall, fully loaded wine wall is a heavy wall fixing, and if the mounting points do not land on solid material, that is a carpenter visit, not a longer screw.

Tools and materials: a tape measure, a level, a pencil, a stud finder if the racks are wall mounted, a drill and driver, the racks themselves with their supplied hardware, and a thermometer for the placement check in step one below. Nothing on that list is optional except the stud finder, and it stops being optional the moment a rack carries glass bottles over a floor people walk on.

Bottles: flat or tilted, and deep enough to matter

Orientation comes first. Bottles should sit horizontally or tilted downwards, so the wine stays in contact with the cork and the cork stays moist instead of drying, shrinking, and letting air in to oxidise the wine (s1, s2). Upright is a serving position, not a storage position, for a corked bottle.

Depth comes second, and it is where display racks and storage racks part company. Quality wood wine racks are a full 13.5 inches deep to hold all types of wine bottles (s7). Against that, a popular wall display rail is only 3 inches deep: the Sorbus wall rack measures 25 inches high by 10.25 inches wide by 3 inches deep per rack, holding nine bottles on nine single shelves (s3). Both figures are honest. They describe different jobs. The deep rack supports the bottle along its length. The shallow rail presents the bottle, neck held, label out. The catalogue rule for ring style racks makes the same point: rack depth is measured front to back, and bottle length will be longer than that figure (s6).

Third, accept what a rack is not. A wine rack is short term display and organisation. It has no climate control system, unlike a cabinet or a cooler, so the room has to do the climate work (s1). Bottles that are being kept to age belong in the coolest, darkest, stillest spot available, and the pretty wall near the dining table is for bottles in current rotation.

Glasses: hanging by the base, sized by measurement

Stemware stores upside down, hanging by its base, for two practical reasons. The bowl stays clear of shelf dust, and the glasses stop standing in a crowd where rims chip against each other. The fit is a measurement, not a guess, and the listings publish the numbers that matter. A single Dicunoy row is 13.8 inches long with a 3.5 inch opening to hang a glass, and holds 3 to 4 wine glasses per row (s5). A four row under cabinet unit from FOMANSH measures 44 centimetres deep by 24 centimetres wide by 8 centimetres high, in powder coated metal, with no assembly required (s4). The Knape and Vogt catalogue holder, model ST11.50, is 4 inches wide, 2 inches high, and 11 and 9/16 inches deep, which is the depth the bowl and stem need below the shelf it hangs from (s6).

So measure three things on the largest glass in the set: the base diameter, which has to pass the row opening; the bowl width, which sets how many glasses share a row before bowls touch; and the full height, which has to clear the counter or shelf below with room to lift the glass in and out. A row rated for 3 to 4 glasses (s5) holds 3 large bowls comfortably. Count it as 3 and the glasses stop clinking.

Measure, mount, and load: a numbered procedure

  1. Run the placement check first. Put a thermometer where the wall will go for a day. The spot should sit near a constant 55 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, away from direct sunlight, because intense light breaks down the wine and ages it artificially, and away from doors and vibrating appliances, because vibration shakes the sediment in the bottle (s1, s2). Fail any one of the three and choose a different wall.
  2. Measure the glasses. Base diameter against the row opening, bowl width against row length, full height against the drop below the cabinet (s5, s6).
  3. Decide the bottle job. Display in rotation takes the shallow rail; storage takes full depth at 13.5 inches (s3, s7). Mark the rack outline on the wall in tape at the chosen depth.
  4. Find solid fixing. Mark studs or solid material behind every mounting point, level the first rack, and fix it with the hardware supplied (s3).
  5. Mount the glass rows under the cabinet or shelf. Work from the deepest glass outward, leaving the measured clearance below each bowl (s4, s6).
  6. Load one bottle and one glass, then test. Slide each bottle out and back; lift each glass in and out by its stem. Then load the rest, bottles flat or tilted with the cork end down (s1, s7).

What success looks like: every corked bottle lying flat or tilted with wine against the cork, every glass hanging clear of its neighbours and of the surface below, and nothing on the wall that has to be wrestled past another piece to reach.

The mistakes that chip rims and dry corks

Storing corked bottles upright for months dries the cork, and a dry cork shrinks and lets air in (s1, s2). Mounting the display rail on a door wall adds the vibration the storage guidance says to avoid (s1). Buying glass rows by glass count alone, without the base and bowl measurements, is how a rack rated for a dozen glasses ends up holding eight with chipped rims. And keeping the good bottles on a sunlit kitchen wall confuses display with storage: the rack has no climate control, so a bottle kept to age needs the cool, dark room, not the bright one (s1). An opened bottle, for reference, stays fresh three to five days stored chilled or in a dark, cool spot, so the rack is also the wrong home for last night's open red (s2).
