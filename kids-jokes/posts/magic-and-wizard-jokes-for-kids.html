Magic and Wizard Jokes for Kids Ages 9 to 12

🪄 Magic · 7 min read · Updated November 2026

Magic jokes for ages 9 to 12 work best when the wizard is generic, the spells are gentle, and the wordplay does the sparkling. The big lists rarely offer that mix. One collection rounds up over 167 wizard jokes for all ages and still finds room for a wizard carrying a ladder into a bar, pages away from its own kids and families subsection. Another gathers over 140 wizard puns, and its kids section is mostly one-liners built on wand-erful and enchant-mint. A third builds every joke around one famous book franchise, which shuts out readers who have not read it. The set below keeps the wizard nameless and kind, sorts the jokes by pun family, and explains each family before you raid it.

Spells and spelling

Spell is the finest double agent in the wizard word box. It means a magic charm, and it also means saying a word letter by letter. That single overlap powers a whole family: every spelling lesson becomes a magic lesson, and every magic lesson can go politely wrong in a classroom. Read these ones a touch slower than the rest. The laugh often arrives when the listener realises both meanings were standing there the whole time, wearing the same hat.

  1. Why did the young wizard love spelling homework? Every correct word felt like a tiny spell that actually worked.
  2. What did the spell book say to the wizard? “Read me carefully. My best tricks hide between the chapters.”
  3. Why did the wizard whisper his spell in the library? Loud magic is still loud, and the quiet sign applies to everyone.
  4. What do you call a spell that fixes a squeaky door? A hinge enchantment, with silent and deeply satisfying results.
  5. Why did the wizard practise his spell backwards? So he could undo the glitter explosion before dinner, hopefully.
  6. What did the beginner wizard say after his first working spell? “Abracadabra, I did it, and nobody turned into a teapot.”
  7. Why do wizards write their spells in pencil? So a mistake can be rubbed out before it becomes a frog.

Wands and wand waving

A wand is a stick with a public relations team. Wand jokes wave at music conductors, picnic pointers and very fancy dress socks, because a thin stick that points at things shows up in ordinary life more often than in a tower. The family rule is simple: the wand stays cheerful and a little proud of itself, the wizard stays kind, and nobody gets turned into anything. Sparkles are allowed. Showing off is gently teased, never punished.

  1. Why did the wand choose the quiet wizard? It preferred a partner who waved with intention, not just for sparkles.
  2. What do you call a wand that loves music? A magic conductor, keeping the whole orchestra in tune with one wave.
  3. Why did the wizard carry two wands to the picnic? One for magic, and one for pointing at the best sandwich from a safe distance.
  4. What did the wand say on its day off? “No waving, no sparks, just a long nap in a velvet box.”
  5. Why did the young wizard polish her wand before the show? A shiny wand makes even a simple card trick look important.
  6. How does a wand say thank you? It tips its point, sparkles once, and behaves for the rest of the evening.
  7. Why did the wand hide in the sock drawer? It heard dress socks were the fanciest outfit in the whole house.

Potions and cauldrons

Potion jokes bubble up from cooking, and cooking is a scene every reader knows. A cauldron is a soup pot with a dramatic agent. A potion is a recipe where the stirring matters and the labels matter even more. That is why this family lands so well at family tables: grandparents who have never waved a wand still know exactly what happens when a pot bubbles over at the wrong moment. Keep the potions kind ones, for courage, cheer and warm drinks.

  1. What does a wizard put in a kindness potion? Warm milk, honey, and instructions to share it while it is still warm.
  2. Why did the cauldron get promoted? It handled pressure, kept its cool, and never spilled a secret recipe.
  3. What did the potion say to the spoon? “Stir gently. This fizz has feelings too.”
  4. Why did the wizard label every potion bottle twice? The first label fell off during the great bubbling incident of Tuesday.
  5. What is a cauldron’s favourite dance? The bubble shuffle, best performed on a very steady floor.
  6. Why did the young wizard bring a notebook to potions class? Great potions start with careful lists, not lucky guesses.

Brooms, hats and robes

Wizard gear is household gear in a costume. A broom sweeps a kitchen long before it flies anywhere, a tall hat is a storage problem with a point, and a robe is a dressing gown that has read more books. This family suits readers who like their magic practical. The broom still has to do its chores, the hat still falls off in a strong wind, and the robe pockets are mostly full of snacks and spare pencils.

  1. Why did the wizard’s broom get a weekend off? It had swept the tower, the stairs and the competition, in that order.
  2. What did the tall hat say to the wizard? “I am not lost. I am stored vertically, as is traditional.”
  3. Why did the young wizard sew extra pockets into her robe? One for the wand, one for a pencil, and one reserved for emergency biscuits.
  4. What do you call a broom that tells jokes? A sweep talker, available for floors and parties.
  5. Why did the wizard hang his hat by the door? So every entrance could be tall, pointy and slightly dramatic.

Knock-knocks and one-liners

Magic words were born for knock-knock jokes, because abracadabra already sounds like somebody arriving with news. Wanda hides inside wonderful, spell hides inside an instruction, and both tricks reward a reader who says the answer out loud first. Keep these short ones for hallways, bus queues and the last five minutes before dinner, where a joke has about ten seconds to appear, sparkle and vanish politely.

  1. Knock, knock. Who’s there? Wanda. Wanda who? Wanda-ful to see you, the show starts in five minutes.
  2. Knock, knock. Who’s there? Abra. Abra who? Abra-cadabra, the door is open and the rabbit is impressed.
  3. Knock, knock. Who’s there? Spell. Spell who? Spell it out slowly, the wizard is still learning long magic words.
  4. Why do wizards make patient teachers? They know every good trick takes practise, a pencil, and a second try.
  5. What did the rabbit say after the hat trick? “Lovely home, great view, happy to visit again next show.”

Which magic joke for which moment

  • Spelling homework on the table → a spell joke. The double meaning is already sitting in the exercise book.
  • A talent show or music practice → a wand joke. Conductors and wands wave in exactly the same hopeful way.
  • Family dinner → a potion joke. Everybody at the table has watched a pot bubble at the wrong moment.
  • A card for a book-loving cousin → a knock-knock. It fits in two lines and invites the reader to say half of it aloud.
  • Only ten seconds → a one-liner. Skip the setup-heavy jokes; a short line survives a noisy hallway.

Write your own magic joke in three steps

  1. Pick a family. Spell, wand, potion, or broom, one family per joke. Two families in one punchline muddle the magic.
  2. List three double-meaning words. Spell (letters, and a charm), stir (to mix, and a fuss), sweep (to brush, and to win everything). Pick the pair that surprises you most.
  3. Put a wizard in an everyday scene. A library, a picnic, a rainy bus stop. Write the plain sentence first, then let the second meaning deliver the punchline last.

What success looks like: a listener tries the joke on someone else before the day is out. A magic joke that travels on its own has cast its best spell.
