After you apply: the underwriting timeline, stage by stage

Under the hood · 6 min read

A quote is not coverage, and an application is not a policy. Between submitting a life insurance application in Canada and actually being covered, there is a defined sequence — application, underwriting review, decision, policy issue, delivery, and a short window to change your mind — and most surprises happen because one of those stages was misunderstood. This guide walks the sequence in order, explains what the insurer is doing at each stage, and flags the two moments that matter most: when coverage actually starts, and the free-look period after the policy arrives.

Stage 1: The application itself

The application collects the information the underwriter will rely on: health history, lifestyle and habits, family medical background, medications, occupation, and the amount and type of coverage requested, along with existing coverage and any other pending applications. Accuracy here is not a formality. The answers become part of the contract's foundation, and material omissions discovered later — especially inside the contestability period — can support a denied claim. Answer what is asked, completely, and keep a copy of everything submitted.

Stage 2: Underwriting review

Once the application is submitted, underwriters review it to assess the applicant's risk level, and that assessment is what sets the final premium — which is why a quote, being an estimate made before this review, can move. Depending on the amount of coverage, the applicant's age, and the answers given, the review may include:

  • A paramedical exam — typically height, weight, blood pressure, and blood and urine samples, often conducted by a nurse arranged through the insurer.
  • A telephone interview to clarify answers on the application.
  • Medical records requested from the applicant's physician, and sometimes follow-up tests, a driving record, or evidence of employment.

A request for more information is routine, not a warning sign — it is how the insurer completes the picture it needs to price the risk. Complex health factors, high coverage amounts, and longer terms typically lengthen this stage.

Stage 3: The decision — and the paths that lead to it

Not every application travels the same path. In broad terms, Canadian applications move along a spectrum from the most questions to the fewest, with the price per dollar of coverage generally rising as the questions disappear:

The decision itself lands in one of a few forms: approved as applied for, approved with a rating (a higher premium reflecting the assessed risk), approved with an exclusion, postponed pending more information or a change in circumstances, or declined. A rating or exclusion is not the end of the road — another insurer may assess the same facts differently — but it should be read carefully before acceptance, not discovered in the delivered contract.

Stage 4: Policy issue — when coverage actually starts

Approval is not coverage. As the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada frames it, the applicant applies, meets the conditions to be approved, and then the insurer issues the policy. Coverage typically takes effect once the offer is accepted and the first premium is paid — the insurer sets a deadline for both. Some insurers offer temporary or conditional coverage from the application date under stated conditions; whether yours does is a question worth asking explicitly, because the gap between applying and issue is exactly the window in which people most often assume they are covered when they are not.

Stage 5: Delivery and the free look

When the policy is delivered, a free-look period begins — a right to cancel within a set period, usually 10 days, and receive the premiums back. This is the one moment when walking away costs nothing, and it exists so the contract can be read properly before the commitment hardens. Three checks make the free look worthwhile:

  1. The benefit amount and term match what was applied for — not a reduced amount or shorter term substituted during underwriting.
  2. No rating or exclusion was added quietly — compare the delivered premium against the quote and read any exclusion wording in full.
  3. The beneficiary is named exactly as intended — full legal name, correct person, and a contingent beneficiary recorded.

What success looks like: by the end of the free-look period, the delivered contract has been read at least once, the premium, amount, term, exclusions, and beneficiary all match the application, and the policy documents are stored where the beneficiary could find them.

How long does the whole sequence take?

Timelines vary widely and any single number would mislead. Insurers describe the traditional process as taking from a few days to several weeks; accelerated paths can approve eligible applicants the same day, while standard underwriting involving exams and physician records can run six to eight weeks. The variables are the ones already named: the amount requested, the complexity of the health picture, how quickly exams are booked and records returned, and how completely the application was answered the first time. The practical response to a slow file is not anxiety but a single question to the advisor or insurer: which stage is the file in, and what is it waiting on?

Questions people commonly ask

Does the premium quoted at application stay fixed? The quote is an estimate. Underwriting sets the final premium, and for term policies the premium then stays fixed for the term; renewal at the end of the term is priced higher, reflecting the older age.

Can an application be withdrawn? Yes, at any point before acceptance — and during the free-look period after delivery, with premiums returned.

What if the decision is a decline? Ask which facts drove it, correct any that are wrong, and get advice before reapplying — applications and their outcomes can be visible to other insurers through industry databases, so a rushed second application rarely helps.

Keeping the file moving: what applicants control

Applicants cannot set the underwriter's pace, but they control most of the common delays. Book the paramedical exam promptly when the examiner calls rather than negotiating for a perfect week; return signed forms the day they arrive; tell your physician's office that records have been requested, so the request is expected rather than filed as junk; and answer follow-up questions completely the first time, since each partial answer generates another round trip. Keep a simple log — date applied, exam date, each request received and answered — so that a status question to the advisor is specific: not "how is it going?" but "the file has waited on records since this date; which office are they with?" Specific questions get specific answers, and a file that is visibly tracked rarely becomes the file everyone assumed someone else was watching.
