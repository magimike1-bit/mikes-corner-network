Life insurance beneficiary designations, explained

Fine print, translated · 8 min read · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

A beneficiary designation looks like a single line on a form, but that line does a large job. It names the person, people, or organization who should receive the death benefit when the insured person dies. Understanding that line matters because a policy can be the right amount, bought at the right time, and kept paid for years, yet still send money somewhere the policyholder no longer intended if the designation was never updated. This guide explains the main kinds of designation in Canada, how they differ, what happens with minors and estates, and how to carry out a calm check of a designation already on file. It is general information only, not advice about any one family's estate plan.

What a beneficiary designation actually does

When a beneficiary is named directly on a policy, the insurer pays that beneficiary directly. Sun Life describes two main ways to name beneficiaries in Canada: on the beneficiary form provided by the insurance company, and in a will. The form route is the most common way designations are recorded at application and updated afterwards. A direct payment to a named beneficiary is treated differently from money that flows through an estate. As Sun Life and other insurer guidance explain, naming a beneficiary can help loved ones receive money more quickly and directly, without waiting for the estate process that applies when no direct beneficiary is named.

The contrast is clearest when the estate itself is the beneficiary, or when no beneficiary has been named at all. The University of Waterloo's benefits guidance, which reflects a common plan pattern, explains that if the estate is the beneficiary, the proceeds form part of the estate, are distributed according to the will, and are not accessible until the will has been probated. Desjardins guidance makes a similar point: with no designated beneficiary, or with the estate designated, the benefit is paid to the estate and distributed under the will or, if there is no will, under applicable legal provisions. That route is not automatically wrong. Some people deliberately choose it as part of a wider estate plan, with legal advice. The important point is that it is a different route, with different timing and different exposure, and it should be chosen rather than fallen into by default.

Privacy and timing are part of that difference. Because a directly named beneficiary is paid by the insurer rather than through the estate, the payout does not have to wait for the estate to be settled in the same way. That does not make a designation a secret estate plan or a substitute for a will. A will still governs the estate. A designation governs the policy it is attached to. The two documents need to agree with each other in intention, even though they operate separately, and a change made in one place should prompt a check of the other.

Primary, contingent, revocable, and irrevocable: the four distinctions

Four words explain most designation forms. A primary beneficiary is first in line to receive the benefit. A policy can name more than one primary beneficiary, with shares set out on the form. A contingent beneficiary, sometimes called a secondary beneficiary, receives the benefit if the primary beneficiaries are not living at the time payment is made. Desjardins describes the contingent role in exactly those terms, and university plan guidance gives the familiar example of a spouse as primary and children as contingent. Naming a contingent beneficiary is a simple safeguard against a gap that many people do not consider until it is pointed out: what should happen if the first choice cannot receive the money?

Revocable and irrevocable describe whether the designation can be changed. A revocable designation can generally be changed by the policyholder by submitting the insurer's form, without needing the beneficiary's consent. An irrevocable designation is deliberately harder to move: changing it generally requires the written consent of the irrevocable beneficiary. That lock is sometimes intentional, for example where a designation supports a legal agreement. It should never be selected casually, because the consent requirement also applies to some other changes under a policy, depending on its terms.

Quebec adds a specific rule that deserves attention rather than a footnote. Guidance summarizing the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada position notes that in Quebec a spouse named as beneficiary is considered irrevocable unless the designation is stated to be revocable. Rules outside Quebec vary by province and by policy. Anyone with a Quebec policy, or a spouse designation made years ago, should confirm revocability directly with the insurer and, where the stakes are significant, with a lawyer in that province. A short confirmation now is far easier than a dispute about consent later.

Naming minors, trustees, your estate, or a charity

Children are often the reason a policy exists, which makes the minor-beneficiary rules especially important. A child can be named, but a young beneficiary cannot simply receive and manage a large payment in the same way an adult can. University plan guidance for Ontario explains that if a named beneficiary is under the legal age of 18, proceeds can be held in trust by the province, with money and interest paid when the beneficiary reaches 18, and that naming a trustee is a way to make funds available in a more timely and managed way. Desjardins similarly notes that, in all provinces except Quebec, a trustee can be appointed when designating a minor beneficiary, and that Quebec handles payment to a minor through a tutor, curator, or a valid trust. The exact mechanism is provincial and contractual, so the form and the insurer's instructions matter.

A trustee arrangement should be discussed with the proposed trustee before it is filed, not discovered by that person after a death. The trustee's role, how money may be used for the child's support or education, and how long the arrangement lasts are legal questions. This article does not answer them for a specific family; it flags them so they are asked while there is time to answer them properly.

Other designees are broader than many people expect. Desjardins lists, among others, a married or civil-union spouse, a common-law spouse, a child, a friend, a relative, a charity, and any other individual. A charity designation should use the organization's exact legal name, confirmed with the charity and the insurer, so payment is not delayed by an ambiguous description. An unborn child may also be designated in some cases, subject to verification when the benefit becomes payable, according to the same Desjardins guidance. In every case, precision beats shorthand: full legal names and the relationship help an insurer identify and locate the right people quickly, a best practice Sun Life highlights explicitly.

How to check and change a designation: a numbered procedure

This is a how-to topic, so here is a procedure a policyholder can follow with the insurer. It does not require special expertise, only care and written confirmation.

  1. Find the policy number and the insurer's current beneficiary form, or sign in through the insurer's official channel and locate the designation on record. Do not rely on memory or on an old photocopy alone.
  2. Read the current record literally: primary names, shares, contingent names, and whether any designation is marked irrevocable. Check that any percentage shares add up to the total the form requires.
  3. Compare that record with present circumstances: marriage, divorce, a birth or adoption, a death, a child reaching adulthood, or a charity or trust that has changed its legal name.
  4. Decide what needs to change, including the contingent tier and any trustee for a minor. If an irrevocable beneficiary is involved, expect a written-consent requirement and ask the insurer exactly what form that consent must take.
  5. Complete the new form with full legal names and relationships, sign it as instructed, and submit it to the insurer or, for group coverage, through the employer or plan administrator channel the plan directs.
  6. Wait for written confirmation that the insurer has recorded the change, then file that confirmation with the policy. A completed form kept only at home is not the same as a designation recorded by the insurer.
  7. Tell the relevant people where the policy and confirmation are kept, without assuming a beneficiary needs every policy detail. A beneficiary who does not know a policy exists may struggle to start a claim promptly.

What success looks like: the insurer's recorded designation matches the intended primary and contingent choices, shares total correctly, any trustee for a minor is named as the form requires, revocability is understood, and a dated written confirmation is filed with the policy. If any of those points cannot be confirmed, the check is not finished yet.

Decision framework: matching the designation to the situation

No framework can choose a beneficiary for someone else, but a few questions sort most situations. First, who is this money meant to protect immediately? That answer usually identifies the primary tier. Second, what if that person cannot receive it? That answer belongs in the contingent tier, not as an afterthought. Third, is there a reason payment should go through the estate or a formal trust instead of directly to a person, for example because a beneficiary is a minor or has complex needs? If so, legal advice should shape the wording before the form is filed. Fourth, is there any reason to lock a designation as irrevocable, knowing consent will be needed to change it? If the honest answer is uncertainty, pause and ask rather than locking by default. Finally, when did anyone last look at this form? A designation that predates a marriage, children, or a divorce deserves a fresh look regardless of what the answers were when the policy began.

Questions people commonly ask

Does changing a will automatically change a policy beneficiary? Not safely. A beneficiary can be named in a will in some circumstances, but a later designation and provincial rules affect how documents interact. The reliable approach is to confirm the designation recorded by the insurer and make sure the will and the policy do not point in different directions. Get legal advice where the two documents appear to conflict.

Can percentages be split unevenly? Yes, a form can set different shares for multiple beneficiaries. The practical requirement is clarity: names, relationships, and shares that total as the form requires, so the insurer does not have to guess at an intention.

What if no beneficiary was ever named? Desjardins guidance states the benefit is then paid to the estate and distributed under the will or, without a will, under applicable law. That outcome can be corrected for the future by filing a designation now, while the policyholder is able to do so.

Should a beneficiary be told? There is no single rule in the sources used here, but a beneficiary or executor who knows where to find the insurer and policy information is better placed to start a claim. At minimum, keep the policy and the recorded designation confirmation somewhere a trusted person can find them.
