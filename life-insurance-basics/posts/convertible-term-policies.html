Convertible term life insurance, explained

Fine print, translated · 8 min read · Published October 17, 2026 · Last updated October 17, 2026

Convertibility is the least dramatic line in a term life insurance policy and, for some families, the most valuable one. It is a contractual option to exchange term coverage for permanent coverage later, without new medical evidence. The option costs nothing to hold on many policies, expires on a date most policyholders never write down, and becomes priceless in exactly one situation: health changes before the need for coverage ends. This guide explains the mechanics, verifies the deadlines against insurer documents, works through a partial conversion with real published figures, and sets out when converting beats the alternatives. It is general information only, not advice about any one policy.

What convertible actually means

A convertible term policy can be exchanged, in whole or in part, for a permanent policy from the same insurer during a defined conversion period. PolicyMe, a Canadian insurer, describes the core promise plainly: conversion to permanent coverage later with no medical exam, and it notes that most term policies in Canada are convertible, though the feature should be confirmed with the provider rather than assumed. Canada Life adds the two conditions that govern every conversion: the policy must contain a conversion option, and the request must fall inside the conversion period set out in the contract.

Three clarifications prevent most misunderstandings. First, conversion is an option, never an obligation; holding a convertible policy and never converting it is a perfectly ordinary outcome. Second, conversion is not renewal. Renewal extends term coverage for another term, also typically without a medical exam. Conversion changes the kind of coverage, from expiring to lifelong. Third, the coverage amount does not grow at conversion. The amount converted stays the same; what changes is the premium, which is recalculated at the policyholder’s age on the conversion date. A conversion preserves insurability, not the old price.

What permanent coverage offers in return is duration and, on many plans, value inside the policy. Canada Life describes permanent insurance as guaranteed lifelong coverage while premiums are paid, which can build value the owner can access through a loan or withdrawal, with the caution that accessing that value can reduce the eventual payout. Those features are reasons some people convert. They are also why the new premium is higher: the insurer is now pricing a claim it expects to pay eventually, at an older age, with no fresh health evidence.

One practical check follows from how conversions are actually used. A Canadian broker source advises confirming that the conversion expiry runs past the end of the term itself, because conversions are most often exercised in the final years of a term, when health and needs have had time to change. A 20-year term bought at 50 whose conversion right ends at 65 leaves the last five years, the riskiest ones, without the option.

Partial conversion and the term reset, worked through

Conversion is not all-or-nothing. Part of a term policy can be converted while the rest continues unchanged, and Canada Life documents both a plain partial conversion and a house variant called conversion with reset. The reset feature converts an eligible amount of term coverage into a new permanent policy that itself carries a term insurance benefit. At least 40 percent of the converted amount must be permanent base coverage, and the new term benefit is priced at the policyholder’s age at conversion, without underwriting.

Canada Life’s own worked example makes the arithmetic concrete. A policyholder converts $500,000 of a $1,000,000 term policy using the reset feature. Because he converts $500,000, at least $200,000, which is 40 percent, must go to the new policy’s permanent base coverage. He ends up with $200,000 of permanent base coverage plus a $300,000 converted term benefit on the new policy, and he may keep the remaining $500,000 on the original term policy, convert it later, or cancel it. The converted term benefit must run at least as long as the original policy’s term length. In a second example, a full conversion at age 54 resets the term benefit’s renewal date to age 64, instead of age 55 under the original policy, which is the “reset” the feature is named for.

The strategic point of a partial conversion is sizing. Permanent coverage is expensive per dollar, and most households need only a modest amount of it for life: final expenses, a small legacy, or support for a lifelong dependent. Converting $100,000 or $250,000 of a large term policy, rather than the whole amount, matches the permanent layer to the permanent need and keeps the rest of the coverage at term prices for as long as it is still required. Broker illustrations make the same point with health changes: after a heart attack at 55, a policyholder in one published example converts $250,000 to permanent coverage with a signature, at standard risk-class premiums, because the conversion right was already in the contract.

What carries over, and what is repriced

The Canada Life My Term advisor guide specifies three carry-overs that consumer explainers almost never mention. Risk class: conversion pricing uses the insured’s original risk class, so a health decline after issue does not re-rate the converted policy, though premiums are based on the age nearest the new policy date. Riders: riders and benefits on the original term policy can be carried to the new policy where an equivalent rider exists, at the original risk class. The guide lists guaranteed insurability, accidental death benefit, waiver of premium, and child life insurance riders as carryable, and excludes the business growth protection rider. The two clocks: the suicide clause and the incontestability clause continue to be based on the original policy, provided there has been no increase in coverage, no reinstatement, and no change in risk class. A converted policy does not restart those clocks from zero.

What does not carry over is the term premium. The new premium reflects permanent coverage at the attained age, and it will be materially higher than the term premium it replaces. That is not a defect in the feature; it is the price of exchanging expiring coverage for lifelong coverage without proving health. The comparison that matters is not “old premium versus new premium” but “new premium versus the cost, or impossibility, of buying equivalent permanent coverage on the open market with today’s health.”

Convert, renew, or buy new: a decision framework

When a term nears its end and coverage is still needed, three doors are usually open. Buy new is normally cheapest for someone still in good health, because a fresh application re-earns standard rates and allows shopping across insurers; PolicyMe makes the same point, noting that a healthy policyholder at expiry will likely do better with new quotes than with conversion. Renew fits a need that is still temporary but longer than expected, such as a mortgage that will be gone in ten more years; it requires no medical exam, but renewal premiums rise with age and the coverage still expires. Convert fits a need that has become lifelong, or a health change that makes new coverage expensive or unavailable: a lifelong dependent, final-expense and estate costs that will exist whenever death occurs, or a diagnosis during the term. Two questions sort most cases. Is the remaining need permanent or temporary? And could this person still qualify for competitively priced new coverage today? Permanent need plus a difficult new application points to conversion; temporary need plus good health points to renewal or a new term policy.

One deadline discipline applies regardless of the answer: diarize the conversion expiry date itself, not the term expiry date, because the two are different dates in the same contract, and the option that lapses first is the one that cannot be recovered.

Questions people commonly ask

Does converting require a medical exam or health questions? No. The verified insurer documents above describe conversion without medical evidence of insurability and without new health questions. That is the entire purpose of the feature.

Can part of a policy be converted and the rest kept? Yes. Partial conversion is documented by insurers, and the unconverted balance can continue as term coverage, be converted later inside the window, or be cancelled.

Will the premium stay the same after conversion? No. It is recalculated for permanent coverage at the age at conversion, using the original risk class. Expect it to be higher than the term premium, and lower than what the same person might face, if they could buy at all, after a serious health change without a conversion right.

What happens if the conversion deadline is missed? The option is gone. The remaining choices are renewal if the policy provides it, a new application with full underwriting, or letting the coverage end. There is no general right to convert late, which is why the date belongs in a calendar, not just in the contract.
