The free-look period in Canada: cancelling a new policy without penalty

Fine print, translated · By Penny Hughes · Published October 28, 2026 · Last updated October 28, 2026

Many buyers count the free-look period from the wrong day. They count from the sales meeting, the application, or the first payment — and by that count the window can look open after it has already closed. In the provincial rules retrieved for this article, the clock runs from a different moment: the day the policy is received. Get that starting point wrong and the right to cancel without penalty, with the whole premium back, can expire while the paperwork is still sitting on the kitchen table.

What the BC and Alberta rules actually say

British Columbia's Insurance Regulation, section 9, gives an insured person a cooling-off right: the life insurance contract may be rescinded by notifying the insurer within 10 days after receiving the policy, or within a longer period if the contract itself specifies one. On rescission, the insurer must refund the whole premium paid.

Alberta's Fair Practices Regulation, section 5, provides a parallel 10-day rescission right with a full premium refund. That Alberta rule has been tested in court for replacement policies, covered in the next sections.

Two limits matter. First, BC's cooling-off right does not cover every contract: the regulation excludes travel insurance under 190 days, annuity contracts, and variable insurance contracts. Second, the sources retrieved for this article cover BC and Alberta in this detail. Regulation text for the other provinces was not retrieved, so do not assume every province words the right the same way — read the rescission wording in the policy that arrived, and check your own province's rule.

Why published day counts disagree

One Canadian broker describes the free-look period as 10 to 14 days. That conflicts with the 10-day figure in the BC and Alberta regulations above. The regulations resolve the conflict in one direction: 10 days is the floor in those rules, and a longer window exists only where the contract itself specifies more. A general explainer from Dundas Life puts the free-look period at usually 10 days.

So treat any range quoted in marketing as a prompt to check, not as the deadline. The deadline that governs a particular policy is the one in that policy and in the applicable provincial regulation.

How to cancel inside the window

This is a followable procedure, built from the rescission right described above. It suits a pure explainer topic turned practical: the right is only useful if it is exercised in time and the exercise can be shown.

  1. Write down the date the policy was received. That date — not the application date — starts the 10-day count in the BC and Alberta rules.
  2. Read the policy during the window, not after it. Check that the benefit amount and the term match what was intended, look for exclusions or ratings that were not expected, and confirm the beneficiary is named exactly as intended.
  3. Check the contract for a longer period. If the contract specifies more than 10 days, that longer period is the one to work to; if it does not, work to 10 days from receipt.
  4. Notify the insurer within the window. Put the notice in writing so there is a record of what was sent and when, and keep a copy with proof of the sending date.
  5. Confirm the refund. On a valid rescission under the BC rule, the insurer must refund the whole premium paid; keep the policy and the notice together until the refund arrives.

What success looks like: a dated receipt note, a written notice sent inside the window, proof of that date kept on file, and the full premium refunded — with no gap in coverage the household still needs.

The replacement trap: two policies, two clocks

Replacing an old policy with a new one creates the riskiest version of this decision. Alberta's rescission rule was litigated on exactly this point: the Alberta Court of Appeal held that the rescission right applies to replacement policies, and that the insurer bears the risk during the 10 days. The Supreme Court of Canada denied leave to appeal that ruling.

The practical sequencing follows from that. A new policy has its own free-look clock, starting when the new policy is received. Cancelling the old policy before the new one has been received, read, and confirmed leaves a household uninsured if something in the new policy — an exclusion, a rating, a wrong beneficiary — turns out to be a reason to rescind it. Keep the old policy in force until the new one is past the point where rescission is still being considered.

After the window closes

Once the free-look period ends, cancelling looks very different. For term insurance, cancelling after the window returns nothing, because term insurance has no cash value. There is no premium refund to recover and no value built up inside the policy to take out.

That changes the decision framework. Inside the window, the question is simple: does this policy, as written, match what was intended? Outside the window, cancelling a term policy is a decision to end coverage with nothing back, so the useful questions shift — is replacement coverage already in force, is the amount or term wrong enough to justify starting again, and what would a new application cost at a new age and health status? Those later questions need a licensed professional in your province; the free-look questions above are the ones a buyer can settle alone, in 10 days, from the mailbox.
