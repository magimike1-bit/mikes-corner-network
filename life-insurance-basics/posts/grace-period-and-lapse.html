Life insurance grace period, lapse, and reinstatement, explained

Under the hood · 8 min read · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

A missed life insurance premium feels alarming, and the language around it — grace period, lapse, reinstatement — can make a simple timeline sound like a legal maze. The reassuring starting point is that a single missed due date does not usually end coverage that same day. Most individual policies include a grace period during which coverage stays in force while the overdue premium can still be paid. The serious point comes afterwards: if the grace period expires unpaid, the policy can lapse, coverage ends, and a death after lapse will not produce a payment under that policy. This guide lays out that timeline in Canada, explains how permanent policies with cash value behave differently, and gives a numbered recovery procedure for anyone who discovers a payment has been missed. It is general information only; the policy contract and provincial law govern any specific case.

A missed payment is not an instant lapse

A grace period is the window after a premium due date in which a late payment can still keep the policy in good standing. During that window the policy remains in force. Ontario guidance states that a 30-day minimum grace period is required by law for most individual life insurance policies in Ontario, under provincial insurance law overseen by the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario. Other provinces and individual contracts can vary, and group life policies may have different terms, so the number that matters is the one in the actual contract, read alongside provincial rules. Treat 30 days as a common reference point, never as a personal guarantee.

Two practical cautions follow. First, insurers are not legally obligated to remind a policyholder that a policy is about to lapse, even though many send courtesy notices. A notice that never arrives, goes to an old address, or lands in an unread inbox does not extend the window. Second, the reasons payments are missed are usually mundane rather than dramatic: a bank account changed, a card expired, an address was not updated, or a difficult month pushed a premium down the list. Lapse guides list exactly those ordinary causes. The system does not distinguish between a careless miss and an accidental one once the window closes, which is why prevention habits matter as much as recovery steps.

Reinstatement deserves careful reading because published windows differ widely. Ontario-focused guidance notes that reinstatement may be possible, with conditions that can include a time limit, updated health information or a medical exam, payment of all missed premiums possibly with interest, and an application process, and that approval is not guaranteed. Another Canadian lapse guide describes insurer timeframes ranging from 30 days to two years depending on the company. Those are not contradictions to average out; they are evidence that reinstatement is a contract term, not a universal right with a single national deadline. The only reliable deadline is the one the insurer confirms for that policy, in writing, as soon as a lapse is discovered.

What happens to a claim at each stage

During grace, coverage is still in force, so a claim arising in that window is generally treated as a claim under an active policy. Lapse guidance explains that a claim in the grace period would generally be paid, usually with the outstanding premium deducted from the benefit. That deduction detail matters for expectations: the beneficiary is protected, but the unpaid amount does not simply vanish.

After lapse, the position reverses. Canada Life's guidance on why life insurance may not pay out states the position plainly in general terms: premiums must be kept up, a policy can lapse when payments are missed, and if a person dies while the policy has lapsed the insurer will not pay the claim. The same logic applies when a term simply reaches its end date or when group coverage ends after leaving a plan: a death outside the period of coverage is outside the contract, however long premiums were paid before. Harsh as that sounds, it is the core bargain of insurance: payment follows coverage that is in force at the time of the insured event.

That is also why the distinction between grace and lapse should never be blurred in family conversations. Saying a policy exists is incomplete. The useful statement is whether it is in force today, whether any premium is overdue, and what date would change that answer.

Permanent policies and cash value: help and trap

Permanent policies, such as whole life, can behave differently because they may build cash value. Some contracts can draw on that cash value to cover a missed premium, sometimes described as an automatic premium loan feature. Lapse guidance notes that an insurer can use available cash value to pay a premium after a miss, keeping the policy going for a time. That can quietly buy breathing room after a banking error or a short cash crunch.

The trap is in the word quietly. Each premium taken from cash value reduces what is available to support the policy later. If missed premiums continue, the cash value can run out, and at that point the policy can still lapse. A household may believe the problem solved itself months ago while the policy has been consuming its own value in the background. Loans or draws can also affect the eventual death benefit under the contract's terms. Anyone told that cash value covered a premium should ask for the current cash value, how much was used, what happens at the next due date, and whether regular payments have actually resumed. Comfort should come from those numbers, not from silence.

Term policies have no such cushion. There is no cash value to draw on, so the grace period is the whole buffer between a missed payment and lapse. That simplicity is part of term's lower cost, but it makes payment reliability especially important for term coverage.

How to recover after a missed payment: a numbered procedure

  1. Act the day the miss is discovered. Find the policy number, the amount overdue, and the grace end date by contacting the insurer through its official channel or checking the policy service record. Do not estimate the date from memory.
  2. Ask one direct question and get the answer in writing: is the policy in grace, already lapsed, or reinstated-pending? The recovery path is completely different at each stage.
  3. If still in grace, pay the full overdue amount through an official payment channel, keep the acknowledgement, and then confirm separately that the payment was applied and the policy is in good standing.
  4. If already lapsed, ask immediately about reinstatement: the deadline for this policy, the health evidence required, the total needed including any interest, and whether coverage resumes only after approval. Write down the name, date, and reference for the answer.
  5. Complete any reinstatement application honestly and promptly. Expect that updated health information may be required and that approval is the insurer's decision, not a formality.
  6. Fix the cause before celebrating the cure: update bank or card details, correct the mailing and email address, and set automatic payments if the insurer offers them and the household budget supports them.
  7. Calendar the next two due dates as a check, and after the second successful payment, file the confirmation with the policy so the episode has a clear end.

What success looks like: the insurer confirms in writing that the policy is in force and in good standing, the amount and date of the curing payment are recorded, any reinstatement conditions have been met rather than merely submitted, and the payment method that failed has been replaced or corrected. A submitted form without that confirmation is progress, not success.

Decision framework: reinstate or apply anew?

If reinstatement is available, it often preserves the original policy's pricing based on the age and health at first application, which can be valuable, especially if health has changed. Against that, reinstatement can require paying all missed premiums, possibly with interest, and can be refused after health review. A brand-new policy starts fresh at the current age, with current underwriting, and may be simpler or more expensive depending on health and the amount needed. The sensible comparison uses real figures from the insurer for reinstatement and a real quote for new coverage, side by side, with attention to any gap in coverage while either route is pending. Where health has worsened, reinstatement — if offered — usually deserves urgent attention before its deadline passes. Where the old policy no longer fits the household need at all, a professional can help weigh replacement without leaving a coverage gap. Never let an old policy lapse on the assumption a new one will be approved; sequence the decisions so protection is confirmed first.

Questions people commonly ask

Will the insurer warn a policyholder before lapse? Many insurers send courtesy notices, but Ontario guidance notes they are not legally obligated to remind the policyholder. Keep contact details current and do not treat the absence of a warning as evidence that all is well.

Are premiums paid before a lapse refunded for a term policy? A term policy that lapses has provided coverage during the period it was in force; the sources used here do not promise any refund of premiums for that past protection. Any specific non-forfeiture or surrender term belongs in the contract, particularly for permanent policies, and should be confirmed with the insurer.

Does difficulty paying mean coverage must simply end? Not necessarily as a first step. Canada Life guidance suggests contacting the insurer if paying is difficult and asking whether coverage can be adjusted. A smaller amount kept in force can protect a family better than a larger amount allowed to lapse without a conversation.
