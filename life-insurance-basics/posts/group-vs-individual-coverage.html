Group vs individual life insurance in Canada: what actually differs

Thinking it through · 8 min read · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

Many Canadians first encounter life insurance through a workplace benefits plan and reasonably ask whether that coverage is enough on its own. The answer is rarely found by comparing a single price. Group and individual coverage differ in who owns the contract, who controls its terms, whether premiums are guaranteed, how the amount is set, what happens on a job change, and whether medical evidence was required at the start. This guide works through those differences in plain language, explains the conversion option that can apply when group coverage ends, and offers a framework and a job-change procedure for testing whether group coverage needs an individual supplement. It is general information, not a recommendation for any one household.

Two different owners, two different promises

The deepest difference is ownership. With individual coverage, the person owns and controls the policy: they choose the amount, the term or type, the beneficiaries, and any optional features the contract offers, and the policy stays with them when employment changes. With group coverage, the group — typically the employer or an association — holds the master contract, and the employee or member holds coverage under that contract while eligible. Advisor guidance comparing the two forms makes this ownership contrast the starting point, and for good reason: control over changes, and the right to keep coverage, follow ownership.

That structure explains why group coverage feels effortless at enrolment and uncertain at exit. Joining a plan is usually automatic or simple, with coverage starting under the plan's eligibility rules. Leaving is the moment the limits appear. Group coverage is tied to employment or membership, and it usually ends when that relationship ends, unless a conversion or continuation option in the plan applies. Individual coverage has the opposite profile: more work at the start, including an application and usually medical underwriting, in exchange for a contract that does not depend on a particular job.

Neither structure is a trick. Group coverage spreads risk across many people, which is why it can be offered broadly and simply. Individual coverage prices and guarantees a specific person's contract. Problems arise when a household treats the first structure as if it had the promises of the second — assuming an amount chosen by a plan formula is a personal plan, or assuming coverage will travel automatically to the next job, a new business, or retirement.

Premiums, amounts, and medical evidence

Group premiums are blended. The price reflects the group as a whole rather than one person's health, and the comparison guidance used for this article notes that group rates are not guaranteed and do not offer health-based discounts in the way an individually underwritten policy can reward a person in strong health at application. An employer may also pay some or all of the cost while a person is employed, which makes group coverage look free or very inexpensive — until the employment relationship changes and that subsidy ends at the same moment the coverage does.

Individual premiums work differently. Because the insurer underwrites the applicant, a healthy applicant can obtain a contract with premiums guaranteed at issue for the policy's premium period, in an amount chosen for the household rather than by formula. The trade-off is the application itself: questions, and often medical evidence, take time, and an insurer can charge more, add terms, or decline based on underwriting. That is not a reason to avoid individual coverage; it is a reason to apply while options are open rather than waiting until a job loss and a health change arrive together.

Amounts deserve the same scrutiny. A salary multiple can sound large in isolation. Tested against a mortgage balance, years of household spending, childcare, and other debts — the kind of needs analysis covered elsewhere on this site — it may cover only part of what a family would need. Association coverage, often a flat basic amount, can be even further from a full need. Conversely, a dual-income household with older children, little debt, and substantial savings might find its group amount closer to sufficient. The formula alone settles nothing; the household arithmetic does.

What happens when you leave, retire, or lose eligibility

When group coverage ends, a conversion option may allow the person to move some or all of that group life coverage to an individual policy without providing medical evidence of insurability. Conversion guidance for Canada states that this step must generally be taken within 31 days of the group coverage ending. That deadline is the single most time-sensitive fact in this article. Miss it, and the no-medical-evidence route can be gone, leaving a fresh application — with underwriting — as the remaining path.

Conversion is a bridge, not automatically the cheapest destination. The same guidance notes that because the insurer accepts conversion without assessing the applicant's current health risk in the usual way, converted coverage tends to carry higher premiums, and a healthy, insurable person may pay less by shopping for a newly underwritten individual policy instead. Both routes can be explored in parallel inside the window: apply for conversion protection so coverage does not lapse, while comparing a new individual application if health and time allow. A licensed professional can help sequence that without letting the deadline slip.

Canada Life's general life insurance guidance offers a useful reminder underneath all of this: life insurance pays a one-time, tax-free payment to the chosen recipient when the insured dies, as long as premiums continue to be paid. Coverage that has ended, whether group or individual, does not pay. Continuity is therefore not an administrative detail; it is the product itself.

How to handle a job change: a numbered procedure

  1. Before the last day, obtain the group plan booklet or a written summary showing the life coverage amount, the exact date coverage ends, and the conversion terms, including the deadline and the maximum amount that can be converted.
  2. Mark the conversion deadline on a calendar as a hard date. Conversion guidance puts the window at 31 days without medical evidence; confirm the precise count and start date with the insurer or plan administrator in writing.
  3. While still covered, review the household need: mortgage, debts, income to replace, and dependants. Compare that need with the group amount rather than assuming the salary multiple was designed for this household.
  4. Ask for conversion figures and, separately, consider an application for a new individual policy. If health is strong, a new policy may cost less; if health has changed, conversion's lack of medical evidence may be its main value.
  5. Submit any conversion application and first premium inside the window, and keep written confirmation. Do not let a pending new application cause the conversion deadline to be missed without a deliberate decision.
  6. Once replacement or converted coverage is confirmed in force, record the new insurer, policy number, amount, premium, and beneficiary designation together, and check that the beneficiary form for any new policy has actually been filed.

What success looks like: there is no day on which the household believes it is covered but is not, the conversion deadline was either used or consciously allowed to pass with replacement coverage already in force, and the amount in force was tested against the household's own numbers rather than inherited from a plan formula.

Decision framework: is group coverage enough on its own?

Ask five questions. Is the amount, tested against debts and income needs, actually sufficient, or merely present? How secure is the connection to the plan — is a job change, contract end, disability, or retirement plausible in the next few years? Has health changed since joining the plan, making conversion protection more valuable and new underwriting harder? Who controls the coverage if the employer changes insurers or reduces benefits? And what would coverage cost to replace at an older age if the group route disappeared? If the amount is sufficient, the employment connection is stable, and replacement would be straightforward, group coverage may be doing its job as a foundation. If any answer is uneasy, an individual policy owned personally is the usual way households add stability, and comparison sources describe combining group and individual coverage as a complementary approach rather than an either-or choice.

Questions people commonly ask

Can an employer change or cancel group coverage? The employer or association controls the master contract, so plan terms can change under that contract. That loss of personal control is a core difference from an individual policy, whose guaranteed terms are set at issue.

Is group coverage ever the better buy? While eligible, employer-paid or subsidized group coverage is valuable and should not be discarded lightly, especially where no medical evidence was required. The question is not whether to value it, but whether to rely on it alone for a need that will outlast the job.

What if a new job also has benefits? A new plan may have a waiting period before eligibility, and its amount and terms will differ. Conversion or an individual policy can bridge a gap; do not assume two group plans join seamlessly until both booklets confirm dates and amounts.
