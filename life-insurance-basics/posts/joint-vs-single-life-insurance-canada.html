Joint vs single life insurance in Canada: first-to-die, last-to-die, or two policies?

Thinking it through · 8 min read

Couples often start with a simple question: should there be one policy that covers both partners, or a separate policy for each person? The honest answer is that these are not three versions of the same product at different prices. They are three different arrangements that pay at different moments, leave the surviving partner in different positions, and suit different jobs. Choosing only on the lowest premium can mean buying cover that does not arrive when the household actually needs it.

This guide separates the three structures clearly: joint first-to-die, joint last-to-die (also called second-to-die), and two single policies. It explains what each one does, what happens after a claim, where flexibility is gained or lost, and how to compare the options in a deliberate order.

One policy or two: what the choice actually changes

A joint policy covers two people under one contract and, in the forms described here, pays one death benefit in total. A single policy covers one person, so two single policies are two separate contracts that can each pay on the death of the person insured under that contract. That single difference — one payout or potentially two — drives almost every other trade-off.

Three other differences follow from it. First, a joint policy is priced on both partners together, reflecting both risk profiles, while a single policy is priced on its own insured person. Second, a joint policy ties the partners together: changing, splitting, or keeping cover after a separation is harder than with separate contracts. Third, the timing of the payout differs by design. A first-to-die policy is built around the first death. A last-to-die policy is deliberately built to wait until the second death. Two single policies respond to each death independently.

Before comparing prices, it helps to name the job the money must do. Is the main worry that one income disappears and the survivor must keep paying a mortgage and household bills? Is there a single shared debt that needs clearing once? Or is the concern an estate cost that only arises when the second partner dies, with the survivor able to manage in the meantime? Each answer points toward a different structure, which is why the decision framework near the end of this guide starts with the job rather than the premium.

Joint first-to-die: one payout, then the policy ends

Joint first-to-die insurance pays the death benefit when the first partner dies. Both partners are insured under the same policy and, in the term form described by the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC), receive the same coverage amount. The surviving partner is commonly named as the beneficiary, so the money arrives for the person who must now run the household on one income.

The crucial consequence comes next: once that payout is made, the policy has done its job and ends. The survivor no longer has life insurance under that contract. As FCAC puts it, if a partner dies, the survivor will need to apply for a new policy to continue coverage. Applying later means applying at an older age and with whatever health position exists at that time. That does not make first-to-die a poor choice — for a shared, one-time need it can fit well — but the survivor gap must be part of the decision, not a surprise discovered after a claim.

FCAC also notes two practical points about this structure. A joint first-to-die policy is usually less expensive than two identical single policies, because the insurer is pricing one payout rather than two. It is also less flexible if the couple separates or divorces. A couple whose lives may diverge needs to ask, before buying, what happens to the joint contract in that situation and whether it can be split, and on what terms.

Joint last-to-die: money only after both deaths

Joint last-to-die insurance reverses the timing. No benefit is paid when the first partner dies. The benefit is paid only after both insured people have died, to beneficiaries who are usually heirs — the partners themselves cannot receive it, because both must have died for it to be payable.

That timing makes last-to-die an estate and legacy tool rather than income protection. It can suit a couple whose survivor will be financially secure after the first death, but who want a sum available at the second death for estate costs, a legacy, or a gift. It does not replace income for the surviving partner, pay the mortgage in the difficult first months, or solve a cash problem at the first death. Treating it as if it did is the most common misunderstanding with this structure.

Because the payout is deferred until the second death, last-to-die is generally discussed in a permanent, estate-planning context rather than as basic family income cover. Couples considering it should be explicit about the question it answers: not “how does the survivor manage?” but “what sum should exist once both partners are gone, and who should receive it then?”

Two single policies: separate cover, separate payouts

With two single policies, each partner has an individual contract, an individual coverage amount, and an individual beneficiary designation. Each policy pays when the person insured under it dies, regardless of whether the other policy has paid. If one partner dies, the other receives that policy’s benefit and keeps their own cover in force — a direct contrast with first-to-die, where the survivor must start again.

Separate policies also allow the amounts to differ. Partners rarely have identical incomes, debts, and responsibilities, so identical coverage is often symmetry rather than planning. One policy can be larger, or run for a different period, than the other. Each contract can also be changed, converted where its terms allow, reduced, or ended independently of the other.

FCAC notes that single cover is usually more expensive than a joint first-to-die policy. The same agency notes the offsetting flexibility: because each partner has their own policy, changing a beneficiary after a separation or divorce is relatively easy. Separation does not automatically change a beneficiary designation, so a review is still essential — but separate contracts are structurally simpler to adjust than a shared one. Some couples also discover that a joint policy cannot always be split later without requalifying, which is another reason to ask about separation terms before signing rather than after.

How to compare the three options: a numbered procedure

Use this procedure as a comparison exercise before requesting quotes or deciding. It is a review procedure, not a recommendation of any structure.

  1. Write the job in one sentence. For example: replace income after either death, clear a shared debt once, or leave an estate sum after both deaths. If two jobs matter, write both — one structure may not do both well.
  2. List what must be paid at the first death. Include mortgage or rent, other debts, childcare, and a realistic period of household expenses. Note the total without rounding it down to make a premium look comfortable.
  3. List what must be paid at the second death. Include estate costs the couple is planning for and any intended legacy. Keep this list separate from the first-death list.
  4. Check the survivor position under each structure. For first-to-die, note that cover ends and a new application would be needed. For last-to-die, note that nothing is paid at the first death. For two singles, note that the survivor keeps their own policy.
  5. Compare like with like. Ask for the benefit amount, the period or permanence of cover, renewal and conversion terms, and what happens on separation for each option — not just the monthly premium.
  6. Ask the separation and splitting questions in writing. Can the joint policy be split, converted, or continued by one partner, and would anyone need to requalify or prove insurability again?
  7. Record beneficiaries for each option. Confirm who receives each payout, and name contingent beneficiaries where appropriate, using the same care as on any policy.

What success looks like: at the end, each structure has a written first-death outcome, second-death outcome, survivor-cover outcome, and separation outcome — and the chosen structure matches the job written in step 1. If the only completed column is price, the comparison is not finished.

Decision framework: matching the structure to the job

  • If the survivor needs money and ongoing cover after either death: two single policies match that shape most directly, because each death is covered and the survivor’s own policy remains.
  • If one shared obligation must be cleared once, at whichever death comes first: joint first-to-die matches that shape, provided the survivor’s loss of cover is planned for rather than ignored.
  • If the survivor is secure and the need arises only when both partners have died: joint last-to-die matches that estate or legacy shape, and should not be bought as income protection.
  • If the relationship, finances, or coverage amounts are likely to change separately: independent contracts preserve more room to adjust one partner’s cover without disturbing the other’s.
  • If more than one job matters: some couples use different tools for different jobs rather than forcing one contract to do everything. That is a structure to explore with a licensed professional, with each layer’s purpose written down.

Questions people commonly ask

Can both partners be covered for different amounts on a joint policy? In the joint first-to-die term form FCAC describes, both partners receive the same coverage. Different amounts for each partner are a feature of holding separate single policies.

Does a joint policy pay twice if both partners die? No. The joint structures in this guide pay once in total — either at the first death or after the second death, depending on the type.

What if a couple with a joint policy separates? FCAC warns that joint cover is less flexible on separation or divorce. The exact options depend on the contract, which is why splitting and continuation terms should be obtained before purchase.

FAQ

Is joint life insurance cheaper than two single policies in Canada? A joint first-to-die policy is usually less expensive than two identical single policies, according to FCAC — but it also provides only one payout, and the survivor loses cover. Lower price and lower total protection go together.

What happens to a joint first-to-die policy after the first partner dies? The benefit is paid, typically to the surviving partner, and the policy ends. The survivor who still needs cover must apply for a new policy.

Who receives a joint last-to-die payout? Usually heirs or other named beneficiaries, because the benefit is payable only after both insured partners have died.
