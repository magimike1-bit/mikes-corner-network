Life insurance and taxes in Canada: what is taxed and what is not

Fine print, translated · 8 min read

“Life insurance is tax-free in Canada” is a sentence that is mostly right and occasionally misleading. It is right about the central event most families care about: a death benefit paid to a named beneficiary. It becomes misleading when it is stretched to cover everything connected to a policy — surrendering a permanent policy during life, taking money out of it, naming the estate instead of a person, or earning interest on a benefit after it is paid.

This guide keeps those situations separate. It is grounded in what the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) and the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) publish, and it deliberately gives no invented figures: tax outcomes depend on the policy, its adjusted cost basis, the beneficiary designation, and provincial estate rules, so any specific number would need a specific policy and professional advice.

The short answer: the death benefit itself

CRA lists “most amounts received from a life insurance policy following someone’s death” among the amounts that do not have to be reported and are not taxed. FCAC describes the same payment in plainer language: life insurance provides a one-time, tax-free payment, called a death benefit.

In practice, that means a named beneficiary does not report the death benefit itself as income and does not pay income tax on it. The money can then be used for the purposes FCAC lists — replacing income, providing for children or dependants, paying funeral expenses, paying off debts, or making a gift to charity. The tax-free treatment attaches to the benefit itself. It does not automatically extend to income the beneficiary later earns by investing or depositing that money, a distinction covered below.

Two words in the CRA wording matter. “Most” is a reminder that structure matters, and “following someone’s death” limits the statement to the death benefit. Amounts received during the policyholder’s lifetime — on a surrender, a withdrawal, or a policy loan that is not repaid in certain circumstances — sit in a different part of the rules.

Named beneficiary versus estate: why the name on the form matters

A named beneficiary receives the benefit directly from the insurer under the policy designation. Because the money is paid to that person rather than flowing through the estate, a named-beneficiary payment bypasses the estate administration process — commonly described as bypassing probate — and is not distributed under the will. It arrives outside that process, which is also why keeping the designation current matters so much: the policy form, not a later wish expressed elsewhere, directs this payment.

Naming the estate produces a materially different route. FCAC states that if the estate is named as beneficiary, the death benefit becomes part of the estate, the estate distributes it according to the terms of the will, the amount is subject to estate taxes, and creditors may claim it to pay outstanding debts. The benefit can therefore be delayed by estate administration, counted in the value on which provincial estate or probate charges are calculated where they apply, and exposed to claims that a direct payment would not face in the same way.

The same estate outcome can happen by accident. FCAC notes that if no beneficiary is named, the insurer will by default assume the estate is the beneficiary. A blank designation is therefore not neutral — it is, in effect, an estate designation. Naming a contingent beneficiary also matters, because it gives the policy a direct recipient if the primary beneficiary has died before or at the same time as the insured.

None of this makes naming the estate always wrong. Some estate plans do it deliberately, for example where the will’s distribution structure is the point. The warning is narrower: do not drift into the estate route by leaving a form blank or stale while expecting the direct, outside-the-estate route.

When tax can arise while the policyholder is alive

Permanent life insurance usually builds cash value inside the policy. That cash value is where most lifetime tax questions live. Term insurance, by contrast, has no cash value to surrender or withdraw, so this section is mainly about permanent policies.

The key concept is the policy’s adjusted cost basis — broadly, a measure of the amounts that have gone into the policy for tax purposes, calculated under the tax rules and tracked by the insurer. Guidance on surrendering a policy is consistent on the core point: if a policyholder surrenders (cancels) a permanent policy and receives an amount, the portion above the policy’s adjusted cost basis can be taxable as income in the year it is received. The portion up to that basis is treated differently from the gain above it. Withdrawals from a permanent policy can raise the same kind of comparison between the amount taken out and the adjusted cost basis.

That is why surrendering should never be treated as simply “cashing in savings.” Before surrendering, the policyholder needs the insurer’s figures: the surrender amount, the adjusted cost basis, and the resulting taxable amount if any. Those figures are policy-specific and change over time, which is why this guide gives no example amounts. A related caution from FCAC concerns borrowing: a policy loan must be repaid, and an unpaid loan can reduce both the amount a beneficiary receives and the amount returned if the policy is cancelled. Loans and cancellations interact with the tax position, so both belong in the same professional conversation.

Transferring ownership, collapsing a policy with an outstanding loan, and other lifetime changes can also have tax consequences beyond the simple surrender case. The safe general rule is: a death benefit to a named beneficiary is the tax-free case; a lifetime disposition of, or withdrawal from, a permanent policy is where tax needs checking before acting, not after.

Interest, instalments, and money earned afterwards

CRA makes a second point that is easy to miss: income earned on a non-taxable amount is taxable. CRA gives the general example that interest earned when a non-taxable amount is invested must be reported. Applied to life insurance, the benefit itself is not taxed as income, but interest the beneficiary earns on it afterwards is income like any other interest.

This matters most when a benefit is not simply taken as an immediate lump sum. If the insurer holds the benefit, or it is paid in instalments under a settlement option, the interest portion built into or earned during those payments can be taxable, while the underlying benefit portion retains its character. Guidance on Canadian life insurance taxation identifies interest earned on a death benefit held in instalments as taxable for this reason. A beneficiary choosing between a lump sum and instalments should ask the insurer to separate, in writing, how much of each payment is benefit and how much is interest, and how the interest will be reported.

The same logic applies after a lump sum is received: deposit interest, investment income, and capital gains earned by investing the proceeds are taxed under their own normal rules. “The insurance payout was tax-free” describes receipt of the benefit, not everything that happens to the money for the rest of its life.

How to review a policy’s tax position: a numbered procedure

This is a review procedure for gathering the right information. It does not calculate anyone’s tax, which requires the actual policy figures and, for anything material, a qualified tax professional.

  1. Confirm who is named. Obtain the current beneficiary designation from the insurer — primary and contingent — and confirm whether any designation names the estate.
  2. Check for blank or stale designations. If no beneficiary is named, note FCAC’s default: the estate is assumed. If life circumstances have changed since the designation was made, record that a change may be needed.
  3. Identify the policy type. If it is term, note that there is no cash value. If it is permanent, continue to the cash value steps.
  4. Ask the insurer for two figures in writing: the current cash surrender value and the policy’s adjusted cost basis. Do not estimate either one.
  5. Ask what a surrender or withdrawal would trigger. Specifically, ask how much, if any, would be taxable as income in the year of surrender or withdrawal, and what slip or reporting would follow.
  6. Ask about any policy loan. Record the outstanding amount and how an unpaid loan would affect the death benefit and any surrender amount.
  7. If a claim is being made, ask about payment options. For instalment or held-benefit options, request the benefit-versus-interest split in writing before choosing.

What success looks like: the review ends with a confirmed beneficiary designation (not an assumption), written surrender and adjusted-cost-basis figures for any permanent policy, and a written benefit/interest split for any instalment choice. If any of those three is missing, the tax position has not actually been reviewed.

Decision framework: questions to ask before changing anything

  • Is the goal a direct payment or an estate distribution? A named person points to the direct route; the estate points to distribution under the will, with estate taxes and creditor exposure to weigh deliberately.
  • Is anyone about to surrender or withdraw from a permanent policy? Stop and obtain the adjusted cost basis first. The taxable portion cannot be judged from the cash value alone.
  • Is a benefit about to be taken in instalments? Separate benefit from interest before agreeing, and keep the insurer’s reporting statement with tax records.
  • Has a designation gone stale? A designation that names a former spouse, a deceased person, or the estate by default can redirect the entire outcome. Review designations after major life events.
  • Is the amount material or the structure unusual? Corporate ownership, trusts, assignments, and large permanent policies raise questions beyond this general guide and belong with a tax professional and a licensed insurance professional.

Questions people commonly ask

Does Canada tax inheritances from life insurance differently from other inheritances? The life insurance death benefit to a named beneficiary is covered by CRA’s statement that most amounts received from a life insurance policy following a death are not reported or taxed. What the beneficiary later earns on that money is taxed normally.

Are premiums tax-deductible? This guide does not treat premium deductibility for individuals, which depends on the policy’s purpose and ownership structure. That question, especially for business-owned policies, needs advice on the specific arrangement rather than a general statement.

Does the size of the death benefit change its tax treatment? Nothing in the CRA or FCAC descriptions quoted here makes the named-beneficiary treatment depend on the size of the benefit. Structure — who is named and what happens during the policy’s life — is where the differences arise.

FAQ

Does a life insurance beneficiary pay tax in Canada? Not on the death benefit itself. CRA lists most amounts received from a life insurance policy following someone’s death as amounts that are not reported or taxed, and FCAC calls the death benefit a tax-free payment.

Is life insurance taxable if it is paid to the estate? If the estate is the beneficiary, FCAC says the benefit becomes part of the estate, is distributed under the will, is subject to estate taxes, and may be claimed by creditors. That is a different outcome from a direct payment to a named person.

Do you pay tax if you surrender a life insurance policy in Canada? Tax can arise on a surrender: guidance on Canadian policy taxation states that the amount received above the policy’s adjusted cost basis is taxable as income in the year received. Ask the insurer for both figures before surrendering.
