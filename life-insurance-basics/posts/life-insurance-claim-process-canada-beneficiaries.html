The life insurance claim process in Canada: what beneficiaries actually do, step by step

Under the hood · By Penny Hughes · Published November 10, 2026 · Last updated November 10, 2026

A life insurance policy does not pay itself. Sun Life puts the starting point plainly: an insurer will not make a payment until the beneficiary files a claim, and it is up to the beneficiary to tell the insurer that the insured person has died. Everything after that is a short, document driven procedure. Three documents start almost every claim, a few situations add extra documents on top, and the timeline that frustrates families usually comes from mixing up three different clocks. This guide walks the procedure in order, separates those clocks, and ends with a triage for the claims that stall.

The three clocks: filing window, review, and payment

Published timelines for Canadian claims look contradictory, one guide saying one to two weeks, another saying a few months, because they are measuring different things. Separating the clocks makes the numbers agree.

The filing window is how long a beneficiary has to start the claim. Sun Life, citing the Canadian Life and Health Insurance Association, puts the filing deadline at 90 days to 12 months depending on the policy, and notes that a death reported more than 12 months later will likely still be processed if the death meets the policy conditions. Alberta Blue Cross, in its Basic Life claim guide, tells claimants to submit at their earliest convenience and no later than one year following the date of death. The window is a start by date, not a pay by date.

The review clock starts when the insurer has a complete file, not on the day of the death and not on the first phone call. PolicyMe describes the review as the insurer verifying the policy details and the cause of death once the required documents are submitted. Manulife adds a step beneficiaries rarely see named: it verifies that the claimant is the correct recipient before paying the funds, and it may ask for additional documents after reviewing the claim form.

The payment clock is the shortest of the three. Sun Life states that insurers pay claims in anywhere from a few days to a few months, by electronic funds transfer or by cheque. PolicyMe puts the typical range at 2 to 6 weeks where the documentation is complete and the claim is uncontested, and at two weeks to 60 days in most cases. A straightforward claim sits at the fast end of those ranges. A claim still collecting documents has not really started the payment clock yet.

The claim, step by step

  1. Find the policy, or at least the policy number. Sun Life notes that an annual statement might be easier to find than the original policy document, and that the key item on either document is the policy number. If no document surfaces, the person’s advisor, lawyer, or banker may know the insurer, and an insurer may be able to track a policy from the insured person’s name and birthdate.
  2. Notify the insurer and open the claim. PolicyMe lists the notification routes as the insurer’s website or a phone call. Ask what claim form the insurer wants and whether each beneficiary files separately, because Manulife’s Claimant’s Statement states that a separate form is required for each claimant.
  3. Gather the three core documents. Sun Life lists them as a death certificate, the policy itself or an annual policy statement, and the insurer’s claim form. The funeral director can provide copies of the death certificate; if no funeral director is involved, the provincial or territorial government provides them. Depending on the cause of death, the claim form may need a signature from a medical professional.
  4. Complete the claimant’s statement carefully. Manulife’s form asks for the policy number if available, information about the deceased and their death including the date and the cause of death, and funeral home information. Manulife states that insurance claims cannot be paid without the cause of death, so a vague entry at this step is a delay built in at the start. The claimant also states the capacity in which they claim: named beneficiary, executor, trustee, or other.
  5. Add the branch documents for your situation, using the table in the next section. An estate claim, a claim for a minor, and an accidental death claim each carry extra documents beyond the core three.
  6. Choose how to be paid and send banking information if you want direct deposit. Manulife’s form takes banking information for payment by direct deposit, the deceased’s social insurance number if the claim is made on behalf of the estate, and a business number for corporate beneficiaries.
  7. Keep copies of everything sent, and note the date the file became complete. That date, not the date of death, is the one the payment ranges above are measured from, and it is the date to quote if the claim needs a follow up call.

What success looks like: the insurer confirms the claim is complete, the claimant’s identity and capacity are verified, and payment arrives by transfer or cheque inside the published ranges for a complete, uncontested file. If weeks pass with no confirmation that the file is complete, the claim is still in the review clock, and the triage section below is the next step.

The document table branches by who is claiming

Most claim guides print one document list. Insurer forms do not work that way: the core three documents are joined by different extras depending on who is claiming and how the person died. The branches below come from Manulife’s group life claim form and Alberta Blue Cross’s Basic Life claim guide.

SituationBeyond the core documentsWhy
Named beneficiary, ordinary claimNothing extra in the standard case; smaller group claims can proceed on a funeral director’s statement of deathManulife’s group form accepts a funeral director’s statement of death for proceeds under $300,000
Larger claimA provincial death certificate, or an attending physician’s reportManulife’s group form requires one of those for proceeds of $300,000 and over
The estate is the beneficiary, or no beneficiary was namedProof of executorship, such as a probated will or a grant of administration; the executor or administrator submits the claimAlberta Blue Cross requires proof of executorship for benefits payable to the estate, and PolicyMe confirms unnamed proceeds go to the estate
A minor is the beneficiaryCourt appointment of guardianship documents for the minor’s estate, on larger amountsManulife’s group form requires the court appointment for proceeds over $50,000 paid to a minor
Accidental deathAn attending physician’s or coroner’s statement; an insurer may also request police reports or medical recordsManulife’s form requires the physician or coroner statement for accidental death, and PolicyMe lists coroner reports, medical records, and police reports as documents an insurer may require in some cases

Two tax points belong with the estate branch. Sun Life confirms the death benefit itself is tax free to a named beneficiary, and that if there is no named beneficiary, or the beneficiary is the estate, probate may be required. Sun Life also notes that any interest that builds up between the date of death and the payment date is taxable, even though the benefit is not. A named beneficiary claim is therefore both the fastest route and the cleanest one, which is a paperwork fact, not a sales point.

When a claim stalls: a delay triage

A stalled claim usually has one of a small set of causes. Match the symptom to the cause before calling, because each one has a different fix.

SymptomLikely causeMove that unblocks it
The insurer keeps asking for one more documentThe file is not complete, so the review and payment clocks have not startedAsk for the full outstanding list in one message, including which branch documents apply, and send them together
The death was recent and the policy was bought within the last two yearsThe claim is inside the contestability period and the application is being reviewed more closelyExpect a longer review; the site’s contestability guide explains the two year window and the denial route in full
Payment is waiting on court papersThe benefit is payable to an estate or to a minor, so probate or guardianship documents are part of the claimStart the court process early; it runs on court timing, not insurer timing
Nobody can find the policyThe claim cannot start until an insurer and a policy are identifiedWork the search in the next section, including the OLHI policy search if its conditions fit
Several beneficiaries, only some have filedEach claimant files their own statement, and the insurer verifies each recipientHave every beneficiary file promptly; one missing claimant can hold up a shared payment

If the policy cannot be found

Canada has no single registry a family can query, so the search is layered. Start with the person’s papers, annual statements, and bank records, then the advisor, lawyer, and employer routes Sun Life describes. Quebec’s Autorité des marchés financiers notes that some insurers’ websites also allow searching for lost life insurance policies at no cost.

The national backstop is the policy search run by the OmbudService for Life and Health Insurance, OLHI. The AMF explains that OLHI will send a search request to its member companies if certain criteria are met, and that those member companies account for approximately 99% of Canadian life and health insurance companies. The conditions matter: the family is expected to have searched on its own first, there must be evidence suggesting a policy exists, and OLHI’s search window covers a death that happened more than three months and less than two years ago, for a policy issued in Canada. A search that finds nothing is not proof no policy existed, because a non participating insurer or some group coverage may sit outside it.

If years have passed, check the unclaimed property route as well. The AMF notes that when an insurer cannot find the beneficiary in Quebec, it has to deliver the insurance amounts to Revenu Québec, which administers them, and that owners can claim through the register of unclaimed property. Other provinces run their own unclaimed property programs, so the register to check is the one in the province connected to the policy.

The cheapest preparation happens before any claim

Every delay above traces back to information a living policyholder could have left in one folder. Sun Life’s closing advice to its own readers is the practical version: make sure the beneficiaries on your policy know who they are. A beneficiary who knows the insurer’s name and can find an annual statement starts at step two of the procedure instead of at the search. The claim process itself is short. Finding out that a policy exists is the part that can take months, and it is the only part that can be fixed in advance.
