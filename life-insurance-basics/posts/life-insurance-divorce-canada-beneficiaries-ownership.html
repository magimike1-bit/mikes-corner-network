What happens to life insurance in a divorce in Canada: beneficiaries and ownership

Fine print, translated · 7 min read · Published October 26, 2026 · Last updated October 26, 2026

A divorce does not change a life insurance policy. Not the beneficiary, not the owner, not who can cancel it. Until the policy owner acts, the designation recorded with the insurer is the one that governs — which means an ex-spouse can still be the named beneficiary on the day a divorce is finalized, simply because nobody filed a change. The trap is assuming the legal process reaches into the policy on its own. It does not, and the rest of this guide is about who actually holds the controls.

The trap: when your ex owns the policy on your life

Three roles get tangled in conversation, so separate them. The insured is the person whose life the policy covers. The beneficiary is who receives the death benefit. The owner — the policyholder — is the one who controls the contract. Only the policy owner can change beneficiaries, and only the policyholder can cancel or modify a policy.

That produces the exposed position, and it is common: one spouse owns a policy on the other's life, perhaps taken out years ago when who-signed-what seemed like paperwork. After separation, if the designation on that policy is revocable, the owner can change the beneficiary, stop paying the premiums, or cancel the policy outright — with no notice to the insured person. The coverage someone believes is standing behind child support or a separation agreement can be gone, silently, while the paperwork saying it exists is still in a drawer.

The safety grid: who owns it, and are you revocable or irrevocable?

Two facts about each policy decide how safe its coverage is: who owns it, and whether the beneficiary designation is revocable or irrevocable. A revocable designation can be changed by the owner. An irrevocable designation is the locked kind — the safety grid published on this point treats exactly two positions as safe, and they are worth memorizing:

Your positionWhat it meansStanding
You own the policyChanges, payments, and cancellation are in your handsSafe
You are the irrevocable beneficiaryThe owner cannot simply redesignate the benefit away from youSafe
Your ex owns the policy, and you are a revocable beneficiaryYour ex can change the beneficiary, stop paying, or cancel — with no notice to youExposed

One default matters here: outside Quebec, beneficiary designations are revocable unless stated otherwise. So a designation nobody ever discussed is, in most of Canada, a revocable one — changeable by the owner alone. Quebec readers should not assume the same defaults apply to their policies; family-property rules there differ, as the tax section below touches on, and a Quebec policy deserves a province-specific check.

When a court can lock the beneficiary in place

There is an exception to the owner-controls-everything rule, and it exists precisely because support obligations can outlive a marriage. In Ontario, section 34(1)(i) of the Family Law Act lets a court order a spouse to designate the other spouse or a child as an irrevocable beneficiary of a life insurance policy. Read that alongside the safety grid: a court-ordered irrevocable designation moves the receiving spouse from the exposed row to the safe one, using the statute's own mechanism.

An order only protects anyone if it is actually carried out at the insurer. Manulife's checklist of family-law obligations and life insurance warns that failing to designate an ex-spouse as a judgment requires is a breach of the divorce order. The designation on file with the insurer — not the intention in the courtroom — is what a claim will be paid on, so the follow-through step is filing the designation the order describes and confirming the insurer has recorded it.

The statute cited here is Ontario's. Support and property law is provincial, and readers in other provinces should treat the mechanism as a question to put to their own family lawyer — does an equivalent order exist here, and has it been made — rather than as a Canada-wide rule.

Children on the policy: why a trustee has to be named

Divorce settlements often aim the death benefit at the children, and there is a mechanical problem with naming them directly: minor children cannot be paid a death benefit directly. A trustee must be named to receive it for them.

Skipping that step does not make the money disappear, but it leaves the payout without the person chosen to receive and manage it for the child. Where a settlement or court order counts on insurance supporting children, the trustee question belongs in the same conversation as the beneficiary line itself — who the trustee is, named on the policy paperwork, not settled informally afterwards.

Joint policies, transfers, and the tax wrinkle

Couples sometimes hold one joint policy rather than two individual ones, and divorce forces the question of what happens to it. Splitting a joint policy can have tax consequences, and those consequences vary by province — so the cost of unwinding a joint policy is not a number anyone can quote in a general guide, and it should be priced with advice before the split is agreed, not discovered after.

Quebec adds its own wrinkle. Under Quebec's partnership of acquests regime, a policy's value may be partitionable — part of the family property to be divided — a treatment Manulife's family-law checklist flags as specific to Quebec. A policy that looks like a purely personal contract in one province can carry a divisible value in another, which is one more reason the province on the policy matters as much as the names on it.

The post-divorce policy audit: a document-by-document check

None of the risks above require new insurance to fix. They require reading what already exists. Work through every policy in the household, in this order:

  1. List each policy and record three facts for it: who is insured, who is the beneficiary, and — the fact most people have never checked — who owns it.
  2. For each beneficiary designation, record whether it is revocable or irrevocable, using the insurer's record rather than memory.
  3. Place each policy on the safety grid. Flag every policy where an ex-spouse owns coverage and the other spouse's position is revocable.
  4. Read the separation agreement and any court order for insurance requirements. If an order requires a designation, confirm the insurer's record matches it exactly — a mismatch is a breach risk, not a technicality.
  5. Where children are beneficiaries, confirm a trustee is named for any child who is still a minor.
  6. For joint policies, get the tax treatment of a split checked for the province involved before agreeing to unwind it.
  7. Where the audit shows an exposed position, the fixes run through ownership and designation changes — or, in Ontario, a court-ordered irrevocable designation — with a family lawyer, because only the current owner can make changes until then.

What success looks like: every policy in the household has a known owner, a known beneficiary with a known revocable-or-irrevocable status, a trustee named for minor children, and an insurer record that matches whatever the separation agreement or court order requires. Any policy that cannot be described that way is the next phone call.
