Life insurance for newcomers to Canada: who can buy, how much, and when

Thinking it through · By Penny Hughes · Published November 21, 2026

Can someone who landed in Canada three months ago buy life insurance here? Usually, yes. The honest answer has two parts: your immigration status rarely closes the door outright, but it does set the ceiling on how much coverage an insurer will offer and how long you may need to be in the country first. Permanent residents sit at one end of that scale, visitors at the other, and permit holders in a middle band where the rules change insurer by insurer.

The short answer, by status

Your statusCan you apply?What limits usually apply
Permanent residentYes, from the day you landTreated like a citizen; no status based maximum
Work permit holderYes, at many insurersCaps that rise with time in Canada; some insurers decline permits entirely
Study permit holder, 18 or olderYes, at some insurersLower caps, commonly $250,000 to $500,000
Visitor or super visa holderGenerally noExcluded by most insurers

Permanent residents are the simple case. Insurers treat them like citizens, with no minimum time in Canada, and published guidance names major insurers covering permanent residents from the day they arrive. If you hold a PR card, the application in front of you is the same one a citizen fills out, priced the same way, and the rest of this guide matters to you mainly for the timing notes in the first 90 days.

Why status sets a ceiling, not a yes or no

For permit holders, insurers are managing a practical risk: a policy is a long contract, and a temporary permit is a short document. The response is rarely a refusal. It is a cap that grows as your footing in Canada grows. One published insurer guideline, from Empire Life, puts work permit holders at up to $500,000 of life coverage after 3 months in Canada, rising to $1,000,000 after 2 years, with study permit holders at up to $250,000 after 3 months. Treat those figures as one insurer's guideline, not a national rule. Another insurer may be more generous, and a handful will not take a work or study permit at all, which is why the same newcomer can be declined by one company and approved by the next without anything about them changing.

Two smaller rules catch people out. First, you must be physically in Canada to apply; an application cannot be started from abroad in advance of landing. Second, most insurers want your permit to remain valid for at least three months past the application date, so a permit deep into its final months can stall an application that would otherwise pass. If your permit is close to expiry, the extension paperwork is worth filing before the insurance application, not after.

The first 90 days, in order

Newcomer finances have a queue, and life insurance is not first in it. Provincial health coverage comes first, because a hospital bill is the immediate risk. Note that health coverage waits and life insurance rules are separate systems: Ontario has no waiting period for its health plan, while several other provinces can apply a wait of up to three months, and neither wait changes your life insurance eligibility. Once the health card application is in, the life insurance steps are short:

  1. Confirm your status documents are in hand and your permit, if you hold one, runs at least three months past today.
  2. Decide the amount from obligations, not from a cap. A common rule of thumb is 5 to 10 times income, and the sources behind this guide warn it can be too little or too much; a mortgage and young children push toward the top of the range, and this site's how much coverage guide works the fuller calculation.
  3. Apply while the numbers are fresh. Newcomers in Canada less than a year should expect a blood test as part of underwriting, and larger applications in the first year can add blood work for hepatitis B and C. That is routine first year practice, not a sign your file is in trouble.
  4. Keep the policy documents with your immigration papers. A claim years from now is easier when the paperwork lives in one place.

What happens if your status changes later

This is the question the sales pages skip. A policy already in force is a contract: if you applied as a work permit holder, were approved, and later become a permanent resident, the policy does not need to be rewritten and its price does not change. Status mattered at the application gate, where it set the ceiling you could buy under. It is not re-tested every year. The practical consequence cuts the other way as well. Coverage you could not buy as a newcomer, because a cap held you to $250,000, does not grow on its own when your status improves. Getting to the higher amount means a new application or an increase, underwritten at your later age and health. After about two years in Canada, most insurers treat a file much like a citizen's, so a top up application in year three usually faces fewer newcomer questions than the original did.

Permit expiry deserves one plain sentence: letting a permit lapse is an immigration problem with serious consequences of its own, but an in force life policy you are still paying for does not vanish because a document expired. Keep paying the premiums, keep the insurer's contact details current, and deal with the status question through immigration channels.

When waiting is sensible, and when it quietly costs

Waiting is sensible in one narrow case: your permit has under three months to run and an extension is already filed. A few weeks of patience avoids a stalled application. Waiting is costly in the common case, where a newcomer with dependants and a fresh mortgage postpones the application until they feel settled. Settled takes a year or two, premiums rise with age in the meantime, and the uninsured window is exactly the period when the family has the least savings to fall back on. The cap that applies to you today buys real coverage today; the larger amount can be added later, when your status allows it, as a second policy rather than a replacement.
