Life insurance for new parents in Canada: two calculations, one family

Thinking it through · 8 min read · Published October 26, 2026 · Last updated October 26, 2026

$28 a month. That is the starting premium published for $500,000 of 20-year term life insurance for a healthy 30-year-old non-smoking man, in pricing checked on October 25, 2026. For a woman of the same age buying the same amount and term, the published starting figure is $20 a month. Numbers that small invite a quick decision, and the premium is honestly the easy part. The harder question sits underneath it: is $500,000 the right amount for a household that has just added a baby? Published Canadian guides answer that question with two different methods, and for the same family the two methods can land more than $300,000 apart. This guide runs both, side by side, so the gap is visible before anyone buys anything.

Start with the price ladder: what the term length does to the bill

Term life insurance pays a set amount if the insured person dies during a set number of years — the term. Longer terms cost more per month, because the insurer is on the hook for more years. The published ladder below is for $500,000 of coverage for a 30-year-old male non-smoker, checked October 25, 2026. The annual column is simple multiplication of the monthly figure, shown so the yearly cost is easy to compare against other household bills.

Two things stand out. First, the jump from a 10-year to a 20-year term is $8 a month on these figures, while the jump from 20 to 30 years is $15 a month — the extra decade at the far end costs more than the extra decade in the middle. Second, these are published starting prices, not quotes. A real quote depends on the applicant, so treat the ladder as a way to compare term lengths against each other, not as a promise of any one person's premium.

Multiplier vs DIME: two answers for the same family

The quick method is the income multiplier. RBC Insurance describes expert recommendations of roughly 7 to 10 times annual income. Other Canadian guides present different ranges again, which is part of the problem: the multiplier a family happens to read first can move the answer by hundreds of thousands of dollars.

The slower method is DIME, a needs-based calculation. DIME adds four things: Debt, Income replacement (annual income multiplied by the years it should be replaced), Mortgage balance, and Education costs. Ratehub notes that the 10-times-income rule is somewhat arbitrary compared with a needs-based calculation like this one. Here is both methods run on the same household — one earner at $80,000 a year, using the worked example published with the DIME method:

MethodWorkingCoverage answer
Multiplier, low end$80,000 × 7$560,000
Multiplier, high end$80,000 × 10$800,000
DIME: debtDebts other than the mortgage$22,000
DIME: income$80,000 × 10 years$800,000
DIME: mortgageBalance owing on the home$260,000
DIME: educationSet aside for the child$20,000
DIME total$22,000 + $800,000 + $260,000 + $20,000$1,102,000

The DIME answer is $302,000 higher than even the top of the multiplier range, and nearly double the low end. The reason is structural: the multiplier never asks about the mortgage. A family carrying a $260,000 balance and a family renting with no mortgage get the same multiplier answer on the same income, even though the bills a survivor would face are completely different. DIME is not automatically right — the next sections take it apart — but it at least argues from the household's actual obligations.

Choosing the term: match the years the children depend on you

Amount is only half the decision. The term should cover the years when dependants rely on the income, and for a new baby those years are easy to count: a 20-year term bought at a child's birth runs until roughly that child's 20th birthday.

That arithmetic is a large part of why the 20-year term dominates this market. It is the most common term length chosen in Canada, and it is where most new-parent files land. Shorter terms leave a gap while children are still at home; longer terms keep paying for coverage after the dependence it was bought for has mostly ended — at the higher per-month prices shown in the ladder above.

The parent without a paycheque still needs a number

Both methods above size coverage for the earning parent. They say nothing about a parent who is home with the baby, working part-time, or between jobs — and if that parent died, the surviving parent would suddenly have to buy the childcare and household work that parent was doing for free.

Ratehub's guidance for parents says the DIME calculation should add the cost of childcare. Here is the honest problem: none of the Canadian new-parent guides checked for this article actually prices that cost in a worked example. So no figure is offered here, because inventing one would be worse than leaving the gap visible. Instead, price it from your own household, in three steps:

  1. Count the years until the youngest child would no longer need full-time care. That is the period the replacement cost runs for.
  2. Get a real local price for that care — an actual quote or a published rate from a provider the family could genuinely use — rather than a national average found in a headline.
  3. Multiply the annual price by the years from step 1, and add the result as its own line in the DIME total, next to debt and education.

What success looks like: the non-earning parent's coverage figure traces back to a priced local quote and a counted number of years — not to a guess, and not to zero just because there is no salary to multiply.

Splitting cover instead of buying one big policy

A family that lands on a DIME figure near $1,000,000 does not have to buy it as a single 20-year policy. The need is not flat: it is highest while the mortgage is large and the children are small, and it shrinks as both come down. Splitting the amount across two terms follows that shape.

Using the checked prices above: $500,000 on a 20-year term at $28 a month, plus $500,000 on a 10-year term at $20 a month, costs $48 a month while both policies run. That buys $1,000,000 of coverage for the first decade. After year 10 the shorter policy ends, the bill drops to $28 a month, and $500,000 remains for the second decade — when the mortgage balance and the remaining childcare years are smaller. The trade-off is administrative: two policies means two applications and two premiums to keep track of, and the step-down only works if the family's obligations really do shrink on that schedule.

When the smaller number is the honest one

DIME has a double-counting habit worth seeing before treating its total as a target. It adds the full mortgage balance on top of ten full years of income — yet in a household budget, part of that income is what would have been paying the mortgage. A family that subtracts the overlap will land somewhere between the multiplier and the raw DIME total, and that middle figure is not a compromise born of cheapness; it can be the more accurate count of what a survivor would actually need to replace.

The premium also has to survive an ordinary month. A policy sized perfectly on paper and cancelled in year two because the payment competed with rent protects nobody. On the checked figures, the difference between the 20-year and 30-year term for $500,000 is $180 a year — real money, but small next to the difference between having coverage in force during the childcare years and having none.

One question this article deliberately leaves open: whether to apply before or after the baby arrives. The sources behind the pricing and methods above do not cover how timing affects an application, so ask the insurer or a licensed advisor that question directly rather than guessing from a guide — this one included.
