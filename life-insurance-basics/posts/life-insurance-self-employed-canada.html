Life insurance for the self-employed in Canada: replacing coverage work won't provide

Thinking it through · 7 min read · Published October 29, 2026 · Last updated October 29, 2026

The invoice goes out, the client pays late, and the month still works out. That is ordinary life for a self-employed contractor, freelancer, or incorporated consultant in Canada — right up until the month it does not. An employee who gets sick or dies leaves behind a paycheque problem that a workplace plan partly softens. A self-employed person has no plan underneath them at all. Statistics Canada figures cited in a Quebec guide for self-employed workers put it plainly: the vast majority of self-employed workers have no employer-sponsored dental, health, or disability plan. There is no group life amount, no group disability benefit, and no employer quietly paying part of the premium. Whatever protection exists is protection the person arranged and pays for themselves.

The trap: shopping for life insurance while the disability hole stays open

Most self-employed coverage shopping starts and ends with life insurance, because death feels like the risk insurance is for. The sourced Canadian figures point the other way. Dickson Insurance, citing Statistics Canada 2025 figures, reports that only about 1 in 4 self-employed Canadians has any disability coverage at all. Read that slowly: three in four people whose income stops the moment they cannot work carry no cover for the event that stops the income without killing them.

That ordering matters for a household budget. A life policy pays once, to other people, after a death. A disability policy pays the worker, month by month, during the years when the mortgage, rent, groceries, and the policy premiums themselves still have to be covered. Treating the second policy as an optional extra — something to add later, when business is better — leaves the more exposed hole open for exactly the years when the household has the least slack. The honest shape of the problem is two holes, not one, and for owners with fixed business costs there is a third.

Layer one: term life sized on income you can prove

Start with the life layer, because it is still real: if people depend on the income, its disappearance has to be priced. Employee guides size that cover off a salary — a single, stable number an employer can confirm. Self-employment has no such number. Income arrives in a good year, a lean year, and a year the biggest client left in March.

Here is the uncomfortable part, stated directly: none of the self-employed sources checked for this guide — the Quebec self-employed insurance guide, Dickson Insurance, or the Dundas Life review of Desjardins disability products — sets out a sizing method for variable income. They price disability benefits in detail and leave the life-insurance amount as an exercise for the reader. So no multiplier is offered here, because borrowing an employee formula and presenting it as a self-employed method would be inventing the very thing the sources do not say.

What can be done honestly is a decision framework built from the household's own documented numbers:

  1. Write down the income the household actually lived on in each of the last few years — the filed, provable figures, not the best year and not a hoped-for year.
  2. Work from the lower, steadier end of that range. A cover amount sized on a peak year costs peak-year premiums every year, including the lean ones when the payment competes with rent.
  3. List separately what the money would have to do: clear debts, keep the household running for a counted number of years, and cover any costs that arrive with a death. Add those lines rather than rounding to a slogan.
  4. Check the total against an ordinary month's cash flow. A policy sized perfectly on paper and cancelled in a lean year two because the premium lost to rent protects nobody.

What success looks like: every dollar of the amount traces to a documented income figure or a named household cost — not to a salary that does not exist, and not to the best year on record.

Layer two: disability cover — benefit amounts, waiting periods, and the tax-free point

Disability insurance replaces part of a worker's income when illness or injury stops them working. For a self-employed person it is bought individually, and the published product parameters show the shape of what is on offer. Dundas Life's review of Desjardins Solo Disability describes benefits of $4,000 to $10,000 per month, with benefit periods of 2 years, 5 years, or to age 65, and waiting periods that run from 30 to 730 days. The benefit is capped at $10,000 per month, and premiums can rise at renewal — a clause worth finding in any contract before signing, because a price that resets later is not the price the first year's budget was built on.

Two terms in that paragraph do real work. The waiting period (sometimes called the elimination period) is the stretch between stopping work and the first benefit payment: at 30 days the household self-funds one month; at 730 days it self-funds two years. The benefit period is how long payments can run once they start. A cheaper premium usually means one of those two dials was turned against the buyer, so compare policies dial by dial, not headline by headline.

One tax point works in the self-employed buyer's favour, and Dickson Insurance states it directly: disability benefits from a policy you pay for yourself are received tax-free. A $5,000 monthly benefit is $5,000 the household can spend, not a gross figure that shrinks at tax time — which also means the benefit amount can be compared honestly against actual monthly spending, with no conversion in between.

Layer three: overhead expense cover for owners with rent and staff

A solo operator whose costs are a laptop and a phone line may only need the first two layers. An owner with a lease, equipment payments, or staff has a second business problem running alongside the personal one: the fixed costs keep arriving while the owner cannot work, even if the household's own bills are covered. Dickson Insurance frames the owner's position as two layers of disability need — personal income replacement and business overhead expense coverage. Overhead cover is aimed at those fixed business costs during a disability, keeping the business itself alive to return to.

That framing gives a clean test. If the business would have nothing left to come back to after six months of unpaid rent and idle equipment, the overhead layer is not a luxury add-on; it is the difference between a pause and a closure. If there are no fixed costs to speak of, it is a premium for a risk that barely exists, and the money belongs in the first two layers instead.

What the Quebec numbers can — and cannot — tell the rest of Canada

The clearest published disability pricing retrieved for this guide is Quebec-scoped, and it is presented as exactly that. The Quebec guide for self-employed workers puts a healthy 35-year-old at roughly $85 to $100 per month for about a $5,000-per-month disability benefit. In their fifties, the same benefit runs about $220 to $250 per month.

Run those published figures through a household calculator and the planning point survives even though the prices are one province's:

The annual columns are simple arithmetic on the published monthly figures. Two things stand out. First, waiting costs real money: the fifties price is roughly two and a half times the age-35 price for the same benefit, so a decision deferred is not the same decision at the same price. Second, these are Quebec figures for the profiles described — a reader in another province, another trade, or another health picture gets a quote, not this table. The table's job is to show the order of magnitude a household is budgeting around, and to make the cost of delay visible before a quote is ever requested.

The three layers, checked in order

LayerWhat it replacesThe question that sizes it
Term lifeIncome for the people who depend on it, if the worker diesWhat do the documented income figures and named household costs add up to?
DisabilityThe worker's own income, month by month, while they cannot workHow many months could the household self-fund — and does the waiting period match that?
Business overhead expenseFixed business costs — rent, equipment, staff — during a disabilityWould the business still exist after six months of costs with no owner working?

Work the layers in that order, price each one on its own terms, and be suspicious of any single product presented as covering all three — the sources behind this guide treat them as separate covers with separate jobs. And keep the gap in view while doing it: with only about 1 in 4 self-employed Canadians holding any disability coverage, the most common self-employed insurance plan in the country is still no plan at all.
