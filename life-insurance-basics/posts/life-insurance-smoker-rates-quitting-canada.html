Life insurance smoker rates in Canada — and how quitting changes them

Underwriting · 8 min read

Few single answers on a life insurance application move the price as much as the smoking question. Insurers treat smoking as one of the strongest predictors they are allowed to price, and the rating follows you into every quote until it doesn't. The good news, published by the insurers themselves, is that the rating is not a life sentence: quit, stay quit for a defined period, and the same companies will consider you for non-smoker rates. This guide explains who counts as a smoker, what the rating does to the price, and exactly how the re-rating path works in Canada.

Who insurers count as a smoker

The definition is broader than most applicants expect, and it is based on use within a look-back window, not on how you describe yourself. Sun Life puts it plainly: most insurance companies require you to be smoke-free for 12 months before qualifying for non-smoker rates, and that includes cigarettes, cigars, vaping, and other tobacco products. Manulife's plan wording is in the same territory: non-smoker rates require that, as of the date the application is received, you have not smoked any cigarettes in the past 12 months and you meet the insurer's health standards.

Two consequences follow. First, the “I only vape” and “I only have the occasional cigar” cases are not loopholes for most insurers — vaping is named explicitly in Sun Life's list, and product-by-product treatment varies enough that the only safe move is to answer the question exactly as asked, for the exact product being applied for. Second, smokeless products are not a free pass either: industry guidance for the Canadian market treats chewing tobacco and other smokeless tobacco as a full smoker classification, with no occasional-use exception. Nicotine is what underwriting is detecting, not smoke.

Honesty here is not a courtesy; it is the contract. RBC Insurance warns that it is critical to answer smoking status truthfully, because a policy may be voided or the death benefit not paid out if the answer was wrong. A cheaper premium bought with a false answer is not coverage — it is a receipt for coverage that may not exist at claim time.

What the smoker rating does to the price

Insurer-published summaries agree on the scale, if not the decimal. Sun Life states that smokers can pay two to three times more than non-smokers, and that quitting can lead to substantial savings. RBC Insurance describes smoker premiums as almost double those of non-smokers. Both are describing the same reality from slightly different books of business: smoking status is commonly the difference between a standard rate and a rate two to three times as high for the same person, same age, same coverage amount.

To see what that means in dollars, use one clearly labelled illustrative example rather than a “typical” price, because no typical price exists. One Canadian advisory source illustrates a healthy 40-year-old man in Ontario buying $500,000 of 20-year term coverage at about $126 a month at smoker rates versus about $44 a month at non-smoker rates — a gap of roughly $82 a month, or close to $1,000 a year, for identical coverage. Treat those figures as an illustration checked on 24 October 2026, not a quote: your price will depend on age, sex, province, health, build, family history, and the insurer. The structural point survives every variation of the numbers — the smoker loading is large, it compounds over a 20-year term into five figures, and it is the single largest controllable line in most smokers' premiums.

The 12-month line: what quitting actually unlocks

Twelve months is the number that recurs across insurer sources. Blue Cross Life states that many insurers consider applicants for non-smoker rates after they have been tobacco-free for at least 12 months. RBC Insurance says the same in consumer language: after you have quit smoking for 12 months, you can apply for non-smoking rates for your policy. Sun Life likewise notes that if you quit, you can reapply for better rates later.

Read those sentences carefully, because two words carry the whole mechanism: can apply. Nothing re-rates itself. Your premium does not drop on the anniversary of your last cigarette, your insurer does not monitor your habits, and no refund arrives for the months already paid at smoker rates. The 12-month milestone makes you eligible to ask; the insurer then decides, under its own rules, whether the non-smoker class applies. Some insurers also draw finer distinctions — preferred classes, for example, can involve longer or stricter standards than the basic non-smoker threshold — so “12 months” should be read as the common doorway, not a universal guarantee of the best possible rate at every company.

How to ask for a re-rate, step by step

Because this is a procedure with a verifiable end state, here it is as numbered steps, drawn from the insurers' own published instructions. Note that this is a general-information procedure for approaching your insurer — the exact form names and evidence requirements are your insurer's, and they vary.

  1. Count the full window. Confirm you have been completely tobacco- and nicotine-free for at least 12 months by the definition in your policy or application — including vaping and any nicotine products, if your insurer counts them. If you are at month nine, the correct action is to wait, not to round up.
  2. Contact the insurer and ask for the re-rate process by name. Blue Cross Life describes the route directly: contact your insurer to discuss your options once a year has passed. Manulife's published process asks the policyholder to notify the company after a 12-month period without smoking and complete a Non-Smoker Application.
  3. Complete the declaration or application the insurer requires. Expect paperwork confirming that you have quit. Blue Cross Life notes you may be required to sign paperwork confirming your quit, and some insurers may require medical tests to verify your status — a cotinine test is the usual mechanism, and a positive result ends the application.
  4. Wait for the decision, and note the effective date. A re-rate is an underwriting decision, not a formality. Under Manulife's published wording, if the application is approved, the lower non-smoker rates begin on the first of the month following the date of the Non-Smoker Application.
  5. Confirm the change in writing. Check the next premium notice or policy statement shows the non-smoker class and the new premium. That document is your proof the re-rate actually happened.

What success looks like: a written confirmation or updated premium notice showing the non-smoker classification applied to your policy, with the lower premium in effect from a stated date — not a phone agent's assurance that it is “probably sorted.”

New policy or status change on the current one?

There are two doors, and the right one depends on your situation. The first door is the status change on your existing policy, as above: same coverage, same start date, new classification. It preserves everything about the contract you already hold. The second door is a brand-new policy at non-smoker rates — sometimes cheaper if your overall health picture and the market have moved in your favour, but it is a fresh application, with fresh underwriting and a fresh contestability period, and it should never be set up by cancelling the old policy first. If you explore the second door, the old coverage stays in force until the new policy is approved and issued. An advisor can model both; the insurers' published material supports the first door as the default expectation, since both RBC Insurance and Blue Cross Life describe applying to change the rates on your policy rather than replacing it.

Decision framework: buy now, wait, or buy and re-rate

Smokers facing a coverage need today usually weigh three paths. Path one: buy at smoker rates now, quit when ready, and re-rate after 12 clean months. This protects the family immediately and treats the higher premium as a temporary, shrinking cost — for most people with dependants and a mortgage, this is the path the arithmetic favours, because a year or two of loadings costs far less than a year or two of being uninsured. Path two: delay buying until 12 months after quitting, to start at non-smoker rates. This only makes sense if the coverage need itself can genuinely wait and no one depends on the protection in the meantime — a rare combination, and a gamble on both health and insurability staying unchanged. Path three: buy a smaller or shorter policy now at smoker rates to cover the essential need, then re-rate or top up later. Whichever path you take, diary the 12-month date the day you quit. The most common way smokers overpay is not choosing the wrong path — it is qualifying for a re-rate and never asking for it, leaving the smoker premium running for years after it needed to.

Questions people commonly ask

Does vaping count as smoking for life insurance? For most Canadian insurers, yes. Sun Life's published guidance includes vaping alongside cigarettes and cigars in the 12-month smoke-free requirement. Always answer the exact question on the application.

I quit six months ago. Can I get non-smoker rates now? Generally not yet: the common threshold across insurer sources is 12 months tobacco-free before you can apply for non-smoker consideration. Applying early with an honest answer simply records smoker class; the calendar is the qualification.

Will my premium drop automatically when I hit 12 months? No. Every insurer source describes an application — a notification, a form such as a Non-Smoker Application, possibly verification testing — followed by an approval and an effective date. Ask, in writing, and confirm the result in writing.
