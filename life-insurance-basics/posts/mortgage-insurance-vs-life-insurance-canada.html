Mortgage insurance vs life insurance in Canada

Comparing coverage · 8 min read

At the mortgage signing table, usually near the end of a long appointment, comes a small question with a large consequence: would you like mortgage life insurance with that? It sounds like a routine add-on. It is actually a choice between two structurally different products — and the federal Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) publishes guidance on exactly this choice, because the differences are contractual, not cosmetic.

One clarification before anything else, because the names collide. This article is about mortgage life insurance — an optional product that may pay your outstanding mortgage balance to your lender if you die. It is not mortgage loan insurance, also called default insurance, which protects the lender if you stop paying and which you must buy if your down payment is less than 20 percent of the purchase price. Different product, different purpose, different rules.

Two products that sound alike and are not

Mortgage life insurance is a form of creditor insurance sold alongside the loan. The FCAC describes it plainly: it is an optional product that may pay the balance on your mortgage to the lender upon your death. The premium is based on the amount of your mortgage and your age, and premiums are usually added to your regular mortgage payments. You do not own a standalone policy in the usual sense; the coverage is attached to that specific mortgage, with that specific lender.

Personal life insurance — most often term life for mortgage-sized needs — is a contract you own yourself. You choose the amount, you choose who receives the money, and the coverage exists independently of any loan. The FCAC itself draws the comparison: term or permanent life insurance may provide better value than mortgage life insurance, because with term or permanent coverage the death benefit does not decrease during the policy term, and your beneficiaries may use the insurance money to pay for the mortgage — or for anything else that matters more at the time.

Who gets paid, and who decides

This is the difference families feel first. With lender mortgage insurance, the payout goes to the lender and retires the mortgage balance. That is a real benefit — a home kept, a large payment gone — but it is the only thing the money can do, and it arrives in exactly the amount still owing, no more.

With personal term life, the payout goes to the beneficiaries you named, as a lump sum they control. They can pay off the mortgage in full, pay it down partly and keep cash for income replacement, childcare, or other debts, or keep the mortgage and use the money elsewhere entirely. A mortgage is rarely a household's only financial exposure, and it is not always the most urgent one. Control over the proceeds is not a marketing flourish; it is the ability to match the money to the actual emergency.

The declining balance, in plain numbers

Here is the mechanic the FCAC flags in one sentence: as you pay down your mortgage, the premiums generally remain the same, even though you will owe less on your mortgage over time. In other words, the price stays level while the thing being bought shrinks.

Make it concrete with a simple illustration — arithmetic, not a quote. Imagine a $400,000 mortgage and creditor coverage priced to match it. In year one, the coverage protects roughly $400,000. After years of payments, suppose the balance has fallen to $240,000. The creditor coverage now pays at most $240,000 — yet the premium, set from the original amount and your age at application, generally has not fallen with it. A level term policy bought for $400,000 at the same time still pays $400,000. The gap between those two numbers — $160,000 in this illustration — is the quiet cost of the declining structure: same household, same premium habit, materially less protection delivered in the later years, which are often the years when other debts and dependants' needs have not shrunk at all.

There is a second-order effect worth naming. Because the benefit tracks the balance, the coverage is most generous exactly when the household has had the least time to build other savings, and least generous later, when a survivor might prefer cash flexibility over a fully retired mortgage on a house they may choose to sell. None of this makes creditor coverage useless — it makes it narrow. It does one job, in a shrinking amount, and no other job.

Underwriting timing: approved now or judged at claim time

The least visible difference may be the most important one. With personal term life, medical underwriting happens when you apply: health questions, sometimes a medical exam, and a decision made while everyone is alive and able to provide evidence. Once the policy is issued, the insurer has assessed the risk up front.

Creditor coverage commonly works the other way around. There is usually no medical exam at application, which feels convenient at the signing table — but underwriting typically occurs when a claim is made. That means eligibility can be re-examined after death, against the health answers given on a short application form, and a payout can be denied if the answers or the health history do not hold up. The convenience is real, and so is the trade: certainty is deferred to the worst possible moment to discover a problem. This is also why accuracy on any insurance application matters so much — the FCAC notes that when a lender offers an optional product, it must tell you the charges, get your express consent before providing it, and give you the option to cancel. Optional means optional: a lender cannot force you to buy a product or service as a condition of getting another one, a practice called coercive tied selling, and declining mortgage insurance does not affect your mortgage approval or your interest rate.

Portability: what happens when the mortgage moves

Canadian mortgages move. People sell, refinance, switch lenders for a better rate, or pay the loan off early. Personal term life does not care about any of that: it is portable, following you whether you move house, pay off the mortgage, or change lenders, because it was never attached to the loan in the first place.

Creditor coverage is tied to the mortgage and the lender. Switch lenders, sell the home, or pay off the mortgage, and that coverage ends; protecting the new mortgage means applying again — at an older age, and possibly with a changed health picture that makes new coverage more expensive or harder to get. Households that expect to move or refinance within the term should weigh this heavily. Coverage that evaporates at exactly the moments life changes is coverage you may end up buying twice.

Decision framework

Run these steps in order if you are deciding today — including if you already hold creditor coverage from a past signing. First, inventory what you already have: employer group coverage or an existing personal policy may already cover the mortgage need, and the FCAC's first advice is to check existing coverage before buying more. Second, size the real need: mortgage balance plus income replacement and other debts, not the balance alone. Third, compare structures, not just prices: a level, portable, beneficiary-paid benefit and a declining, lender-paid benefit are different goods, so compare quotes for personal term coverage before concluding the creditor premium is “cheap.” Fourth, mind the sequence: if you decide to replace creditor coverage, apply for the personal policy first and cancel the creditor coverage only after the replacement is approved and in force — never leave a gap because paperwork felt slow. Fifth, revisit at every mortgage event: renewal, refinance, and moves are the natural moments when portability stops being theoretical.

Are there households for whom creditor coverage is a reasonable stopgap? It can be — for example, as immediate cover for someone who cannot currently qualify for medically underwritten coverage, held with clear eyes about the claim-time underwriting risk. That is a judgement call to make with a licensed professional who can see the full picture, not a default to drift into because the form was on the table.

Questions people commonly ask

Is mortgage life insurance mandatory in Canada? No. It is an optional product. Your lender must get your express consent, must disclose the charges, and must let you cancel. It is also different from default insurance, which is mandatory only when a down payment is under 20 percent.

Can my family use a term life payout to pay the mortgage? Yes. That flexibility is the point: beneficiaries receive the money and may use it to pay the mortgage, in whole or in part, or for any other need.

What should success look like? Coverage whose amount, owner, and beneficiary you can describe in one sentence each — and that still exists, unchanged, after your next move or refinance.
