No medical exam vs fully underwritten life insurance, in plain language

Under the hood · 8 min read · Published October 17, 2026 · Last updated October 17, 2026

“No medical exam” sounds like one product. In Canada it is at least three different products, plus a fourth route that healthy applicants often miss. They differ in what the insurer asks, how much coverage is available, when the coverage actually pays in full, and what the premium buys over a whole term. This guide separates them, puts dated example pricing beside each route, and calculates the total cost over 20 years so the trade-off is visible in dollars rather than percentages. It is general information only, not a recommendation for any one applicant.

Three products hide behind one label

Simplified issue skips the medical exam and lab work but still asks a limited set of health questions on the application. Answers are checked, and a “yes” to a serious-condition question can still lead to a decline or a different plan. In most cases coverage starts from day one. Guaranteed issue asks no health questions at all and no exam. Qualification is generally based on age and residency, which is why the coverage amounts are small and the price per dollar of coverage is highest. Deferred plans sit between the two in spirit: they accept more health history than simplified issue, and in exchange the full death benefit is deferred, as explained below.

The fourth route is accelerated underwriting. It keeps the extensive health questions of a traditional application but usually waives the nurse visit, fluids, and measurements for applicants who qualify. Coverage is full from day one, and published broker guidance describes limits up to $5,000,000 depending on age. Accelerated underwriting is not a product for a serious health condition; it is a faster lane through ordinary underwriting. Lumping it in with guaranteed issue, as many shopping pages do, leads healthy readers to accept low limits they never needed to accept.

Fully underwritten coverage remains the baseline. It involves a complete medical and family history and, in many cases, a nurse visit for blood and urine samples, blood pressure, height, and weight. Broker guidance puts the process at roughly four to eight weeks. For an applicant in good health, it provides access to the lowest premium and the highest coverage amounts. No-exam routes trade some of that price and capacity for speed and for a higher chance of acceptance.

Two cautions belong beside any table like this. First, limits vary by insurer, age, and plan; the named plans above are documented examples, not market-wide guarantees. Second, a “no exam” label says nothing by itself about when the benefit pays in full. That timing lives in the waiting-period and graded-benefit wording, and it deserves its own section below.

Dated example pricing, checked October 17, 2026

Percentages are hard to budget with, so here is a concrete published example. NoMedicalLifeInsurance.ca, a Canadian broker site, publishes example monthly pricing for a 20-year term policy for non-smokers. The figures below were checked on October 17, 2026. They are that site’s examples, not quotes, and any individual premium depends on age, sex, smoking status, health class, insurer, and the exact plan.

Read the third column carefully: it buys one-tenth of the coverage in the first two columns. Published comparison tables frequently place these numbers side by side without flagging the difference in face amount, which makes guaranteed issue look merely “more expensive” instead of what it is: far more expensive per dollar of coverage, for a much smaller benefit. That is not an argument against guaranteed issue. For an applicant who cannot pass a health questionnaire and needs a small final-expense benefit, it may be the only available route. It is an argument for comparing cost per $1,000 of coverage, on the same coverage amount, before drawing any conclusion.

The same source summarizes the usual premium relationship this way: simplified issue typically costs 25 to 50 percent more than standard coverage, and guaranteed issue typically 50 to 100 percent more. The age-40 example above sits far below those ranges, which is itself the lesson: ranges describe a market, examples describe one profile, and neither replaces a quote for a specific person.

The 20-year total cost, calculated

A monthly difference looks small until it is multiplied by the 240 payments in a 20-year term. Using the same dated example figures, with no rounding and no assumed increases (these are level-term examples):

The pattern matters more than any single row. At younger, healthier profiles in this example set, the simplified premium penalty is almost trivial, because both prices are low. At age 60 the same structural penalty becomes $6,240 over the term. A total-cost view also reframes the exam itself: the nurse visit is a few hours of inconvenience once, in exchange for a price that is locked for the whole term. Where an applicant expects to qualify for standard rates, that exchange is usually heavily in the applicant’s favour at older ages, and roughly neutral at younger ones. Where an applicant expects a decline, the calculation never gets to run, and acceptance becomes the deciding factor instead.

The first two years: graded and deferred benefits

The most consequential fine print in no-exam coverage is not the premium. It is what the policy pays if death occurs early. On Canada Protection Plan’s Deferred Elite Term, full coverage begins after two years, while an accidental death is covered immediately. A published review of the deferred permanent version describes the graded shape in detail: a non-accidental death in the first policy year returns the premiums paid with no death benefit, a death in the second year pays half the coverage amount, and the full benefit applies after two years. Exact grading differs by plan and insurer, so the contract wording controls, but the shape is common across deferred and guaranteed products: the early years pay less, sometimes much less.

Guaranteed issue is commonly described the same way, with a 24-month waiting period applying to pre-existing conditions. A family buying a guaranteed policy for a seriously ill relative, expecting the full face amount in month three, is buying something the contract does not sell. Simplified issue is the contrast case: the Canada Protection Plan simplified term plan documented above carries immediate coverage with no waiting period. This is why the first question about any no-exam offer should not be “how fast is approval?” but “from what date, and in what amount, does this policy pay for a non-accidental death?”

Decision framework: which route fits which situation

Work through four questions in order. First, could this applicant plausibly pass full underwriting? If yes, get the underwritten or accelerated quote first; PolicyMe notes that failing a fully underwritten application does not prevent a later guaranteed issue application, so the low-price route can be tried without burning the fallback. Second, how much coverage is actually needed? A $750,000 income-replacement need cannot be met by a $50,000 guaranteed policy no matter how fast it issues; if the need is large, simplified or underwritten routes are the only realistic ones. Third, when must the full benefit be payable? If a death in the first two years must pay in full, deferred and most guaranteed structures fail that test by design. Fourth, what does the total term cost look like? Multiply the monthly premium by the full number of payments, as above, and compare totals on equal coverage amounts. Speed is a real benefit, but it should be priced like one.

Questions people commonly ask

Is no medical insurance always much more expensive? No. The dated examples above show simplified issue within a dollar a month of standard pricing for two profiles, and $26 a month more at age 60. Guaranteed issue is a different story: it costs far more per dollar of coverage and provides far less of it.

Does “no medical exam” mean no health questions? Only for guaranteed issue. Simplified issue still asks a short set of health questions, deferred plans ask limited questions, and accelerated underwriting asks extensive questions. Answers on any of them form part of the contract.

Can a healthy person use no-exam coverage just for speed? Yes, and simplified or accelerated routes exist partly for that case. The honest comparison is the total cost over the term against the value of skipping the exam and the weeks of underwriting, using quotes for the same coverage amount.

What if an application is declined? A decline on one route does not close the others. Simplified, deferred, and guaranteed plans exist for applicants with health history or previous declines, and each step down that ladder trades coverage size and early-years payout for a higher likelihood of acceptance.
