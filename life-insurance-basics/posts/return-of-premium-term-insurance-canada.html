Return of premium term insurance in Canada: the break-even return nobody quotes

Fine print, translated · By Penny Hughes · Published November 21, 2026

The pitch sounds like a free lunch: pay for term insurance, outlive the term, and get every premium back. The refund is real. What the pitch leaves out is that the refund was prepaid, month by month, in a higher premium, and whether the deal favours you turns on one number almost no guide calculates: the annual return your extra premium would need to earn, invested instead, to match the refund. This article runs that number.

Three different products hide behind money-back

In Canada, money back from life insurance usually means one of three things, and they are not interchangeable. The first is a return of premium rider, an add-on that refunds premiums if you reach the end of a set period without claiming. The second is the cash surrender value inside a whole life or universal life policy. The third is dividends from a participating whole life policy. One fact reorders the whole conversation: return of premium riders are most common on critical illness insurance in Canada and comparatively uncommon on term life, even though term life is where the advertising points. All three routes cost more than plain term insurance covering the same risk.

The published example, and the extra premium inside it

One widely published comparison prices a healthy 30 year old buying $500,000 of 20 year term coverage at about $25 a month for standard term and about $38 a month with return of premium, and notes that return of premium typically costs 30 to 60 percent more than standard coverage. Those are published estimates, not a quote, and your own figures will differ. They are, however, exactly the shape of every real quote: the same death benefit, the same term, and a monthly difference that looks small enough to wave away.

Do not wave it away. Multiply it out. The difference is $13 a month across 240 months, which is $3,120 of extra premium over the term. The refund at the end is $9,120, being every premium paid at $38 a month. Both numbers matter, because the honest comparison is never refund versus nothing. It is refund versus what the $13 a month becomes if you keep it.

The break-even calculation, worked in full

PathYou pay extraValue at year 20
Return of premium policy$13 a month, $3,120 total$9,120 refund, tax free

Standard term, difference invested at 9 percent a year$13 a month, $3,120 totalAbout $8,690

The invested figures are arithmetic on the stated assumptions: $13 deposited monthly for 20 years, compounded monthly, before tax and before any fees, with no contribution ever missed. Read the table from the refund's side. To finish ahead of the $9,120 refund, the invested difference needs to earn roughly 9.4 percent a year, every year, for two decades. Below that return, the refund wins on pure arithmetic. Above it, investing wins. The refund itself is received tax free in Canada because it is treated as a return of capital, which raises the bar further for any investment path whose returns would be taxed along the way.

So the calculation does not produce the scolding answer many readers expect. It produces a conditional one. A household that would genuinely invest the difference, in a tax sheltered account, at equity like returns, for the full 20 years without interruption, can reasonably buy the cheaper policy. A household that knows the $13 would dissolve into ordinary spending is not choosing between the refund and a portfolio. It is choosing between the refund and nothing, and against nothing, the refund wins easily. That is the entire product: a forced saving mechanism wearing an insurance costume, priced accordingly.

What an early exit returns

The refund's fine print is where the deal sours for anyone whose life changes mid-term. Cancel early and most return of premium arrangements return a reduced percentage of premiums, often 0 percent in the first 5 to 10 years, rising gradually after that. The refund can also be narrower than the headline: rider fees, extra premiums charged for a health rating, and policy fees may sit outside the refundable amount, and a contract can require premiums to have been paid uninterruptedly. A household that might move, separate, or need to cut premiums inside the first decade should price that 0 percent window as part of the product, not as a footnote. The same warning applies in reverse to the investing path: the break-even return assumes 240 unbroken monthly deposits, and a plan abandoned in year three loses to the refund just as surely.

Who the math favours, and who it does not

The refund favours buyers who will hold the policy for the full term, who would not otherwise invest small monthly amounts, and who value a certain outcome over a probable one. It works against buyers whose budget is tight enough that the higher premium shrinks the coverage amount itself, because a $500,000 need met with a $350,000 return of premium policy is not a clever saving strategy, it is an underinsured household. It also works against anyone buying mainly for a short, intense need, such as the first decade of a mortgage, where the early exit terms are at their worst. Buy the coverage amount first. Only once the full need is funded does the refund question deserve its calculation, and by then you have the two real quotes in hand to run it with: your standard premium, your return of premium premium, and the difference between them, tested against the return you would honestly earn.
