Term life laddering in Canada, explained

Thinking it through · 6 min read

Most households do not need the same amount of life insurance for the same number of years. The need is usually largest when the mortgage is new, the children are young, and there are the fewest savings to fall back on — and it shrinks as the mortgage is paid down, the children become independent, and savings grow. Laddering is a way of matching coverage to that shape: instead of one large term policy sized to the peak need and held for the longest period, the household holds two or three smaller term policies with different end dates, so coverage steps down as each obligation ends.

How a ladder works, in one example

Consider a household that calculates a total need of $1,100,000 today, made of three pieces with three different lifespans: a $400,000 mortgage with about 20 years left, $500,000 of income replacement needed for about 15 years while the children are dependent, and a $200,000 education goal that matters for about 10 years. A single-policy approach buys $1,100,000 for the full 20 years. A laddered approach buys three layers — $400,000 for 20 years, $500,000 for 15 years, and $200,000 for 10 years. In the first decade the household carries the full $1,100,000 either way. After year 10 the education layer expires and coverage steps down to $900,000; after year 15 the income layer expires and coverage steps down to $400,000; at year 20 the mortgage layer ends. The protection matches the obligations at each stage instead of staying at the peak long after part of the peak has passed.

Why the ladder usually costs less in total

A shorter term is reliably cheaper than a longer term for the same amount of coverage, because the insurer is exposed for fewer years and the insured is younger, on average, across the period covered. The ladder's saving comes entirely from that fact: the household stops paying long-term prices on needs that were only ever short-term. Part of the saving also arrives automatically — when a layer expires, that premium disappears from the household budget altogether, rather than continuing until the longest policy ends.

Two cautions keep the claim honest. First, the size of the saving depends on the specific insurer, the applicant's age, sex, smoking status, and health class at application, so the honest way to evaluate a ladder is to have both structures — single policy and ladder — quoted side by side before deciding. Second, laddering itself earns no multi-policy discount, and it can run the other way at small sizes: each policy carries its own policy fee, and insurers generally price better per thousand dollars of coverage as the face amount rises. Splitting one large policy into same-length pieces would usually cost more, not less. The layers only pay when their end dates are genuinely different.

A decision framework for building the layers

The structure follows from the obligations, not from product brochures. A workable sequence looks like this:

  1. List each obligation with its end date. Mortgage balance and remaining amortization, the years until the youngest child is financially independent, education funding, and any other time-limited debt. If an obligation has no end date — a dependent who will need lifelong support, for example — it does not belong in a term layer at all.
  2. Size one layer per window. Each layer covers the obligations ending at roughly the same time, in the amounts calculated for them.
  3. Round layers to available term lengths. Terms are sold in standard lengths, so a 17-year need usually becomes a 20-year layer — rounding up, not down, so the coverage outlasts the obligation.
  4. Quote both structures. Ask for the single policy and the ladder priced on the same day, for the same person, so the comparison is real rather than assumed.
  5. Set review triggers, not just dates. Revisit the ladder at each mortgage renewal, when a child finishes school, and after any major income change — the moments when the underlying obligations actually move.

What success looks like: every layer on the list names the obligation it covers and the year that obligation ends, the total coverage in each future decade matches the needs calculated for that decade, and both structures were priced before the choice was made.

The risk that deserves the most respect

A ladder assumes the debts shrink on schedule. Real life interferes: a refinance restarts the amortization, a home equity line gets drawn on, a renewal stretches the mortgage out again. If the mortgage layer expires on the original plan while the actual balance is larger and longer than planned, the household is under-covered precisely where it expected to be finished. That is why the review triggers matter more than the original arithmetic — the ladder is a plan that has to be re-checked against the real balances, not a set-and-forget structure. The same applies in reverse: if obligations end early, a layer can be dropped early and the premium saved, which a single large policy does not allow as cleanly.

Can the layers sit with different insurers?

Yes. Canadians can hold policies from several insurance companies, and there can be a pricing reason to do so — some insurers price 10-year terms competitively while others are stronger at 20 or 30 years. Each insurer generally assesses its own claim under its own contract, and holding another policy does not normally prevent a valid claim from being paid. The trade-offs are administrative: separate applications and underwriting, separate billing cycles, and separate renewal dates to keep track of. One disclosure rule applies in every case: when applying for additional coverage, existing policies and other pending applications must be disclosed when asked. Insurers set total-coverage limits based on income, age, debts, and responsibilities rather than on the number of policies, but undisclosed coverage is a contract problem, not a shortcut.

Questions people commonly ask

Is there a legal limit on how many policies one person can hold? No. The practical limit is underwriting: the total amount across all policies has to be justified by the applicant's financial situation.

Does a permanent policy fit in a ladder? It can sit underneath as a base layer that never expires — for a lifelong need such as final expenses — while term layers handle the temporary needs above it. Permanent coverage costs far more per dollar, so it is generally the base, never the temporary layers.

What if health changes after the ladder is built? Layers already in force continue on their original terms. That is one reason to build the full ladder while healthy rather than planning to add layers later — a later application is underwritten on later health.
