Agatha All Along watch guide: nine episodes, the Witches' Road, and the WandaVision homework

Viewing guide · 9 min read

Agatha Harkness was the breakout villain of WandaVision — the nosy neighbour who turned out to have been pulling strings all along — and in September 2024 she got a series of her own. Agatha All Along is a nine-episode miniseries created by Jac Schaeffer, WandaVision's own showrunner, and it is the second instalment of a planned trilogy that began with WandaVision and is set to close with VisionQuest (s1). It is also a genuinely different kind of Marvel series: a dark-comedy fantasy built around a coven walking a legendary road of trials, made — by the studio's own account — for less money than any other Marvel series, with practical effects doing the work CGI usually does (s1). This guide maps the nine episodes, grades the homework honestly, and keeps the show's two biggest reveals quarantined in one clearly flagged section, so the decision about whether to watch can be made unspoiled.

The shape of the series: nine episodes, bookended by double drops

The facts first. Agatha All Along premiered on Disney+ on September 18, 2024 and ran for nine episodes until October 30, 2024, as part of Phase Five; it was the 11th MCU television series and the first produced under the Marvel Television label (s1). The release shape was unusual and, in hindsight, well judged: two episodes on premiere night, weekly singles through October, then a two-episode finale on October 30 (s2). Episodes run 29 to 49 minutes (s1), so the complete series lands somewhere between roughly four and a half and seven and a half hours depending on where each episode falls in that range — most viewers will finish it in three or four evenings. The premise picks up three years after WandaVision: Agatha, still trapped under Wanda Maximoff's spell in Westview and stripped of her powers, is freed by a mysterious teenager who wants to walk the Witches' Road, a legendary route that grants whoever survives its trials the thing they desire most (s1). Powerless, Agatha does the one thing she has always refused to do — she builds a coven and shares the journey.

The coven is the series' engine, and the casting is stacked. Joe Locke plays the teenager, billed only as Teen; Patti LuPone plays Lilia Calderu, a 450-year-old Sicilian witch whose divination gift makes her experience time out of order; Sasheer Zamata is Jennifer Kale, a bound potions expert; Ali Ahn is Alice Wu-Gulliver, an ex-police officer from a family under a generational curse; Debra Jo Rupp returns as Westview resident Sharon Davis, recruited as a stand-in green witch; and Aubrey Plaza plays Rio Vidal, a witch from Agatha's past (s1). Where WandaVision bent a different sitcom era each week, this series bends a different flavour of witch story — each trial on the Road restyles the coven after a pop-culture coven, from coastal-grandma chic to a 1970s rock band to a 1980s summer-camp sleepover (s1). One early review put the structural point plainly: Schaeffer parsed the emotional arcs out at an episodic pace, so the show works as television rather than as a long film chopped into pieces (s3).

Read the map structurally and the design shows: episodes 1–2 are a two-part premiere that works best in one sitting (s3), episodes 3–5 and 7 are trials that each spotlight one witch, episode 6 stops the journey cold to reframe everything before it, and episodes 8–9 are a finale pair that aired together and plays as one long closing movement. The stakes are real in a way Marvel finales often are not: the coven that walks into episode 3 is not the coven that reaches the end of the Road, and the deaths along the way are permanent (s1). That is worth knowing before choosing this as a cosy rewatch — it is a funnier and warmer series than its body count suggests, but the body count is part of the design.

The forward direction matters more than usual. Agatha All Along is officially the middle chapter of a trilogy, and the announced third series, VisionQuest, builds directly on an outcome this finale establishes — explainers aimed at viewers preparing for VisionQuest conclude that Agatha is not optional background but the setup itself (s1, s5). Grade it the way the site grades every middle chapter: watch it after WandaVision, before VisionQuest, and expect it to be required viewing for exactly one future series rather than for the whole franchise.

What the critics converged on — and the one split

The numbers say hit: an 84% approval rating on Rotten Tomatoes from 216 critic reviews, with a consensus praising Hahn backed by a coven of memorable performers in a spinoff that concocts its own distinct brew, and a 66 out of 100 on Metacritic, in the generally favourable band (s1). The praise concentrated in three places. Hahn's performance drew a Golden Globe nomination and the series earned three Primetime Emmy nominations (s1). Rolling Stone's Alan Sepinwall singled out the same structural virtue the early reviews did — the series works as a proper TV show rather than a chopped-up movie (s1) — and The Guardian's Lucy Mangan awarded it five out of five, calling it royally entertaining as it slipped from comedy to tragedy (s1). The split was about the middle: the Seattle Times' Rob Owen found the trial format threatening to repeat itself episode after episode despite the cast's chemistry, and USA Today's Kelly Lawler judged the tone uneven, a show trying to do too many things at once (s1). Both still centred the performances as the reason to watch — which tells a deciding viewer exactly what kind of risk this series is: the structure may wobble; the coven does not.

FAQ

How long is Agatha All Along in total? Nine episodes at 29–49 minutes each (s1) — plan three or four evenings, with the two-part premiere and the two-part finale each watched in a single sitting.

Will there be a season 2? It was made as a miniseries, and the story continues by design rather than by renewal: the trilogy closes with VisionQuest, which picks up threads this finale establishes (s1, s5).

Is Agatha All Along scary? It is a dark comedy with genuine horror beats — a Marvel brand of scary with deadly stakes, in the studio's own framing (s1). Witches die on this road; treat the trials as horror episodes with jokes, not the reverse.

Do I need the Darkhold explained? No. The book matters as history — it is what Agatha was said to have traded her child for, the rumour episode 5 weaponises and the finale recontextualises (s1) — but the series carries everything it needs on screen.
