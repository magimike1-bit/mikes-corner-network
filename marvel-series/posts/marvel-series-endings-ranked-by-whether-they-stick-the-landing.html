Marvel series endings, ranked by whether they stick the landing

Ranking · 7 min read

5.5 and 5.7. Those are the IMDb scores for the finales of Secret Invasion (“Home”) and She-Hulk: Attorney at Law (“Whose Show Is This”) — the two lowest numbers in the one published ranking that bothers to anchor itself to scores at all. Most finale rankings argue from impressions instead: a finale was “divisive,” a speech “didn't land.” Both approaches miss the same second question. A finale can be well received and still not end anything, because the show carried on the next season. This ranking applies two tests together — documented reception, and whether the ending actually closes the story it was handed — and it scores true series endings separately from season finales of continuing shows, because judging a mid-story chapter break as if it were a conclusion is the original sin of this genre of list.

The rubric: reception plus closure

The reception half uses published evidence, not vibes: the IMDb episode scores compiled in StartEfacts' finale ranking, the Rotten Tomatoes figure quoted in ScreenRant's ranking, and the verdict language of the critics who compiled the lists. The closure half asks one plain question — does this ending resolve the show's own story, or does it pause it? A season finale that pauses well can still rank highly inside its own class. It simply does not get to compete with an ending that had to carry the whole series out the door.

Spoiler discipline applies throughout: endings are discussed by outcome and reception, not by plot detail, and nothing below reveals a final-scene twist.

True endings that land

At the top sits Loki's second-season ending, which one catalogue-wide ranking calls one of the best finales in MCU history, with real closure. That pairing — top-tier reception language plus an ending that resolves the show's own story rather than teeing up the next chapter — is exactly what the rubric rewards, and no other documented ending in the research for this piece matches both halves at once.

Ms. Marvel's finale sits a tier below: mid-table in the finale rankings, lifted — in the compilers' own account — by the mutant reveal and the Captain Marvel cameo. That is a landing assisted by surprise guests rather than carried purely by the story's own resolution, which is why it places in the middle of the true-endings class rather than at its top. It ends its series, it was received respectably, and its biggest documented boost came from what it set up rather than what it settled.

True endings that fumble

The documented bottom of the table is not close. Inhumans' finale sits last in ScreenRant's ranking of every live-action Marvel finale, with the series itself at 11 percent on Rotten Tomatoes — the reception floor for the whole catalogue. Secret Invasion's finale, “Home,” holds an IMDb score of 5.5, the lowest figure in the score-anchored ranking. She-Hulk's finale scores 5.7 on the same scale and is described in ScreenRant's ranking as divisive for breaking the fourth wall to confront Kevin Feige — a swing that split its audience straight down the middle.

Season finales are a different sport

Two documented cases show why the classes must stay separate. The Falcon and the Winter Soldier's finale was faulted in the rankings for a speech that doesn't land — while its suit reveal was praised in the same breath. Scored as a series ending, that reads as a fumble. Scored as what it actually was — the end of a season whose story continued into the films — the praised half matters more, because the chapter's job was to hand its hero forward, not to close his story. Moon Knight's finale makes the mirror-image point: the rankings note that it skipped the hero-versus-Harrow fight viewers expected. A continuing show can defer a confrontation; a true ending that skips its own climax has nowhere to defer to.

How to use this ranking

Chasing a satisfying complete story: start with the top of the true-endings class — the Loki conclusion is the documented standout. Catching up on a continuing show: judge its season finales by the season-finale standard — did the chapter hand the story forward cleanly — and do not let a mid-table season ending put you off a series that was never trying to conclude. Deciding what to skip: the bottom of the table is evidence-based, not taste-based: an 11 percent series score and finale scores of 5.5 and 5.7 are the weakest documented receptions in the catalogue, so those endings are the safest skips for a viewer short on time.
