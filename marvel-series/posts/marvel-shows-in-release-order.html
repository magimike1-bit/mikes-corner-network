Marvel shows in release order

Viewing guide · 9 min read

Release order is the least glamorous way to watch Marvel television, and for most people it is also the best. It is the order audiences actually experienced: characters arrive when they were introduced, crossovers land when they were meant to land, and no later series quietly spoils an earlier one. The problem is scale. Marvel TV now spans a network era that began in 2013, a street-level streaming era, and a Disney+ era in which the series became direct chapters of the film story. A simple list of titles does not answer the question most viewers are really asking, which is how much time each era demands and whether the whole thing has to be watched at all.

This guide answers that second question honestly. Release order is presented era by era, with season and episode counts where sources confirm them, a comparison against in-universe timeline order, and a decision framework at the end. Spoilers are kept to premises and release facts only. Dates and counts below are checked as of October 13, 2026, against the sources cited in the notes at the foot of the page.

Why release order is the safest default

Release order protects introductions. When Agents of S.H.I.E.L.D. premiered on September 24, 2013, it was a companion to films that were still unfolding, and later seasons assume that gradual familiarity. When Daredevil arrived on April 10, 2015, it opened a separate, grittier corner that only later folded into a team-up. When WandaVision opened the Disney+ era on January 15, 2021, its mystery depended on viewers not yet knowing how later series would widen the story. Watching in release order preserves those intended reveals without any extra effort from the viewer.

Timeline order does the opposite job. Marvel's own Disney+ timeline, published by Marvel.com, rearranges films and series into in-universe chronology, beginning with Eyes of Wakanda and Captain America: The First Avenger and running through later titles including Wonder Man and Daredevil: Born Again. That is a fascinating second-watch experiment, and a poor first watch: prequels made later routinely reference events a first-time viewer has not seen yet, and animated or period stories placed early in the timeline were written for audiences who already knew the modern characters.

There is also a practical reason to default to release order. Streaming menus, episode numbering, and almost every published guide are organised by release. Following that order means never having to pause a series mid-season to insert another show, except in the one corner where interleaving genuinely matters, the Defenders Saga, which has its own dedicated guide on this site.

The network and early streaming era, 2013 to 2019

The first era is the longest and the most optional. Agents of S.H.I.E.L.D. ran for seven seasons and 136 episodes from September 24, 2013 to August 12, 2020. That single commitment is larger than the entire Disney+ era combined, which is exactly the kind of fact a bare title list hides. Agent Carter followed with two seasons and 18 episodes from January 6, 2015 to March 1, 2016, a compact period piece that rewards film fans but does not gate anything later.

In parallel, the Netflix-produced corner began with Daredevil in 2015 and continued through Jessica Jones, Luke Cage, Iron Fist, the team-up The Defenders, and The Punisher. TheWrap's season-level order interleaves those series rather than treating each show as a single block, because their seasons overlapped and crossed over. Those shows have since moved to Disney+, so a release-order watch no longer requires switching services. The honest summary of this era is that it contains some of the strongest Marvel television ever made and also the largest amount of television a newcomer can safely postpone. Nothing in the Disney+ era requires finishing 136 episodes of network TV first.

A useful way to budget the era is by intent. Completionists get the full seven seasons of S.H.I.E.L.D. plus the street-level saga. Everyone else should treat the era as a menu: pick the corner that matches a preferred tone, crime drama, noir, or spy-fi, and leave the rest for later. Release order still applies inside whichever corner is chosen; it simply does not demand every corner.

The Disney+ era in release order

The Disney+ era is shorter, denser, and far more interconnected. It opened with WandaVision, nine episodes released from January 15 to March 5, 2021, followed by The Falcon and the Winter Soldier from March 19 to April 23, 2021, then Loki, which began on June 9, 2021, and the animated What If...?, which began on August 11, 2021 with nine episodes. Hawkeye closed the first year, running six episodes from November 24 to December 29, 2021.

The middle years widened the range rather than simply adding sequels. Secret Invasion ran six episodes from June 21 to July 26, 2023. Echo arrived as a five-episode release on January 9, 2024, and also served as a bridge back toward the street-level characters. Agatha All Along, the WandaVision spin-off centred on Agatha Harkness, premiered with two episodes on September 18, 2024 and ran nine episodes to October 30, 2024, according to ComicBook's episode guide. In 2025, Daredevil: Born Again ran nine episodes from March 4 to April 15, and Ironheart followed with six episodes from June 24 to July 1.

Two patterns matter for planning. First, Disney+ seasons are short: five to nine episodes is the normal range in the confirmed examples above, compared with thirteen-episode seasons in the earlier streaming corner and very long network seasons before that. Second, spin-offs in this era are direct continuations, so release order doubles as story order for linked pairs such as WandaVision and Agatha All Along. Skipping ahead to a spin-off is the one release-order shortcut that reliably backfires.

The table is the whole argument in miniature. Timeline order is not wrong; it is specialised. It answers a continuity question, not a viewing question, and it becomes more interesting the more of the story a viewer already knows. Release order answers the viewing question directly, which is why it remains the recommendation for anyone starting now, in late 2026, with a decade of television waiting.

How long each era actually takes

Exact runtimes vary by episode, so this guide counts episodes rather than inventing hour totals. The network headline is 136 episodes for Agents of S.H.I.E.L.D. alone, plus 18 for Agent Carter. The Disney+ examples confirmed above are strikingly smaller: nine episodes for WandaVision, six for Hawkeye, six for Secret Invasion, five for Echo, nine for Agatha All Along, nine for Born Again, and six for Ironheart. Added together, those seven Disney+ series total 50 episodes, barely more than a third of the S.H.I.E.L.D. commitment on its own.

That arithmetic changes the emotional shape of a catch-up. The supposedly overwhelming modern era is, in episode terms, the manageable part. The daunting part is the older network era, which also happens to be the most skippable. A realistic plan is therefore not to start at 2013 and grind forward, but to start with the Disney+ era in release order, then go back for the older corners as interest-led side quests. That is still release order, applied era by era, and it is the approach the decision framework below formalises.

A decision framework for using this list

Step 1: name the goal. Catching up for current series, sampling the best shows, or completing everything are three different projects with three different lists. Step 2: pick an era, not a year. Start with the Disney+ era for current-story relevance, the street-level corner for grounded crime drama, or the network era only with eyes open about its size. Step 3: stay in release order inside the era. Linked spin-offs and crossovers assume it. Step 4: promote, do not restart. When an era finishes, add the next one in release order rather than rewatching; timeline order is the reward for a second pass, not a requirement for the first.

For readers who want a narrower starting point, the newcomer picks and the by-mood guide on this site cut the list down by taste instead of date. For readers who want the street-level corner done properly, the Defenders Saga guide gives the season-by-season interleave. Release order is the spine; those guides are the shortcuts that still respect it.

Frequently asked questions

Do the films have to be watched between series? For a series-only catch-up, no. Films deepen several Disney+ series, but each series reintroduces what it needs, and release order for the shows alone remains coherent. Is timeline order ever the right first choice? Only for viewers who actively prefer chronology over introductions and accept the spoiler trade-off described above. Where do animated series fit? Mostly as standalone or parallel viewing; the animated guide on this site maps which ones connect to the live-action story and which stand alone.
