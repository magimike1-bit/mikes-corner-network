The Marvel shows that changed the movies most

Viewing guide · 7 min read

Every Marvel show is described as essential now. Almost none of them are — and the difference is testable. A show changed the movies if a film's plot actually breaks without it: a character's motivation stops making sense, a villain arrives from nowhere, a central event has no cause. A show that merely shares characters with the films, or that a studio executive calls foundational in an interview, has not passed that test until the dependency shows up on screen. Applying it film by film produces a shorter, stranger list than the marketing suggests — and one case where the claimed influence and the visible influence point in opposite directions.

The test: does the film break without the show?

Three grades of connection get confused constantly. Plot dependency is the strongest: the film assumes the show happened. Character introduction is weaker: the show debuted someone the films later used, but the film can introduce them again in a line of dialogue. Homework is the weakest of all: watching the show adds flavour, and the film works perfectly without it. Only the first grade counts here. Claimed influence — what producers say a show made possible — is recorded, but labelled as claimed until a film visibly depends on it.

WandaVision: the clearest case

WandaVision passes the test outright. It was the first Disney+ show directly connected to the movies, built around film leads, and it explains Wanda's turn in Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness — the film's central character shift is the show's storyline arriving on the big screen. A viewer who skips the series meets a Wanda whose damage has no on-screen cause in the film itself. That is plot dependency by definition, and it is why this series, the very first of the Disney+ era, still anchors the list. Its reach extended sideways as well as forward: it later spawned its own spin-off, Agatha All Along, which is influence inside television rather than on the films, but it underlines how much story the films' owner kept pulling out of this one series.

Loki: claimed influence vs visible influence

Loki is the interesting argument. The claimed influence is maximal and comes from the top: Kevin Feige told Marvel.com that Loki and Sylvie's ending “allowed all of this to be possible” — He Who Remains' death enabling both the No Way Home spell and Multiverse of Madness. There is also one hard, visible dependency: Loki introduced He Who Remains, leading directly to Kang in Ant-Man and the Wasp: Quantumania. A major film villain's lineage starts in this show.

And yet the same analysis that quotes Feige makes the counter-observation: on screen, viewers would miss little without having seen Loki. The multiverse films re-establish what they need as they go. So Loki scores high on claimed influence, scores a genuine character-introduction dependency with Kang, and scores surprisingly low on strict plot dependency — the films bend around its absence. One later assessment singles it out as the show that still matters for Doomsday, which may yet move it up this ranking; at the time of the research for this piece, that remains a claim about a future film, not a visible dependency in a released one.

The supporting case

The Falcon and the Winter Soldier connects forward to Captain America: Brave New World: the film inherits the show's endpoint for its hero. That is a real hand-off — the movie's starting position was set on television — but it is closer to character introduction than to a plot that breaks. The film can state its hero's status in a scene; what it cannot do is give the audience the journey, which is a loss of depth rather than a break in comprehension.

The pre-Disney+ verdict

The ABC and Netflix-era shows are usually excluded from this argument entirely, which is its own kind of answer — but exclusion is not a test result, so they were run through the same test for this piece. None of the film-by-film dependencies traced in the research comes from that era. No film's plot was found that breaks without Agents of S.H.I.E.L.D. or a Defenders series; those shows ran parallel to the films rather than feeding them. That does not make them lesser television. It makes them television the films were built to survive without — the opposite structural position from WandaVision.

Ranked: changed the movies, honestly

1. WandaVision — the only clear plot dependency: a film's central turn is unexplained without it. 2. Loki — a real villain lineage into Quantumania, plus the largest claimed influence in the franchise, discounted because the films visibly cope without it. 3. The Falcon and the Winter Soldier — a genuine hand-off into Brave New World, at the character level rather than the plot level. Everything else, from every era, is homework: rewarding, sometimes excellent, and structurally optional. For a viewer catching up before a film release, that ranking is the practical takeaway — watch in that order, and stop wherever time runs out, because the films below the top line were designed to forgive you.
