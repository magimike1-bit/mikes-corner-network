WandaVision: the sitcom eras, episode by episode

Viewing guide · 9 min read

Most Marvel series can be described in one sentence about plot. WandaVision needs a second sentence about format, because the format is the plot device: nine episodes released on Disney+ in 2021, opening Marvel's Phase Four, in which Wanda Maximoff and Vision appear to live in the suburb of Westview while the television around them keeps changing decade. Each sitcom episode advances roughly one era of American TV comedy, and the cracks in that imitation — a dinner guest who will not stop asking questions, a neighbour who seems to know too much — are where the real story leaks through. Guides usually list the eras and stop. This guide does the three things those lists omit: it maps every episode to its era and its named inspirations with the sources separated, it explains what episode 4 changes about how the first three should be read, and it gives a decision framework for the two viewers who ask most often — the Marvel completist wondering whether this oddity is essential, and the sitcom fan wondering whether the superhero ending ruins the experiment.

The episode-by-episode era map

The verified spine of the series is simple. The nine episodes were released between January 15 and March 5, 2021, beginning with a double premiere — “Filmed Before a Live Studio Audience” and “Don't Touch That Dial” on January 15 — and continuing weekly through “Now in Color,” “We Interrupt This Program,” “On a Very Special Episode…,” “All-New Halloween Spooktacular!,” “Breaking the Fourth Wall,” “Previously On,” and “The Series Finale” on March 5. That weekly cadence mattered: unlike a binge drop, the era-hopping was experienced as a slow reveal, one television decade per week.

Two cautions belong with any era map, and both come from the sources themselves. First, the boundaries are blends, not museum labels: ScreenRant's era-by-era account puts the first episode anywhere from 1951 to 1966 because it mixes I Love Lucy and Dick Van Dyke elements, and the second episode's Bewitched and Jeannie influences run into the early 1970s. Second, episode 4 is the exception that explains the rule — era trackers note it has no new sitcom because it steps outside the imitation entirely. Treating the map as approximate homage rather than exact period reconstruction is not a downgrade; it is how the show was built, recreating the aesthetics and directorial styles of each era rather than restaging specific broadcasts.

What the sitcom layer is actually doing

The premise underneath the homage is documented plainly in reference summaries of the series: Wanda and Vision live an apparently idyllic suburban life in Westview, but Vision died at the hands of Thanos in Avengers: Infinity War — so his cheerful presence in a 1950s kitchen is itself the mystery. As the episodes progress, cracks in the facade reveal that Wanda has trapped the town inside a reality-warping field known as the Hex, built to cope with immense loss. That is why the eras keep advancing whether the characters age normally or not, and why the in-episode commercials matter: episode guides that track them note that each fake advertisement appears to be based on a traumatic event in Wanda's life, turning the most disposable part of a sitcom — the ad break — into a compressed biography.

This is also why the series is routinely described as a landmark shift for the franchise: a move away from the blockbuster formula toward a high-concept, character-driven exploration of grief and nostalgia, performed by Elizabeth Olsen and Paul Bettany. A viewer who treats the first episodes as a puzzle box to be solved will find them slow; a viewer who treats them as a grief story wearing sitcom costumes will find the pacing is the point. Neither reading is wrong, but they produce opposite verdicts on the same three episodes, which is most of why this series divides audiences more than any other Marvel show of its size.

What to watch before it — and what it sets up

The homework is unusually light for a series this connected. The essential prior knowledge is a single fact from Infinity War — that Vision died — plus a general sense of who Wanda is; the series itself supplies everything else, including its own mythology, through the outside-Westview material that begins in episode 4. Viewers who want the full context can add Avengers: Endgame, since the series is set after it, but nothing in the sitcom episodes requires scene-level recall of any film.

The forward connections are documented and specific. Reference accounts note that several characters' storylines continued directly into other projects: Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness (2022) for Wanda's arc, the miniseries Agatha All Along (2024) for Westview's most memorable neighbour, and a further miniseries, VisionQuest (2026), for Vision's side of the story. That spread is the honest answer to “does it matter?”: the series is self-contained as a viewing experience, and simultaneously one of the most consequential nine-episode runs in the catalogue, because three separate follow-ups grow out of it. Skipping it does not break any film's plot — each follow-up re-establishes what it needs — but viewers who enjoy recognising where a character's damage came from will get more from all three with this watched first.

Who should watch it, who should sample it first

Watch it in full if: the grief-through-genre premise appeals, or the follow-ups above are on the list — nine episodes is a contained commitment with an outsized downstream payoff. Watch it if: classic sitcoms are a pleasure in themselves; the homage work — aspect ratios, filming techniques, era-specific tropes — is the substance of the early episodes, not decoration. Sample first if: superhero action is the main draw. The fair test is episodes 1, 2 and 4: the two era episodes establish the experiment, and episode 4 reveals the frame around it. A viewer unmoved by all three has learned, cheaply, that the series' pleasures are not theirs. Skip it if: an unresolved-feeling, format-first opening is a dealbreaker regardless of payoff — the finale received more mixed reactions than the run as a whole even among critics who praised the homages, the themes and the performances, so the ending does not convert sceptics by force.

Frequently asked questions

How many episodes is WandaVision? Nine, released weekly on Disney+ from January 15 to March 5, 2021, opening Phase Four. Do the eras follow real sitcoms? Each episode homages named series — The Dick Van Dyke Show and I Love Lucy at the start, through Bewitched, The Brady Bunch, Family Ties and Malcolm in the Middle, to a Modern Family-style mockumentary — but as blends, not exact recreations. Is Vision alive in it? That is the central mystery: Vision died in Avengers: Infinity War, and the series is set after Endgame in Westview, inside the reality-warping Hex. What should be watched after? Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness (2022), Agatha All Along (2024) and VisionQuest (2026) continue its characters' storylines.
