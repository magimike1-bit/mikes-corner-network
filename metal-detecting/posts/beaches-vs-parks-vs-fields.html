Beaches vs. parks vs. fields

Getting started · 6 min read

Once you've picked a machine and sorted your permissions, the next question is where to point it. Beaches, parks, and fields are the three classic beginner grounds, and they behave very differently: digging, trash, targets, and technique all change. Here's an honest comparison to help you choose your first hunts.

Beaches: the best classroom for beginners

Dry sand is the easiest digging you'll ever do. A simple scoop or even a hand trowel cuts through it, targets are shallow, and you can cover a lot of ground in an hour. The targets are modern — coins, jewelry lost in summer, keys, phone cases — which means you'll find things quickly. Early success matters: it teaches you what a good signal sounds like and keeps you coming back.

The wet sand near the waterline is more demanding. Saltwater sand is heavily mineralized, which can overwhelm a basic detector — this is why checking a machine's saltwater suitability matters before you buy. If your machine handles it, the reward is real: heavier items like rings settle in the wet sand and wait there. Just stay aware of the tides, keep your control box dry, and never turn your back on the water. In winter, beaches can be almost empty of targets after storms rearrange the sand; in summer they're re-stocked daily by swimmers.

Trash is manageable on beaches: bottle caps and pull tabs mostly, plus the occasional fishing gear near piers. A scoop with a mesh bottom makes recovery fast — shake out the sand and the target stays behind. If you hunt early morning, you'll have the sand to yourself and the finds from the previous day.

Parks: convenient, trashy, and full of coins

Parks are where most beginners spend their first months, and for good reason: they're close to home, permission is often straightforward, and decades of lost coins sit in the turf around benches, paths, and playground edges. Tot lots (playgrounds with wood-chip or sand surfaces) are a beginner favorite — soft digging and a constant turnover of dropped change.

The downside is trash. Decades of picnics and sports have buried a layer of pull tabs, bottle caps, foil wrappers, and can slaw (shredded aluminum from mowers hitting cans) that sounds exactly like a coin to a beginner's ear. This is frustrating but educational: parks force you to learn what signals are worth digging. Dig more at first, not less — skipping every iffy signal teaches you nothing.

Digging technique matters more here than anywhere else. Parks departments notice scars, and one messy detectorist can get the hobby banned from a park. Learn to cut a clean plug, pop the target, and press the turf back so it looks undisturbed. It takes practice; practice it on your own lawn first if you have one.

Fields: patience, permission, and the oldest finds

Fields — pasture, stubble, and plowed farmland — are where the oldest finds live. Coins from past centuries, buttons, buckles, and tools all wait in ground that's been worked for generations. But fields are advanced hunting in disguise. You can't hunt a field without the farmer's explicit permission, full stop, and targets are often deeper, sparser, and harder to date than anything you'll find in a park.

Each type of field hunts differently. Pasture is kind on your digging tool but the thick turf hides deep targets; plowed fields expose everything but the turned soil is full of iron and fragments that will test your patience. Stubble fields are the middle ground — just watch your feet. Never detect a growing crop, and time your hunts to the farmer's schedule, not yours.

For a beginner, fields are a poor first choice: you'll walk a long way between targets and won't yet have the ear to distinguish a worn old coin from iron. Come back to fields after a season of parks and beaches, when you've learned to read signals and dig cleanly.

Where should you start?

Dry sand first, park turf second, fields later. The beach teaches you signal and recovery speed with almost no digging effort; the park teaches you target selection and clean digging under real conditions; the field rewards the patience and ear you've built along the way. Hunt whatever's legal and nearby rather than driving two hours to the "perfect" site — the first hundred hours matter more than the first hundred targets.

One more thing applies everywhere: take the trash with you. Every bottle cap you remove is one less false signal on your next visit, and it's the habit that keeps all three of these grounds open to detectorists.
