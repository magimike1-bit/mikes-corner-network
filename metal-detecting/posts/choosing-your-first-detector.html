Choosing your first detector

Getting started · 6 min read

Shopping for your first metal detector is confusing because every manufacturer claims theirs goes deeper, finds more, and is easier to use than anyone else's. It doesn't help that most advice online is really advertising. Here's how to cut through it: what actually matters for a beginner, in roughly the order of importance.

Start simple, not cheap

There is an important difference between a simple machine and a bad one. A good beginner detector has few controls — a volume knob, a discrimination control, maybe a sensitivity knob — and it beeps when it finds metal. That's it. A complicated machine with twenty settings is not more capable in beginner hands; it's a machine you will run on the factory defaults for a year while resenting it.

Entry-level machines from established manufacturers are genuinely good instruments. The extra money in higher-end models buys depth, features for difficult ground, and speed on sites littered with trash — advantages that only matter once you know how to hunt. Your first machine should be easy to understand in an afternoon, not something you need to study like a pilot's license.

Discrimination and target ID

Almost every modern detector can ignore iron to some degree — this is called discrimination. A simple detector lets you set a level: turn it low and you hear everything (including nails); turn it up and the machine stays silent over iron but may also ignore good targets. For your first machine, you want a machine that lets you adjust this with one knob, not a machine with thirty separate rejection slots you'll never touch.

Many machines also show a target ID — a number or a category like "coin" or "iron" that guesses what the target might be. Treat these as hints, not answers. No machine can see through the ground; target IDs get fooled by rusted iron, overlapping targets, and odd angles all the time. A beginner who digs everything teaches their ear faster than a beginner who trusts the screen.

Ground balance: what it means and what you need

Ground balance compensates for the natural minerals in soil, which detectors otherwise read as one giant false target. Some machines have fixed ground balance (preset at the factory — fine for mild soil), some are automatic, and some let you set it manually. For your first machine, preset or automatic is the right choice. Manual ground balance is a genuinely useful skill, but it's one more thing to get wrong while you're learning to swing, dig, and identify targets.

If you plan to hunt on saltwater beaches, though, look for a machine described as suitable for saltwater use. Wet ocean sand is heavily mineralized and overwhelms many basic machines — check this specifically before buying, because it's the most common "my detector doesn't work" complaint from new beach hunters.

Coil size, weight, and balance

The coil — the round or oval disc at the end of the shaft — determines the detection area. The standard coil that ships with a machine, usually around 8 to 10 inches across, is the right one for a beginner. Big coils see more ground and go a bit deeper; small coils separate targets in trashy spots. You'll learn which you want only after you've hunted a while. Don't buy extra coils up front.

Weight matters more than most beginners expect. You'll swing that coil for hours. A machine that feels fine in the shop can feel like a brick after ninety minutes on a beach. If you can, hold the machine assembled, with the coil out in front as if you were sweeping, before you buy. A well-balanced light machine beats a heavy "deeper" one you leave in the closet.

Waterproofing and headphones

Most beginner machines are not fully waterproof — the coil can be submerged but the control box cannot, or the whole machine is only splash-resistant. That's fine for land hunting. Just be honest with yourself about where you'll hunt: rain happens, coils get dunked in puddles, and one careless slip at a riverbank ruins a non-waterproof control box. If you know you'll be around water, buy a waterproof machine rather than hoping yours survives.

A headphone jack matters more than you'd think. Headphones let you hear faint signals in wind and crowds, and they save your batteries. Any machine without one is a poor pick for a beginner.

Buying used and buying smart

A used machine from an established manufacturer is an excellent first detector, provided it works — detectors don't wear out in ways that matter unless they've been abused. Buy from a dealer or a local detecting club where someone can show you it working. Avoid obscure brands with no spare parts and no manual available online.

One last thing: the dealer who sells you the machine is worth something. A local shop or a helpful online retailer that answers questions will save you more frustration than any feature. Buy the machine from someone who wants you to succeed at the hobby, and don't overthink the choice — the first hundred hours of hunting matter far more than the first hundred dollars of equipment.
