Cleaning and identifying your finds

Finds · 6 min read

Pulling a target out of the ground is only half the fun — figuring out what it is, and cleaning it so you can actually see it, is the other half. It's also where beginners do the most damage: aggressive cleaning has ruined more interesting finds than all the plows in the county. The rule is simple: you can always clean more later, but you can never un-clean.

The first step is documentation

Before you clean anything, photograph it as found — both sides, in good light, with something for scale like a coin you know. Note where and when you found it. A blurry photo and a vague "found it somewhere near the park" is how interesting objects lose their story. If it turns out to be something old or unusual, the context of the find is part of what makes it valuable to researchers.

Bag delicate finds separately. Small objects rub against each other in a finds pouch and lose their details fast. A few small zip bags or a compartmented box costs almost nothing and preserves the crisp edges that identify coins and tokens.

Start with the gentlest method

For most finds, a soak in plain water followed by a soft toothbrush is the right first move — and often the last one needed. Work under running water, be gentle, and stop as soon as you can see the object's details. Dry brass and copper items can often be improved with nothing more aggressive than this.

Never put a find in your mouth to "clean" it, no matter how many people on forums swear by it — soil carries bacteria and old metal can carry worse. Wear gloves if you have cuts on your hands, and wash up after handling soil-covered objects.

Old coins: less is more

Coins with any potential age or collector interest should be treated with special care. Collectors value original surfaces and patina — the toning that builds up over decades — and harsh cleaning destroys it along with the value. Don't polish old coins, don't put them in acid or commercial dip unless you're willing to accept the loss of collector value, and never scrub one with anything abrasive.

If you can't identify a coin by sight, that's fine — photograph it and research it first (see below). If you later decide it's a common modern coin and you just want it presentable, then and only then consider stronger cleaning. Cleaning decisions are reversible in one direction only.

Identifying coins

Modern coins are easy: the date and denomination are usually visible after a rinse. Older or worn coins take more work. Look for any legible text, a portrait profile, or distinctive designs, and search those details online alongside terms like "coin identification." Coin collector forums and social media groups are full of people who enjoy identifying mystery finds — post clear photos of both sides and your approximate region.

Reference books on coins of your country, available at most libraries, are worth an afternoon if you're finding older coinage regularly. Once you've identified a coin yourself a few times, you'll start recognizing them in the field before you even clean them.

Identifying other finds

Buttons, buckles, tokens, tools, and toys all follow the same identification recipe: note the material, any markings or maker's stamps, the style of design, and where you found it. Maker's marks are the single best clue — a name or logo can take you straight to a manufacturer and a date range.

Your local detecting club is the fastest identification resource you'll ever find. Club members have seen thousands of finds and recognize common objects instantly; many clubs also have reference libraries. Local museums and historical societies can help with objects that look genuinely old, and they'll appreciate being consulted — though expect them to be interested in the find itself if it's significant.

When a find might be important

Sometimes you'll dig something that feels like more than a curio: very old coins in quantity, objects with museum-quality craftsmanship, or anything that might be from a historically significant site. In many jurisdictions, certain categories of find — hoards, burials, precious-metal objects above an age threshold — have legal reporting requirements. Know your local law before this happens, not after. If in doubt, contact your local museum or archaeology office and ask; they would far rather hear about a false alarm than about a find that vanished into a drawer.
