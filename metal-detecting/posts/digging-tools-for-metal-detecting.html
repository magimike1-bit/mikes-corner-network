Digging tools for metal detecting: hand digger, shovel, or sand scoop

Gear & technique · 10 min read

The detector finds the target; the digging tool decides what the ground looks like afterwards — and, on many sites, whether you get invited back. Beginner advice usually arrives as a list of brand names, which skips the actual decision: there are three classes of digging tool, each belongs at a different kind of site, and the wrong class in the wrong place is a problem even when the digging itself is allowed. This guide works in the order the decision should be made — permission and surface first, soil second, target depth third — compares the three classes on their published specifications rather than their reputations, and finishes with the plug procedure a hand digger is specifically shaped to perform.

Start with permission and surface, not steel

MetalDetector.com's digging etiquette guide sets the baseline every tool choice sits under: filling all holes is imperative, and the ground should look intact when you leave. That single standard quietly rules tools in and out. A long, aggressive shovel is an asset in a ploughed field and a liability on manicured park turf — a trade-press feature on digging tools puts it bluntly: aggressive detecting shovels are too tough on public places, while fields and woods are exactly where the extra muscle is needed. The same source notes the hand digger's advantage on turf: it cuts a hinged plug that can be replaced without killing the grass. So the first question is never "which tool is best?" It is "what will this surface, and whoever granted permission for it, accept?" A smaller digging tool also makes smaller holes and less landscape damage, and there is a second, selfish reason to keep holes small and cuts clean: hasty digging can scratch or damage the find itself.

Two spec lessons hide in that table. First, hand diggers from competing makers converge on nearly the same tool — a blade around 7 to 7.5 inches on a 12-inch overall length — because the plug work dictates the size, not the marketing. When two brands agree that closely, buy on steel, edge and sheath rather than on claims. Second, blade width is a working measurement, not a detail: the Lesche's blade is 1.75 inches wide, the Sampson's is 4 inches wide, and the width of the blade is the width of the cut you make in the turf. Narrow blades make narrow, discreet plugs; wide blades move real soil.

Match the tool to the ground: a decision framework

MetalDetector.com's retrieval guide frames the underlying rule: soil conditions and target depth determine the tools needed, and beach retrieval is totally different from digging hard-packed loam or clay. Regton's guide adds the location dimension. Worked together, the decision runs like this:

  1. Manicured turf — parks, yards, sports fields. Hand digger, full stop. It is the only class built to cut a small hinged plug and leave the surface intact, it rides in a belt sheath, and it pairs with a pinpointer for controlled recovery of smaller plugs. If a site's rules or a landowner's comfort exclude digging tools entirely, no tool choice fixes that — the permission question comes first, always.
  2. Fields, pasture and woodland. A long-handled detecting shovel earns its carry here: Regton recommends a long-handled spade for cutting neat plugs in pasture without damaging the grass, and notes that in woodland a smaller hand digger still manoeuvres better around roots and uneven ground — which is why many field hunters carry the shovel for the plug and the hand digger for the finish. Really old, deeply buried relics are the case MetalDetector.com reserves for a strong, durable shovel.
  3. Beaches and loose sand. Put both cutting tools away. Sand is much easier to dig, and most detectorists swap the spade for a dedicated sand scoop, which sifts sand quickly and recovers the target without excessive digging. A scoop in turf would be useless; a shovel in dry sand is nearly as pointless, collapsing the hole faster than you can empty it.
  4. Depth as the tiebreaker. Within soil, depth settles hand digger versus shovel. The trade-press guidance puts hand diggers at their best on average-depth targets — less than about 6 inches — and warns that deeper targets mean much more work with a hand tool and a greater risk of damaging the turf in the process. Consistently deep targets are the shovel's argument; consistently shallow ones are the hand digger's.

The plug procedure a hand digger is built for

The serrated edge, the blade guard and the sheath all exist for one procedure: the flap, or hinged-plug, method that MetalDetector.com publishes as the preferred way to retrieve a coin or small object under sod.

  1. Cut three sides of a square around the target with the serrated edge, going about 3 to 4 inches deep. The uncut fourth side is the hinge — some roots stay attached and alive.
  2. Lift the flap with the tool to expose the hole, folding it back on its hinge rather than tearing it free.
  3. Search the flap first, then the hole, with a pinpointer or probe. If the target is not visible, check the flap itself before digging deeper — shallow targets often come up inside the plug.
  4. Keep loose dirt on a towel or a sheet of plastic beside the hole as you work deeper, so every spoonful can be poured straight back in afterwards instead of scattered across the grass.
  5. Replace the plug and step firmly down on it with your foot. Then pour back any remaining loose dirt first if you dug past the plug, so the flap sits at its original level.

What success looks like: from standing height, the spot is indistinguishable from the turf around it — no loose soil halo, no scalped patch, the flap sitting flush. If a passer-by could point to where you dug, the procedure, not the site, is what needs work.

Care, carry, and the garden-tool question

Two practical points close the decision. Carry is part of the design: both verified hand diggers ship with belt sheaths — a nylon sheath with the Garrett, a Cordura sheath with a plastic insert protecting it from the serrated edge on the Lesche — because a digging tool you have to go back to a bag for is a tool that tempts you to improvise with something worse. And the question every beginner asks — can a normal garden spade or trowel do this? Regton's answer is honest: yes, it can be used, but it is not recommended, mainly for weight. Detecting spades are designed to be lightweight and to cut through roots and vegetation, and a smooth garden trowel lacks the serrated edge the plug procedure depends on. A garden tool is a reasonable first day out; it is a poor long-term substitute for a 12-inch serrated digger that costs little and disappears into a belt sheath.

FAQ

If only one tool fits the budget, which class? The hand digger. It is the only class welcome at every turf site, it handles the shallow targets beginners mostly dig, and its published class specs — roughly a 7-inch blade, 12 inches overall — make even a first purchase hard to get wrong. Add the scoop when beaches become regular, and the shovel when fields and deeper targets do.

Why serrated on one side and smooth on the other? The Lesche's published design splits the jobs: the serrated edge saws through roots, weeds and sod, while the smooth edge handles clean cuts and material such as weed mat or twine around landscaped sites. Two edges, two kinds of cutting, one 12-inch tool.

Is a bigger shovel always faster? Only where the ground allows it. In sand a shovel is slower than a scoop because the hole collapses; on turf it is not slower or faster but simply unacceptable, because the standard is an intact surface. Speed follows the site match, not the tool size.

How deep can a hand digger realistically work? Comfortably to average coin depth — the trade guidance puts its best range at targets less than about 6 inches deep. Below that, the narrow blade removes soil slowly and widening the hole starts to cost the turf, which is the signal to switch classes where the site permits a shovel.

Digging rules, tool restrictions and permissions vary by place and landowner — some sites prohibit digging tools or digging altogether. This guide is general information about tool classes and technique: always check local laws and get permission before detecting or digging on any land, and verify specifications against the manufacturer's current listing for the exact tool you are considering.
