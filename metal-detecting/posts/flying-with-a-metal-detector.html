Flying with a metal detector from Canada: batteries, bags, and the real cost

Travel & rules · Published Nov 2, 2026

$120. That is the number to start with: at Air Canada's current North American fees, a metal detector travelling as a second checked bag adds $60 in each direction, $120 on a round trip, before taxes. Whether the machine should fly at all is decided in a fixed order. The battery decides which bag the parts may ride in. The bag decides the fee. The destination's land rules decide whether any of it was worth paying. Work the three decisions in that order and the flight is routine; work them backwards and the expensive mistake is usually discovered at the check in counter.

The number first: what the detector adds to the fare

Air Canada raised its North American checked bag fees for tickets purchased on or after April 13, 2026. For Basic and Standard Economy fares on routes within Canada and between Canada and the US, the published fees, before taxes, are:

BagFee each way, CAD, before tax
First checked bag$45
Second checked bag$60
Each additional bag$100

Fees checked November 2, 2026 against the published schedule; with taxes the first and second bag fees can reach $54 and $72. Each checked bag may weigh up to 23 kg (50 lb) and measure up to 158 cm (62 in) in total dimensions. The trip cost calculation is then simple arithmetic. A traveller whose clothing already fills the first bag pays $60 each way for the detector case, $120 round trip before tax, and that figure belongs in the trip budget next to the fare, not discovered after it. A traveller who can pack the broken down detector inside the first bag pays nothing extra, which is why the packing split below matters more than the fee table. Fares, fare classes, and status change allowances, so confirm the fee on your own booking before relying on it; the figures here are general information from the published schedule, not a quote.

The battery decides the bag

Departing a Canadian airport, the screening rules are CATSA's, and for batteries they are built on one number: 100 watt hours. CATSA states that a lithium ion battery of 100 Wh or less may be carried inside a device in either carry-on or checked baggage. Spare lithium ion batteries of 100 Wh or less are different: they must be packed in carry-on baggage, never in a checked bag. Batteries between 101 and 160 Wh ride only with air carrier approval. Spare batteries also need their terminals protected against short circuit, in original packaging, taped, or in a protective case.

The pattern is the same on the US side for a transborder trip: the FAA states that spare lithium batteries are prohibited in checked baggage and must travel in the cabin, and that a device packed in a checked bag should be turned completely off, protected from accidental activation, and packed against damage. Detector batteries make this concrete. A machine with a built in lithium pack, such as the Nokta Legend with its internal 5050 mAh lithium polymer battery, can ride in either bag as a complete device. The moment a spare battery pack, a power bank, or loose cells for a pinpointer enter the luggage, those items are cabin only, on both sides of the border. Damaged or recalled batteries may not fly at all.

The packing split, part by part

Control box and headphones: carry-on if the bag allows, checked if it must be, with the machine switched fully off. This is the expensive, fragile part, and the cabin keeps it in reach.

Shaft sections and coil: checked baggage, padded inside clothing. An assembled detector is long, the Legend is 52 inches (132 cm) assembled, so the machine travels broken down; the size limit that matters is the checked bag's 158 cm total, measured as length plus width plus height.

Spare batteries and power bank: carry-on, always, terminals protected. This is a rule, not a preference, under both CATSA and FAA guidance.

Digging tools and pinpointer: checked baggage. A digging tool has no place in a cabin bag, and the pinpointer rides with it, switched off, unless its spare batteries are loose, in which case the batteries alone move to the cabin.

The destination check that comes before booking

None of the above matters if the destination is closed. Parks Canada restricts metal detector use in Cape Breton Highlands National Park to permit holders, with the restriction in effect at all times, and states for Waterton Lakes National Park that holes may not be dug and objects may not be removed without a permit issued by the Superintendent. A trip built around a national park lake or beach is a trip built around closed ground. Municipal and provincial rules vary by place, and private land needs the owner's permission. Run the land check for the specific beach, lake, or park on the itinerary before the fare is paid, not after the detector is unpacked. This is general information, not legal advice.

A pre-flight checklist

  1. Run the destination land check. Confirm the specific sites on the itinerary allow detecting, in writing where the site publishes rules.
  2. Sort the batteries. Built in pack stays in the machine. Every spare battery and the power bank go in the carry-on, terminals taped or cased.
  3. Break the machine down and measure the bag. Total dimensions at or under 158 cm, total weight at or under 23 kg, coil padded inside clothing.
  4. Price the bag honestly. Detector as a second checked bag is $60 each way before tax at the April 2026 Air Canada schedule; add it to the trip budget or repack until the detector fits the first bag.
  5. Switch everything off. Detector and pinpointer fully off, not sleeping, before the bags close.

What success looks like: screening with no bag opened for a battery, no fee at the counter that was not already in the budget, and a destination where the detector may legally be switched on. All three are decided at the kitchen table, which is the cheapest place to decide them.
