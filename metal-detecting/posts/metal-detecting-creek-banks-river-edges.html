Metal detecting creek banks and river edges: read the waterline, stay on the bank

Technique & settings · Published Nov 10, 2026

Two pieces of standard river advice deserve a second look before they cost you a hunt. The first is that creek and river detecting means getting in the water, which is why the usual gear lists start with a waterproof detector and a wading staff. The second is that the outside of a bend is where the current drops its load. The geology says the opposite, and the bank itself says the rest, if you read it before you swing. A creek bank hunted from dry ground is not a lesser version of wading. It is a different site, with its own productive strip, its own hazards, and its own order of operations.

The three bank mistakes that waste the hunt

Hunting the wrong side of the bend. Guide advice on bends is not consistent: one widely read river guide states in one section that deposition builds along the inner curve, then in a later section tells readers to sweep the outer curve where the current deposits heavier objects. The US National Park Service's fluvial geology page settles the mechanics. As water flows around a bend, the outer edge moves faster than the inner edge, which makes the outer bank an erosional surface, the cut bank, and the inner edge a depositional surface, the point bar. The cut bank is where the river is taking ground away. The point bar is where it is putting sediment down. Losses carried by the current settle with that sediment, not against the eroding face.

Standing on the bank the river is removing. The top edge of a cut bank is the worst standing room on the creek, and it is exactly where a hunter following the wrong bend advice ends up. The South Australian government's riverbank collapse guidance, written for the lower Murray but describing bank behaviour generally, lists the warning signs to check from a setback: cracking in the bank, deep or shallow, is a likely sign of instability, trees leaning towards the river are another, and steep banks with a deep drop off to deep water are at particular risk of collapse. The same guidance is blunt about the response: an area showing those signs should be avoided on foot.

Treating the water as the site. Entering the creek changes the hazard list from footing to current, concealed drop offs, and cold water, and on most creeks it is unnecessary. The Central Lake Ontario Conservation Authority's spring safety message warns of slippery, unstable streambanks and extremely cold water near Ontario waterways, and notes that banks can become unstable in spring from snowmelt and erosion.

That warning cuts both ways: respect the bank, and stay out of the water unless the hunt genuinely requires it. Low water does the exposing work for you, as the next section covers.

What the waterline is telling you

The waterline is a moving boundary, and the ground it has recently left is the freshest hunting on the creek. River guides agree on the timing even where they disagree on bends: low water periods are optimal because they expose previously submerged sections of bank and bed. On the bank, that exposed strip has three readable parts.

The point bar surface. The inside of the bend, where the Park Service notes that reduced current deposits coarse sediment, especially sand. This is a working surface, not scenery: it is where the river has sorted and laid down material, and it is hunted on foot, from the dry side, like any other gravel bar.

The crossing banks. Shallow crossings, fords, and swimming holes concentrate people, and guides consistently name them, along with bridge abutments, as the zones where human activity and sediment deposits concentrate finds. The bank approaches to a crossing, where people entered and left the water for generations, are bank hunting, not wading.

The cut bank toe, at a distance. Erosion at the outside of a bend releases older material, and some of it comes to rest at the base. That base is read from a stable setback, never from under an overhang and never by leaning over a cracked top edge. If the bank fails the stability check in the procedure below, the toe is skipped entirely. No target is worth standing in a collapse zone.

The bank-only search, step by step

  1. Sort permission before the trip. A creek bank belongs to someone. Private banks need the landowner's explicit permission before you enter, and agency managed public banks may require a permit, so the land manager is the first call, not the last.
  2. Run the stability check from a setback. Before stepping onto any bank top, look for cracks in the bank, trees leaning towards the water, overhanging or undercut edges, and a steep face dropping to deep water. Any one of these ends the check: hunt somewhere else.
  3. Read the bend and pick the point bar. Find the inside of the nearest bend and the exposed strip along the current waterline. That strip, plus the crossing approaches, is the hunt plan. Everything else is walking.
  4. Work the strip in lanes. Keep the coil low, sweep slowly, and overlap each pass by roughly half a coil width, the overlap figure river guides give for shallow water work, applied here to the exposed bank. Finish one lane before moving up or down the bank.
  5. Recover from the bank side. Dig from stable ground, keep spoil on the bank, and restore the spot so the bank face is left as found. Sharp trash comes out in a gloved hand and leaves with you.
  6. Reset after the water moves. A rise or fall in level redraws the strip. A bank hunted at one level is a new site at another, and the stability check runs again from the start, because moving water is also what undermines banks.

What success looks like: a hunt that never required entering the water, a point bar and a crossing worked in complete lanes, every dug spot restored, and a bank that was checked for cracks and lean before the first swing rather than after the first scare.

Permission, and the banks that are closed

Bank ownership is the quiet complication of creek hunting: the water may be public while the bank you stand on is private, and the permission question is about the ground under your feet. National park waterways are the clearest closed category. Parks Canada states for Waterton Lakes National Park that metal detecting is not encouraged, that it is illegal to disturb, deface, or remove a natural or cultural object from a national park, and that holes may not be dug and objects may not be removed unless the Superintendent has issued a Restricted Activity Permit or a Research and Collection Permit. Treat national park creeks and rivers as closed ground and hunt elsewhere. Everything here is general information about reading banks and published rules, not legal advice for a specific creek.

The strip is the site

The working habit that ties this together is a two visit rule for any creek you hunt more than once. Visit at the water level you find, hunt the exposed strip, and note the level in a finds log or on the map you used. Return after the level drops and the strip widens: ground that was under water on the first visit is new ground on the second, exposed by the creek itself, and it is reached without wading either time. The bend does the sorting, the waterline does the exposing, and the bank hunter's whole job is to arrive after both, with permission sorted and a stable place to stand.
