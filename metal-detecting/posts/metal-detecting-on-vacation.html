Metal detecting on vacation: packing a detector and checking local rules

Travel & rules · Published Oct 28, 2026

The common assumption is that the hard part of detecting on vacation is the flight — that airlines will balk at a long metal machine with a coil on the end. In practice the flight is the easy half. Detectors can travel in carry-on or checked luggage, and the packing question has a workable answer. The half that ends trips is the destination: a place where detecting is restricted, or where the machine itself is not welcome, turns a well-packed suitcase into dead weight. So this guide runs in trip order — rules first, while changing them costs nothing, then the packing split, then what to do on the ground.

Check the destination before you pack anything

Start with the starkest example, because it sets the tone for everything after it: in the United States it is illegal to use — or even to carry — a metal detector in a national park. No permit conversation, no quiet corner of the park where it is probably fine. Other destinations draw their lines in other places, which is exactly why a destination check belongs at the top of the planning list, before the detector comes out of the closet.

The check also has a harder edge for international trips. Some countries ban civilian import of metal detectors entirely, and customs will confiscate the device on arrival. That is not a fine you can budget for; it is the machine gone, on day one. A destination where the detector cannot legally enter is a destination where the whole plan changes — and it is far better to learn that at the kitchen table than at the arrivals hall.

The four-step rule check, in order

Rules for a specific beach, park, or town are rarely in one place, so work down the layers in this order — each step either clears the next or stops the plan while it is still cheap to change:

  1. National and heritage law first. Is the destination a national park, a protected site, or ground covered by heritage rules? In US national parks the answer ends the check: detectors may not be used or even carried. Deep or historically significant finds may also need to be reported to local authorities before digging, so know the reporting duty before the first hole, not after something old is sitting in your hand.
  2. The land manager second. Verify the rules with the authority that runs the specific ground — the park office, the beach authority, the municipality — and secure any permit required for state parks and protected areas. A blog post about the region is not the authority; the office that manages the site is.
  3. The beach rule, where there is a beach. Public beaches are often legal between the low and high tide marks, except at protected sites — a useful starting point, but still one to confirm at step two, because the exception list is local.
  4. Private land last, in writing. If any part of the plan involves private ground, get written authorization from the owner. Verbal agreements are not sufficient proof of permission when someone asks what you are doing there.

What success looks like: before you pack, you can point to a named authority for each place you plan to hunt, hold any permit the site requires, and carry the written permission for private land. If one of those three is missing, the trip is not ready — the detector stays home or the itinerary changes.

Packing: what flies in the cabin, what goes in the hold

The airline advice online contradicts itself because both halves of it are partly right. Airlines generally do not permit a large detector as carry-on — it must be checked, and carriers differ. At the same time, the electronics are usually better off with you: the control box is the expensive, fragile, battery-carrying part, and it belongs in the cabin where it cannot be crushed or lost. Reconciled, the packing rule is a split, not a choice:

PartWhere it travelsWhy
Control box / electronicsCarry-onThe costly, delicate part; sources consistently put the electronics box in the cabin
Shaft sections and coilChecked bagA full-size detector generally cannot fly as carry-on; broken down and padded, the frame rides safely in the hold
HeadphonesCarry-onSmall, light, and the piece you would miss most if the checked bag went astray
Digging tools and pinpointerChecked bagTools belong with the frame in the hold, wrapped so nothing sharp works loose in transit

Because carriers differ, confirm your own airline's baggage rules for the assembled size of what you are checking before you leave — the split above is the shape of the answer, not a substitute for your carrier's page.

On the ground: permission in writing, finds reported

Arriving legally is only the entry ticket. Carry the written permission when you detect on private land, and keep any permit with you rather than in the hotel room — the person asking is standing in front of you, not at the front desk. On public beaches, fill all holes immediately and keep 20–30 feet from other beachgoers; on a holiday beach, how a detectorist behaves is what the next visitor gets judged by. And if something deep or historically significant comes up, remember step one of the rule check: in some places it must be reported to local authorities before you dig further, not posted online after.

When the answer is no

Sometimes the four-step check comes back with a closed door: a protected coast, a park system that says no, a country that will keep the machine at the border. That is a result, not a failure — you learned it before the suitcase was packed. Leave the detector home, and spend the trip the way the rule check quietly teaches you to anyway: walking the ground, noting where people gather, where the old paths run, and which authority manages what. That map is worth more on the next trip — to a place where the answer is yes — than any single holiday find.
