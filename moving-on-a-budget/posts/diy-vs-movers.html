DIY vs Hiring Movers: The Real Cost-Comparison Framework

The cheapest-looking option usually isn't — because the costs people forget are the ones that decide the outcome.

Moving on a Budget · Guide 1 of 5

Most people compare moving options wrong. They put the rental-truck quote next to the mover's quote, pick the smaller number, and call it a decision. Then the real costs show up anyway — fuel, insurance, feeding six helpers, a second truck trip because the first one was too small, a scratch on a doorframe that eats the deposit.

The fix is simple: compare total cost, not quote cost. Here's how to build the comparison honestly.

Step 1: List every line item, not just the big ones

For each option, write down a number for every one of these. If you don't know a number yet, mark it "unknown" instead of pretending it's zero — that's where the surprises hide.

The DIY move

  • Truck or van rental — base rate plus mileage. Many rentals charge per kilometer/mile after a small allowance, and a second day costs nearly as much as the first.
  • Fuel — rental trucks are thirsty. Budget for the full round trip, not just one way.
  • Insurance — the rental company's damage waiver, or check whether your auto policy or credit card covers rental trucks (many exclude box trucks).
  • Dolly and blankets — renting these at the counter is normal; buying a cheap dolly and selling it after is sometimes cheaper.
  • Packing supplies — see the free-supplies guide; even "free" packing takes a tape-and-paper budget.
  • Help — pizza and drinks for friends is a real line item, and so is the risk that two of them cancel. Paid day-labor for heavy furniture has a cost too.
  • Time — a DIY move eats a full day (often two). If you earn hourly or freelance, a day off work is part of the cost.
  • Damage risk — movers carry liability coverage; your friends don't. One dropped TV or gouged doorframe has a price. Check your renter's or homeowner's policy for what's covered during a move.

The hired movers

  • The quote — is it binding or an estimate? Estimates rise on moving day more often than they fall.
  • Stairs, long carries, and bulky items — most companies charge extra for flights of stairs, long walks to the truck, pianos, and oversized furniture. Ask; don't assume.
  • Packing services — many movers offer packing at a per-box rate that can double the bill. Pack yourself whenever possible.
  • Valuation coverage — basic "released value" coverage pays pennies per pound; full-value protection costs extra. Know which you're getting.
  • Tips — customary for good work; budget for it rather than being surprised.
  • Your own packing supplies — movers move boxes; someone still buys them.

Step 3: Apply the decision rules

After running the numbers, these rules of thumb handle most situations:

  • Small, simple, local → DIY usually wins. A studio or one-bedroom with no oversized furniture and a short drive is the classic DIY sweet spot, especially with reliable help.
  • Heavy items change everything. A piano, a solid-wood armoire, or a washer/dryer set can make professional movers cheaper than the damage risk and extra labor of DIY.
  • Distance favors movers. Long drives multiply fuel, fatigue, and the cost of anything going wrong. One-way truck rentals also price differently than local ones — check before assuming.
  • Consider the hybrid. Rent the truck yourself, pack yourself, and hire two movers for a few hours to load and unload the heavy stuff. For many moves this is the cheapest reliable option.
  • Off-peak saves real money. If you have any flexibility, mid-month and mid-week moves are typically cheaper than end-of-month weekends — both for rentals and movers.

Step 4: Decide, then commit

The most expensive move is the indecisive one — booking movers last-minute at peak rates, or starting DIY and discovering at noon that you needed professionals. Pick your option at least two to three weeks out, book it, and move on to getting quotes that don't surprise you.
