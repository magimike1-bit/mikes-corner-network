Moving Expenses You Can Deduct in Canada: The 40 Kilometre Rule and Form T1-M

Forty kilometres is the number that decides whether your moving receipts are a deduction or just receipts. Measure it the CRA way, before you spend, not at tax time.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

40 kilometres, measured by the shortest public route, is the gate. The Canada Revenue Agency rule is that your new home must be at least 40 kilometres closer to your new work location or school than your old home was. Note what that measures: not how far you moved, but how much closer you ended up. A 200 kilometre move that lands you only 30 kilometres closer to the new job fails the test, and a short move across a city can pass it. Everything else in this article only matters after that gate swings open, which is why the cheapest step in the whole claim is a map measurement taken before a single box is packed. This guide is general information, not tax or financial advice.

Who the gate opens for

The CRA frames eligibility around why you moved. You moved to a new home to work or to run a business out of a new location, or you moved to be a student in full time attendance in a post secondary program at a university, college, or other educational institution. Ordinary moves, a bigger apartment, a shorter commute to the same job, a change of scenery, do not qualify, however expensive they were. If your move fits one of those purposes and passes the 40 kilometre test, the claim is calculated on Form T1-M, Moving Expenses Deduction, with a separate form for each eligible move, and the result goes on line 21900 of your return.

Read the table as a shopping list for your records folder. Every category you will claim needs its paperwork kept, because receipts are not sent in with the return but must be available if the CRA asks to see them. A deduction built from a shoebox you assembled honestly during the move beats a larger one reconstructed from bank statements in April.

The cap nobody budgets for: income at the new location

Here is the condition the headline guides bury. You can only deduct moving expenses against income earned at the new location, and the deduction in a given year cannot exceed that income. Unused amounts are not lost: they carry forward to a later year. Worked as a labelled example, with round numbers chosen for clarity and not taken from any source: suppose eligible expenses total $6,000 and you earn $4,000 at the new job before year end. The example claim for that year is $4,000, and the remaining $2,000 carries forward. The budget consequence is real. A household that moves in November should not plan December spending around a full $6,000 deduction landing in the first year, because the calendar, not the receipts, sets the pace. Students face their own version of this cap, since their claim runs against the taxable scholarship and grant income the rules specify, which is one more reason this article stays general information and your own situation deserves a qualified tax professional.

Simplified or detailed: decide before you move

For vehicle and meal expenses on the trip, the CRA lets you choose the detailed method or the simplified method. The simplified method prices meals at a flat rate: the rate published for the 2025 tax year was $23 per meal, up to $69 per day per person, with vehicle amounts priced by a per kilometre rate that varies by province. The detailed method prices what you actually spent, which means it prices what you can prove. That is the whole decision, and it has to be made before the move, because the detailed method's evidence, fuel receipts, meal receipts, a mileage log, only exists if someone collects it from the first kilometre.

MethodWhat it needs from youWhen it tends to fit
SimplifiedKilometres driven and days travelled; no meal receiptsShorter moves, modest actual costs, or a household that will not keep receipts reliably
DetailedEvery receipt, plus records for vehicle costsLong drives with high fuel and lodging costs that exceed the flat rates

The Penny rule for this choice: pick the method you will actually complete. A detailed claim abandoned in a glove box is worth less than a simplified claim filed cleanly.

Traps: reimbursement, house hunting, and the sale loss

The reimbursement trap. If your employer reimbursed the move and that reimbursement was not added to your income, there is nothing to deduct, because you did not bear the expense. If the reimbursement was included in your income, the matching costs can remain deductible. Check the pay paperwork before assuming either way.

The not eligible list. Work done to make the old home more saleable, any loss on the sale of the old home, and house hunting trips before the move are not eligible expenses. Households routinely spend real money on all three while expecting a deduction that does not exist. Price those decisions as what they are, consumption or investment in the sale, never as subsidized costs.

The timing trap. Temporary living stops at 15 days and the vacant home category stops at $5,000. A plan that assumes a month of deductible lodging is a plan with a hole in it.

The pre move records checklist

  1. Measure the distance test first: old home to new work or school, new home to the same place, shortest public route, and keep a screenshot of both routes.
  2. Open one folder, physical or digital, labelled with the move, before booking anything.
  3. Choose simplified or detailed for vehicle and meals, and start the matching records that day.
  4. File every receipt by the CRA categories in the table above as you spend, not afterwards.
  5. Record your start date and first year income at the new location as the year goes, so the cap and the carryforward are planned, not discovered.
  6. Keep the folder after filing. The CRA can ask for the documents, and a carried forward amount needs its evidence in the later year too.

What success looks like: at tax time, Form T1-M is arithmetic, not archaeology, every number on it traces to a document in one folder, and the amount you expected to claim, cap, carryforward, and all, matches the amount the return can actually use.
