The building sets the price

Moving in Toronto: Elevator Bookings, Parking, and Condo Rules That Affect Your Cost

It is the last Saturday of the month, the service elevator is booked in blocks, and more than one household is trying to move through the same loading dock. In a Toronto condo, the building's rules — not the mover's quote — decide whether that day stays on budget.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

Toronto — A condo move has two price lists. One is the mover's. The other belongs to the building: the elevator window you are allowed, the deposit you may have to post, the insurance paperwork your mover must carry, and the spot where the truck is allowed to sit. The second list is the one that surprises people, because most of it never appears on a quote. The figures below come from Toronto mover and condo-move sources checked October 25, 2026, and every building sets its own terms — treat the numbers as the shape of the cost, and get your building's actual terms in writing.

The elevator: why the booking advice conflicts

Search for how far ahead to book a condo elevator and the answers collide. One Toronto mover advises reserving the service elevator two to three weeks before the move date. Toronto condo guidance elsewhere puts the minimum at 48 to 72 hours ahead — while warning that the same booking stretches to weeks at month-end, and in September and May.

Both can be true, because they are describing different buildings on different dates. The 48-to-72-hour figure is a minimum where a building allows it. The two-to-three-week figure is what demand does to that minimum when every lease in the building turns over on the same weekend. The deciding factors are your building's own policy and the calendar, in that order.

So the practical rule is simple. Ask your building first, and ask early enough that its answer can still change your date. If the elevator blocks are already gone for your Saturday, a mid-month or weekday slot is not a consolation prize — it is the move that actually happens, on the day you planned, without a crew standing idle in the lobby.

Service box — what to confirm with your building, in writing: the elevator booking window and the earliest date you may reserve it; any moving deposit or elevator fee, and what makes it refundable; the exact insurance certificate wording the building requires; where the truck may park or load, and for how long; and the hours during which moves are permitted at all. Every entry on this list is building-specific — no guide can fill it in for you.

Deposits and building fees: the unpriced line

Here is an honest gap. Toronto moving sources price the mover's side in detail and say almost nothing, with figures, about what buildings themselves charge. Moving deposits and elevator fees are mentioned in passing and never priced in the sources behind this guide. Anyone quoting you a "typical" Toronto condo deposit from a guide like this one would be guessing.

What can be priced is the mover waiting while the building question gets sorted out. One Toronto mover lists waiting fees of $30 to $60 per 30 minutes when access is delayed. Do the arithmetic on a lobby dispute: one hour of a crew standing by costs $60 to $120, on top of the job itself. That same mover's minimum gives the scale of the job — a two-hour minimum with two movers at $129 to $139 per hour, plus a $150 truck fee, totals $461.04 to $483.64 including 13% HST. An access argument that burns an hour is not a nuisance. It is a fifth to a quarter of the minimum bill, spent on nothing.

The insurance certificate: small paper, total veto

The sharpest rule in the building's list is the certificate of insurance. A Toronto mover notes that the certificate takes one to two days to issue — and that a building can refuse entry without one. Read those two facts together and the failure case writes itself.

If the certificate is wrong, missing, or named incorrectly on move day, there is no negotiation that fixes it before the elevator window closes. The crew is booked, the minimum applies, the waiting clock can run while phone calls are made, and the move itself may have to be rebooked for another day — with a second elevator reservation to chase. The full cost of that scenario was not tallied by any source in the research, which is exactly why it belongs on a checklist rather than in a budget line: it is a cost you avoid entirely, or pay in full.

The avoidance is cheap and early. When you book the mover, ask for the certificate by name, confirm the building's required wording with management, and have the certificate in hand days before the move — not in an inbox you plan to check on the morning.

Street space: parking the truck legally

Not every Toronto building has a loading dock the truck can use, and a truck that cannot sit legally near the door turns into crew time spent carrying further and waiting longer. Whether street space needs a permit, and what that permit costs, is a question for the City of Toronto and your building together — the research behind this guide found the permit figure only in a secondary post, not in a city fee schedule, so no figure is printed here rather than printing an unverified one.

What is safe to plan on: the truck's position is a move-day decision to settle in advance, not on arrival. Ask the building where moving trucks load, ask whether that space is reservable with your elevator block, and walk the route from that spot to the elevator before move day. Surprises on that route are billed by the half hour.

Move-day compliance checklist

Work the list in this order — each step protects the money in the next one:

  1. Book the elevator against the building's real lead time. Two to three weeks ahead is the safe planning figure; 48 to 72 hours is a minimum some buildings allow, not a promise. At month-end, in September, or in May, assume the longer window.
  2. Get the deposit and fee terms in writing. Amount, payment method, and refund conditions — from your building, because no published "typical" figure exists to budget from.
  3. Order the insurance certificate when you book the mover. It takes one to two days to issue. Confirm the building's exact required wording, and keep a copy with your move-day papers.
  4. Settle the truck's spot. Loading area, permitted hours, and any permit question answered by the building and the city before the truck rolls.
  5. Price the delay before it happens. At $30 to $60 per 30 minutes of waiting, every unanswered building question is a meter running. A ten-minute phone call this week is the cheapest line in the whole move.

Success looks like this: the elevator is yours for a named block, the deposit terms are on paper, the certificate is printed, and the truck has a legal place to sit. Nothing on that list is exciting. That is the point — on a Toronto condo move, the boring paperwork is the budget.
