Moving Insurance in Canada: What a Quote's “Coverage” Actually Pays

A $1,000 table can be “covered” for $60. The number that decides which outcome you get is on the bill of lading, not in the sales pitch — and there are two different per-pound numbers in Canadian moving, not one.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

Ask a room of people who have moved what their belongings were insured for and most will say the move “included coverage.” That word is doing heavy lifting. In Canadian moving, three different things get called coverage, they pay out in three different ways, and only one of them is actually insurance. Getting them confused is how a damaged $1,000 table turns into a $60 settlement that the mover can honestly describe as “what the coverage pays.” This guide separates the three, shows the arithmetic on real items, and ends with an audit you can run on any quote before you sign. It is general information about how the system works — not legal advice, and not a substitute for reading your own bill of lading and policy wording.

The distinction is not pedantry. As BrokerLink's summary of Canadian Association of Movers guidance puts it, movers do not insure your belongings — they offer valuation, which sets the maximum the mover will pay, and it is governed by transportation and carriage regulations rather than insurance law. Claims against each layer work differently, are argued differently, and fail for different reasons.

The two per-pound numbers, and why your quote shows the smaller one

Every explanation of Canadian moving coverage starts with $0.60 per pound per article. The Canadian Association of Movers' consumer guidance is the source: under Released Valuation, the mover's liability is limited to $0.60 per pound per article, and if you waive Replacement Value Protection, Released Valuation is what applies. Run the arithmetic and the problem is obvious. A 45 kg sofa — 100 lbs — settles at about $60. A $500 dresser at 32 kg settles at roughly $42. The formula never asks what the item is worth.

But there is a second number, and it lives in the actual conditions of carriage printed with bills of lading. Article 9 computes any loss on the value of the goods at the place and time of shipment. Article 10 then caps the carrier's liability at $2.00 per pound ($4.41 per kilogram) computed on the total weight of the shipment — unless a higher value is declared on the face of the bill of lading. Note the two structural differences: the cap is more than three times higher, and it is measured across the whole shipment rather than item by item. On a 5,000 lb household, that cap implies roughly $10,000 of carrier liability across the load — a very different world from $60 a sofa.

Why do both numbers exist? A detailed analysis of British Columbia's conditions of carriage — the province whose regulation is spelled out most fully in public — reads Article 10 this way: the default is the greater of your declared value or $4.41 per kilogram on total weight, and the $1.32 per kilogram ($0.60 per pound) per-article figure applies only where the shipper has released the shipment in writing to that lower figure. The industry framing, including the Association's own consumer pages and federal consumer guidance, presents the lower figure as the starting point. Those two readings cannot both be the whole story, and the practical takeaway for a budget mover is the same either way: if a mover offers you sixty cents a pound, ask to see the release you supposedly signed. Conditions of carriage and their interpretation vary by province — which is exactly why the paperwork, not the brochure, is the source that counts.

What quotes exclude, line by line

  • Undisclosed items of extraordinary value. Article 13: no carrier is bound to carry documents, cash, or articles of extraordinary value without a special agreement. The Association's guidance says the same from the other side — antiques and other high-value items must be declared, and jewellery, documents, and valuables should travel with you. Undisclosed is unprotected.
  • Vehicles get their own, weaker rule. Motor vehicles, trailers, campers, motorcycles, and boats receive limited protection at current market value, and must be listed separately on the bill of lading.
  • Contents of boxes you packed and sealed. Under the BC conditions-of-carriage analysis, missing contents from a carton you packed are excluded unless the box was opened for the carrier's inspection, itemised on the bill of lading, and receipted. Owner-packing does not erase recourse where carrier negligence caused the damage — but it moves the argument onto negligence, with the burden of proving its absence on the carrier.
  • The exclusions with no negligence argument at all include acts of God, defects or inherent vice in the goods, and damage at a pickup or delivery point where nobody was there to receive the goods.
  • Part of a set pays for the piece, not the set. Lose one of six dining chairs and the liability is for that chair.

What your home or tenant policy actually adds

Your own policy is real insurance, and it usually does cover belongings in transit within Canada — the Insurance Bureau of Canada treats transportation as a named peril for property temporarily away from home. The windows are specific: Intact's tenants wording gives all-risk cover for 90 consecutive days from the date property leaves the home; TD allows up to 45 consecutive days, conditional on insuring both the old and new homes with TD. Storage in the middle of a move is a separate question — ask about it specifically rather than assuming the transit clause stretches over a storage month.

Then come the exclusions that gut most real-world claims. Both wordings above exclude scratching, abrasion, and chipping, and accidental breakage of fragile or brittle articles, unless an insured peril caused the damage. Scratched furniture and broken dishes are the overwhelming majority of moving damage — so “my home insurance covers the move” is technically true and practically narrow. Add the arithmetic: deductibles of at least $500, and more likely $1,000, apply to a home-policy claim, and the Association notes a claim can cost a claims-free discount worth 10 to 20 percent of your premium. On a $900 loss, filing can come out negative.

One more option you will see recommended everywhere: buying third-party moving insurance online. For a move inside Canada, treat that advice with suspicion — the best-known online retail provider is licensed across the United States and does not cover Canadian domestic moves, and no comparable Canadian retail product turned up in the research for this article. The pre-signing audit: a tested procedure

  1. Find the valuation line on the quote. It should name the option (released or replacement value) in writing. If the quote says only “fully insured” or “coverage included,” that is a marketing phrase, not a term — ask for the option name and the rate it pays.
  2. Do the per-pound math on your three most valuable items. Weigh or estimate them. At $0.60/lb per article, write down what each would actually settle for. This five-minute calculation is the moment most people decide the upgrade is worth pricing.
  3. If you buy Replacement Value Protection, check the declared-value floor. The Association documents a minimum: $10.00 per pound multiplied by the shipment's actual weight. A 5,000 lb household must declare at least $50,000. The floor is a minimum you must declare, not a cap on what is paid — your declared amount is the ceiling.
  4. List extraordinary-value items separately. Jewellery, collections, documents, and cash travel with you. Antiques and high-value pieces get declared and described on the bill of lading, or they ride under Article 13's no-special-agreement rule.
  5. In Ontario, use the contract checklist the province publishes. A moving contract over $50 must be in writing, with the mover's name, address and contact information, an itemized price list, the total, start and end dates, and payment terms. The province also says the contract can include the mover's insurance provider and policy number and who is responsible for damage or loss — ask for both, in writing. And an estimate inside the contract has teeth: the mover cannot charge more than 10 percent above it unless you agree to added services.
  6. Refuse to sign a release you have not read. If the bill of lading contains a written release to the $0.60-per-pound rate, that signature is the moment the lower number becomes real. Sign the coverage selection that matches what you decided in step 3 — the Association itself advises that the bill of lading clearly note the coverage selected, signed properly by all parties, with a Carrier Liability Certificate provided.
  7. Photograph and inventory before loading. Whatever layer you claim against, condition and contents are the argument. A dated photo set and a box inventory cost an evening and are the only evidence that survives the move.

The decision framework: how much protection to buy

  • Mostly replaceable, low-value-per-pound load (flat-pack furniture, clothes, books): released valuation plus your home policy's transit window is a defensible budget choice — the items it underpays are the ones you would not claim for anyway.
  • Light and expensive load (electronics, instruments, quality furniture): the per-pound formula fails you hardest exactly here. Replacement Value Protection priced on a realistic declared value is the layer that matches the risk.
  • Anything irreplaceable: no valuation option pays sentimental value. It travels in your car, or it is declared, photographed, and packed by the mover so the negligence argument stays clean.
  • A storage gap in the middle: confirm in writing which layer covers the storage period — carrier, container company, or your own policy with a storage extension — before the load leaves the house.

What success looks like

You have done this right when, before anything is loaded, you can point at the signed bill of lading and read off: the valuation option you selected, the declared value if you upgraded, the per-pound rate that applies if you did not, and the separate listing of every high-value item. You know which of the three layers you would claim against for a scratched table (probably the mover), a smashed television (check the exclusion list first), and a missing box (check whether it was inspected and itemised). If damage happens, the claim is a paperwork exercise, not an argument about what “coverage” meant.

FAQ

Is moving insurance in Canada actually insurance?

Mostly not. The mover's liability and Replacement Value Protection are contractual limits under the conditions of carriage, priced and paid by the mover. The only true insurance in the picture is your own home or tenant policy — which is why the three layers claim differently.

What does $10 per pound mean on a moving quote?

It is the minimum declared value for Replacement Value Protection — $10 per pound multiplied by the shipment's actual weight — not a payout rate and not a cap. Declare 5,000 lbs of household and the floor is $50,000 of declared value; what gets paid is bounded by what you declare above it.

My mover says I am covered at 60 cents a pound. Is that the law?

It is one of two numbers in the system. The conditions of carriage cap liability at $2.00 per pound on total shipment weight unless a higher value is declared, and the detailed BC analysis reads the 60-cent per-article rate as applying only on a written release. Provinces differ — ask which condition of carriage your bill of lading carries, and ask to see any release before you sign it.
