Saving on Your First Month After a Move: Deposits, Hookup Fees, and Restocking

Yes — the first month usually costs more than the moving day. One published teardown puts it at $4,070 into a $1,200-rent home. Here is where that money sits, and the order to pay it in.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

The first month after a move has its own budget, separate from the move itself — and for renters it can be the larger one. Deposits land before the boxes do. Hookup fees arrive with the first utility accounts. The kitchen starts from zero. None of these costs is a surprise individually; the ambush is that they share a calendar. Staging them across four weeks, instead of absorbing them in one, is where the saving lives. The figures below come from the sources checked October 25, 2026, and one warning runs through the whole piece: deposit rules are provincial, and the detailed rules in the research are Alberta's.

The teardown: one first month, line by line

One published worked example totals $4,070 for a local move into a home renting at $1,200 a month. Inside that total, the example itemizes restocking groceries at $150–$300 and about $280 in utility connections and deposits, alongside first-week eating out. The rent itself accounts for $1,200 of the $4,070.

A second source, pricing Alberta rentals, puts the first month typically at $300–$700 above the rent itself, with total upfront costs of $2,500–$4,500 common. Two different sources, two different baskets, one consistent message: budget the first month as its own event, at several hundred dollars above rent, or it budgets itself out of your account.

Deposits: the rules depend on your province

Here is the trap in most first-month advice: it states one province's deposit rules as if they were Canada's. They are not, and the research behind this guide proves the point by accident — the detailed deposit rules it retrieved are Alberta's. In Alberta, the security deposit is capped at one month's rent and held in trust. That is a sourced Alberta rule. It is not stated here as Ontario law, or any other province's law, because no official source for those provinces was retrieved in this research.

So the deposit step is a check, not a number:

  1. Find your own province's rule before paying anything: your provincial landlord-and-tenant authority publishes what a landlord may collect, how much, and when it is due. Read that page, not a summary written for somewhere else.
  2. Compare the lease request against the rule line by line — amount, timing, and what the payment is called.
  3. Keep the paper: receipts and the lease copy go in the moving file. A deposit dispute months later is won with documents, not recollection.

What can be said across provinces, from the Alberta figures, is scale: deposits and first payments together are why upfront totals of $2,500–$4,500 are described as common. If your quote for month one is dramatically below that shape, check which lines are missing before celebrating.

Hookups and utility deposits

Utility hookup fees and deposits are a named part of the first-month stack in the Alberta source, and the worked example prices connections and deposits together at about $280. That figure is one example's line, not a schedule — your providers and your credit arrangements with them set your number. The saving move is timing, not haggling:

  • Book connections before moving day, for the dates you actually take possession. A connection arranged in a rush after arrival costs the same fee and buys a dark, cold first evening that ends in takeout.
  • Close or transfer the old accounts on the matching dates. Overlap you choose is a convenience; overlap by neglect is a second bill for a home you no longer live in.
  • Ask each provider directly whether a deposit applies to your account and what removes it. That answer belongs in the worksheet below as a real figure, not the example's $280.

Restocking from zero, staged over four weeks

The worked example allows $150–$300 for grocery restocking. Spent in one panicked shop on day two, that money buys duplicates of things already in boxes and premium prices on everything. Staged, the same budget covers more:

  • Week 1 — the working kitchen: the perishables and daily basics that end the eating-out streak. First-week eating out sits inside the $4,070 example for a reason; every day the kitchen works is a day that line stops growing.
  • Week 2 — the pantry skeleton: the shelf-stable foundations your household actually cooks from, bought once the boxes are open and you know what survived the move and what space holds it.
  • Week 3 — cleaning and household consumables, after the unpacking shows what is genuinely missing.
  • Week 4 — the honest gaps list: everything deferred from weeks 1–3 that the household still wants. Experience with a staged list is that several week-one urgencies quietly leave it.

Staging is not deprivation. It is sequencing — the restock budget spent in the order the household actually consumes it, with a built-in cooling-off week before the non-essentials are bought.

The overlap problem: two rents, one month

The largest first-month lever is not in any table: overlap. Days where the old rent and the new rent both run are paid for twice-used housing, and the sources' upfront totals assume some version of that squeeze. Reduce it where your agreements allow — possession and move-out dates negotiated as a pair, the move scheduled inside the overlap you have rather than across two — and price whatever overlap remains into the worksheet as a named line. An overlap you chose, counted, and shortened is a cost. An overlap discovered on the bank statement is a failure of planning.

When overlap cannot shrink, protect the rest of the month harder: stage the restock, hold every non-essential purchase until week 4, and let the deposit and hookup lines be the only other big items. One heavy line at a time is survivable. Four in the same week is how the first month becomes a debt.

Your first-month budget worksheet

Copy these lines and fill each with your own figure — a quote, a lease line, or a provider's answer. No line takes a number from this guide's examples; those exist to show the shape, not to fill your cells.

  1. First rent payment, from the lease: $____
  2. Deposit and any other upfront amounts your province's rules allow, verified against the provincial source: $____
  3. Utility connections and deposits, per provider: $____
  4. Overlap days priced at both rents: $____
  5. Restocking, staged across four weeks, with a ceiling you set before week 1: $____
  6. First-week food away from home, capped in advance: $____

The worksheet is filled in correctly when every line carries a source you can point to — the lease, the provincial page, a provider's quote — and the total is a number you saw before the month began. Compare it against the published shapes as a final check: several hundred dollars above rent for the month itself, upfront totals somewhere in the $2,500–$4,500 range the Alberta source calls common. A total far outside those shapes is not wrong, but it earns one more read of the lines.
