Selling or Donating Furniture Before a Move: How a Smaller Load Cuts Your Truck and Labour Cost

Selling a couch for $200 does not save $200. It saves $200 plus whatever its absence takes off the truck and the shipment — minus whatever replacing it costs, and minus what getting rid of the unsold pieces costs. This guide builds that whole ledger, with the published figures checked October 31, 2026.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

The common claim is that selling furniture before a move "pays for the move." Sometimes it helps a lot. Sometimes the proceeds look good and the household still loses money, because the sold pieces have to be bought again at the other end. The honest test is never the sale price alone. It is a four-line ledger, run per piece: proceeds in, moving cost avoided, replacement cost out, disposal cost out. The tables below come first, because the net figure is the point.

The truck ladder, priced — and kept honest

U-Haul's published in-town base rates, checked October 31, 2026 on the company's own comparison page, form a neat ladder: a 10-foot truck at $19.95 a day, a 15-foot at $29.95, a 20-foot at $39.95, and a 26-foot at $49.95 — each plus mileage and fees, with the page noting its prices were sourced in April 2025 and are subject to change. Every step down the ladder is worth exactly $10 a day on the base rate.

Ten dollars is real, and it is not the fortune some guides imply. Read the ladder correctly:

  • The saving is per day, on the base rate only. Mileage and fees sit outside the ladder, and a one-day local move that drops a size saves $10 before those add-ons — worth having, not worth selling a good couch to get.
  • The step has to be real. Selling one chair rarely changes the truck size. The saving appears only when the pieces sold are the pieces that were forcing the bigger truck — usually the bulky furniture, not the boxes.
  • The local rate does not rise by the hour. U-Haul states the local daily rate is the same for a 2-hour or a 24-hour rental; you pay the rate, mileage, and fees. So on a DIY move, a smaller load saves on the ladder, not on a rental clock. The labour clock belongs to hired movers — and labour accounts for 50 to 70% of a full-service quote, which is why the volume-priced column of the ledger above is so much larger than the truck line.

Confirm the ladder against your own local quote before counting it: in-town pricing varies by location and date, and a one-way rental is priced by route, not by this daily ladder.

Run the ledger per piece: the decision framework

For each large piece, answer four questions in order. A piece that fails a question stops there.

  1. Does it fit, and will it be used, at the new place? If not, replacement cost is zero and almost any proceeds are a win. If yes, keep going — the next answers decide.
  2. What would it cost to move? On a volume-priced move, use the sourced illustration as a sanity check: a large piece at around $200 to ship. On a DIY move, ask whether this piece is one of the items forcing the next truck size up — if it is, its share of the step is $10 a day; if it is not, its truck cost is close to zero and honesty requires saying so.
  3. What will it actually sell for? Price from sold listings, not asking prices. As a rough guide from the selling research, flat-pack furniture in good condition often sells for a third to half of its new price, and recent electronics for around half, depending on age.
  4. What does replacing it cost? If proceeds plus avoided moving cost are less than the replacement you will genuinely buy, the ledger says keep it and move it. Sentimental and hard-to-replace pieces belong on the truck on the same logic — the framework prices them by replacement, and some replacements do not exist.

Selling on a deadline: the sequence

The ledger only works if the sales close before the truck is booked. The selling timeline in the research runs like this:

  1. Six to eight weeks out: list the big, cheap-to-replace pieces first — furniture and appliances — on local marketplaces, where buyers collect and nothing gets shipped.
  2. Each week: drop prices on a schedule rather than in a panic — the research suggests 10 to 15% after a week with no serious interest, and again a week later.
  3. The last two weeks: pre-sell the pieces still in daily use — bed, couch, television — with the pickup date set for your last days, so the home stays livable and the sale still closes.
  4. Before booking the truck: count what is actually left, then book the size the remaining load needs. Booking first and selling second locks in the bigger truck and throws the ladder saving away.
  5. The final week: whatever has not sold moves to the donation plan below — not into the truck by default.

Success looks like this: every large piece has a ledger, the truck was booked after the sales closed, and nothing unsold is riding along simply because no exit was arranged for it.

Donation, priced honestly

Donation is often described as the free fallback. In Toronto, it is free in one specific way and paid in another, and the difference is worth more than the truck step-down. Furniture Bank's own donor page, checked October 31, 2026, lists its Etobicoke warehouse drop-off as free — Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 1 p.m., closed weekends, at 25 Connell Court, Unit 1 — while its home pickup service starts at $150, with fees varying by volume and location.

The rest of the terms matter on a deadline. Items must be in clean, usable condition, staff quality-check drop-offs and may decline pieces that cannot be rehomed, and donations valued at $25 or more are issued a charitable tax receipt the next business day. Items left outside the warehouse after hours count as illegal dumping and earn no receipt — a final-week drop-off has to land inside those weekday hours, or it does not happen at all. Other donation routes exist in most cities; price each one the same way — pickup fee, hours, condition rules — before assuming any of them is the free option.

Run the comparison plainly: a $150 pickup for pieces that failed to sell is a cost of the selling plan, and it belongs in the ledger. A free drop-off costs a trip instead. Either can be right; neither is $0 by default.

When keeping it wins

The ledger has a losing side, and it is not rare. Keep and move a piece when it is high quality and expensive to replace, when the new place needs it and a replacement would cost more than the proceeds plus the avoided moving cost combined, or when the piece is one of the small, dense things whose absence changes neither the truck size nor the shipment in any way the bill notices. Smaller loads also start from different places: the Canadian cost guide puts a lightly furnished studio at around 2,000 pounds and a fully furnished three-bedroom home at over 7,500 pounds — the selling lever is worth far more to the second household than the first.

Sell the pieces the ledger clears. Donate the pieces it does not, on a route you have priced. Move the rest without apology — that is what the truck is for.
