The gap decision

Storage Unit, Sell It, or Store It With Family? The Gap-Between-Homes Decision

When the old home ends before the new one begins, every belonging needs a temporary answer — and the cheapest answer depends on the length of the gap and the honest total cost, not the monthly rent alone.

Closing dates and lease ends rarely line up. The old place ends on the 30th, the new one starts on the 12th, and in between there is a couch, a dining set, three beds, and forty boxes with nowhere to go. Storage companies describe exactly this situation as one of the main reasons people rent: a self-storage unit provides a secure place for belongings while the household stays in temporary accommodation, and it works as a renovation buffer and a flexible move-in aid as well. That does not make a unit the automatic answer. There are three real options — rent a unit, store things with family or friends, or sell and rebuy later — and each one wins in a different gap.

The three options, honestly described

Rent a short-term storage unit. Self-storage is normally sold month-to-month with no long-term contract, which is what makes it usable for a gap of unknown length: the rental rolls until the new home is ready. One major operator's size guide maps the common sizes to households — a 5×5 for boxes, seasonal items, and small belongings; a 5×10 for a dorm room, studio, or small one-bedroom; a 10×10 for the furniture and belongings of a one- or two-bedroom home; and a 10×15 for a two-bedroom home including appliances. Facilities also offer indoor and climate-controlled units alongside outdoor drive-up units, which matters for wood furniture, electronics, and records in a Canadian winter or a humid summer.

Store it with family or friends. The rent is zero, and for a short gap that is hard to beat. The costs are real but non-monetary: the host's garage or spare room is occupied for as long as the gap actually lasts — not as long as it was supposed to last — access is by the host's schedule, and anything damaged by a damp basement or a knocked-over stack has no contract and no insurance process behind it. This option also quietly requires two full handling efforts by the same people who just helped with the move.

Sell it, then rebuy what is still needed. Selling converts the problem into cash and removes the storage question entirely. Downsizing guidance from a Canadian storage operator frames the sort the same way this decision should be framed: categorize everything into keep, donate, sell, and store, and let the unit — or a host's garage — hold only the items that survive that sort. Selling is strongest for bulky, low-value, easily replaced furniture. It is weakest for well-made pieces that cost far more to replace than they fetch used, and for anything with genuine personal value.

Count the full cost of each option, not the headline

The storage unit's headline is the monthly rent. Its full cost is longer:

  • Rent for every month the gap actually runs — including the partial final month if the agreement bills monthly.
  • One-time and add-on fees — an administration fee at move-in is common, and most storage operators require the contents to be insured, either through cover the operator offers or through a suitable home insurance policy.
  • A lock, and the trips — at minimum a trip in and a trip out, plus fuel or a second truck rental if the move-in day needs one.
  • Double handling — everything is loaded, unloaded, loaded, and unloaded again. Moving belongings twice, once to a temporary spot and once to the new home, is significantly more expensive and disruptive than one move with storage in between — which is precisely the comparison to run against simply extending a lease or paying for a mover's own storage.

Selling has a full cost too: the time to photograph, list, and meet buyers; any marketplace or delivery costs; and the replacement cost later, which for most furniture categories is several times the used sale price. Storing with family has the smallest cash cost and the largest relationship cost, and it scales badly — a favour sized for two weeks becomes a different favour at two months.

The break-even rule the table demonstrates: selling has a fixed net cost — replacement cost minus sale proceeds. Storage has a cost that grows every month. Divide the selling option's net cost by the unit's true monthly cost (rent plus insurance) and the result is the break-even month: gaps shorter than it favour storage, gaps longer than it favour selling. In the example, $740 ÷ $200 ≈ 3.7 months. A useful ceiling follows from the same math: never let the running storage total exceed what the stored items would cost to replace minus what they would sell for — past that point the unit is storing a loss. One mover's gap guide makes the same point bluntly: the longest gaps are the ones people under-think, ending with months of rent paid to store a sofa that would have been replaced anyway.

Match the option to the length of the gap

Gap length sorts most of the decision before money enters it. A published gap guide from a removal firm breaks it down this way: a gap of one to three days is usually handled by leaving the load on the truck or a short hold at the depot, because unloading into a unit and reloading days later wastes the effort; four days to three weeks suits removalist-managed storage in sealed modules; three weeks to three months suits module storage, or self-storage when regular access is needed; and three months or more calls for long-term storage plus a hard look at what is worth keeping at all. Family storage fits the same scale from the other direction: it is the natural answer for days-to-a-few-weeks, and the strained answer beyond that.

The decision framework: five questions

  1. How long is the gap, honestly? Use the pessimistic date, not the hoped-for one. A gap that "should be ten days" with an unfinished renovation behind it is a one-to-two-month plan.
  2. What survives the sort? Run the keep / donate / sell / store sort first. Storage of any kind should only ever hold the store pile — most households find that pile is one unit size smaller than the whole household.
  3. What is the break-even month? Compute it from real quotes: (replacement cost − expected sale proceeds) ÷ (monthly rent + insurance). Compare it against the honest gap length from question one.
  4. Does anything need climate control or real security? Wood furniture, electronics, instruments, and paper records argue for an indoor or climate-controlled unit over a garage; anything irreplaceable argues against every option except carrying it personally — storage guidance is consistent that valuables and essential documents stay with the household, not in the unit.
  5. Whose labour moves it twice? If the answer is "the same family members who are hosting it," price that honestly. A unit near the new home, loaded once by the moving crew, often beats a free garage across town once double handling is counted.

Canada notes

  • Month-to-month is the norm, and agreements typically run on rolling terms with a short notice period — confirm the notice period and any minimum-stay or admin fees before signing, because those terms set the true cost of a short gap.
  • Insurance is usually required, not optional. Check whether an existing home or tenant policy extends to stored belongings before buying the operator's cover — and keep a labelled inventory list and photos of everything stored, which both the claim process and the eventual move-out depend on.
  • Movers can deliver straight into storage. Many moving firms offer their own storage or will deliver to and collect from a self-storage facility, which removes one full round of handling from the plan.
  • Season matters. An unheated drive-up unit in January is a different environment from the same unit in July. Anything that would be damaged by freezing, condensation, or summer humidity belongs in an indoor or climate-controlled space, or in the sell pile.

What success looks like

The decision is working when: the stored pile was sorted before it was stored, so nothing in the unit is paying rent on a future donation; the break-even month was computed from real quotes and the honest gap is shorter than it (or the sell decision was made instead); the unit is one size smaller than the first instinct, because the sort removed the rest; and every stored item is on a photographed inventory list, with documents and irreplaceables travelling with the household. If the gap stretches, the check repeats monthly: rent paid to date plus one more month, against the same replacement-minus-proceeds ceiling. The month the ceiling is crossed is the month the remaining contents get sold or donated — decided by the arithmetic, not by inertia.

FAQ

Is a storage unit worth it for a two-week gap?

Usually yes if the alternative is two full moves, and usually no if family storage nearby can absorb two weeks without strain. At two weeks the unit's one-time fees and double handling dominate its rent, so compare the full cost — and ask movers about a short depot hold or their own storage first, which can be cheaper than a self-storage round trip at this length.

What should never go into a storage unit during a gap?

Essential documents, medications, irreplaceable items, and anything needed during the gap itself. Storage guidance consistently advises keeping valuables and essential documents with the household. Perishables and anything the facility's agreement prohibits are also out — read that list before loading, not after.

When does selling beat storing?

When the gap is long, the pieces are bulky and common, and the break-even math says so: if the total storage cost over the honest gap exceeds the replacement cost minus the sale proceeds, selling is the cheaper option — and it also removes a second move from the calendar entirely.
