The $39.99 illusion

The Real Total Cost of Renting a Moving Truck in Canada: Mileage, Fuel, and the Fees After the Headline Rate

A short Montreal move published at about $247 started life as a $39.99 headline. The gap between those two numbers is not a scam. It is a list, and you can price every line on it before you book.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

About $247 is what a published Montreal example charges for a short local move that was advertised at $39.99. Mileage $79, gas $80, damage waiver $16, environmental and trip fees $10, tax $21.71, and the truck itself at $39.99. Add them and the total is roughly six times the number on the sign. Nothing on that list is exotic, and that is the point of this article: a truck rental total is a receipt you can rebuild in advance, line by line, from your own route and choices, instead of a surprise you meet at the return counter. The figures below are published Canadian examples checked November 13, 2026, labelled as examples, because your city's live quote is the only price that can book your truck. Two lines do most of the damage, and neither is the truck. Mileage and gas together come to $159 in this example, about two thirds of the total. A household that comparison shops the daily rate and ignores its own route is optimizing the smallest part of the bill. The same source's long distance receipt makes the point at scale: a one way Montreal to Calgary rental in December 2024 came to $3,758.54 on the card plus about $1,400 in gas. Distance is not a detail in truck rental pricing. It is the pricing.

How wide the range runs: an Ottawa cross check

A second published Canadian guide, built around Ottawa local moves, prices the same job from the other direction. Its example puts a 10 foot truck at about $29.95 per day and a 20 foot truck at approximately $39.95 to $59.95 per day for local moves returned to the same location, with local mileage at $0.89 to $1.09 per kilometre and a typical local move covering 30 to 60 kilometres. Run its mileage math and a local move adds about $27 to $65 of mileage before fuel, protection, or tax. Its protection examples, Safemove at $14 per day and Safemove Plus at $28 per day, sit in the same range as the Montreal waiver line. Two cities, two independent examples, one shape: the headline is a quarter to a sixth of the honest total, and mileage is the swing line.

U-Haul's own published starting rates, as cited in rental research, underline the same caution. Pickup, cargo van, and 10 foot trucks are listed from $19.95 per day, 15 foot from $29.95, 20 foot from $39.95, and 26 foot from $49.95, and the company labels these as starting rates. Its comparison material states that in town and one way rates exclude fuel, taxes, fees, and optional add ons. Starting at is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and now you know exactly which lines it excludes.

Mileage: the structure decides the winner

Before comparing companies, compare structures. A per kilometre local rental rewards a short, tight route and punishes every detour: the depot trip, the storage stop, the second load, the return. An unlimited mileage structure, where one is offered for a short local rental window, removes that line entirely and replaces it with a time limit, which punishes a slow load instead. Neither structure is better in general. Each is better for a specific move, and your move has a measurable shape:

  1. Map the full route you will actually drive, depot to old home to new home to depot, including any storage or second trip legs.
  2. Price that distance at the per kilometre rate on any quote you are considering. At the Ottawa example rates, every 10 kilometres is about $9 to $11.
  3. If your total distance is small and your loading is slow, per kilometre billing usually wins. If your distance is large and your loading is fast and single trip, ask explicitly about unlimited mileage options and their time windows.
  4. Write the mileage line into your budget at your mapped distance, not at the distance you hope for.

Fuel: estimate it before you drive

Fuel is the line renters treat as weather, and it is arithmetic. The Ottawa example estimates a 20 foot truck at approximately 14 to 17 litres per 100 kilometres. Your mapped route from the mileage step, multiplied by that consumption range, gives a fuel volume before you leave: a 60 kilometre local day in a 20 foot truck works out to roughly 8 to 10 litres, while a 500 kilometre day works out to roughly 70 to 85 litres. Multiply by the pump price on your street that week and the gas line stops being a surprise. One rule from the rental research governs the end of the day: the truck returns at the fuel level recorded at pickup, so photograph the gauge and the odometer before you drive away. A return below that level is billed by the company, on its terms, not at your pump price.

The five minute quote audit

  1. Base rate and day count. Confirm the truck size, the daily rate, and what counts as a day, including the return deadline.
  2. Mileage. Ask whether mileage is included, capped, or billed per kilometre, get the rate, and price your mapped route.
  3. Fuel. Record the pickup fuel level policy and estimate your litres from the route, as above.
  4. Protection. Decide on the waiver or protection plan before the counter, using the published per day examples as your scale, and check what your own auto policy or credit card already covers, in writing.
  5. Fees and tax. Ask for the environmental and trip fees by name, then add your province's tax to the rebuilt total, not to the headline.
  6. Extras. Dollies, blankets, and a second day are all lines too. Price them now or decline them now; the counter is the most expensive place to decide.

What success looks like: the total on your return receipt lands within a few dollars of the total you rebuilt before booking, and every difference has a name you recognize from the audit. A rental priced that way has no headline left to hide behind, which is the whole point of renting like Penny: the bottom line first, with the working shown.
