Utility Transfers When Moving: The Timing Plan That Avoids Dark Houses and Double Bills

"Call two weeks before" is the only advice most guides give. Real providers run on different clocks — one lets you book a year ahead, another changes your account number when you move. Time each service by its own rules.

Moving on a Budget · Budget move guide

Utility transfers fail in exactly two ways, and both cost money. The gap: you arrive at a dark, cold house with no internet because a start date slipped, and you pay for it in hotel nights, takeout, and a day of work lost. The overlap: services keep running — and billing — at a home you no longer live in, or a final bill goes to an address you have left and quietly becomes a late payment. The generic advice ("contact your providers about two weeks out") prevents neither, because providers do not share one clock, one form, or one definition of a move.

This guide replaces the rule of thumb with the providers' own published mechanics — what each one lets you schedule, how far ahead, and what changes when you move — then builds the week-by-week procedure around them, and prices the one piece of cheap insurance almost nobody budgets for: mail forwarding. Ontario providers are the worked examples because their move rules are published and checkable; the procedure itself works anywhere the names differ.

Two patterns in that table are worth money. First, the account number changes: both Enbridge Gas and Hydro One issue a new one when you move, and Enbridge explicitly warns you to update it in your bank's payment settings so pre-authorized payments land on the new account instead of a closed one. A "paid" bill credited to a dead account is the overlap failure in its sneakiest form. Second, the scheduling windows are wildly unequal — a gas move can be locked in a year ahead, while the general guidance for closing an electricity or gas account is a minimum of five business days' notice, with about a month recommended. Booking early is free; booking late is where fees, rushed technician visits, and gaps breed.

The transfer procedure, week by week

  1. 4 weeks out — build the tracker. One list, every service: electricity, gas, water (confirm with your landlord or municipality whether it is even yours to move), internet, phone, and any rental equipment. For each: provider, account number, start date wanted, stop date wanted, and a blank column for the confirmation number. The tracker is the deliverable — a transfer you cannot point to a confirmation for is a hope, not a booking.
  2. 3–4 weeks out — book the appointment-driven services first. Anything that needs a technician or an installation visit goes first, because those calendars fill and the general two-week guidance assumes your service is a simple account transfer. Transferring service itself usually costs nothing; the published exceptions are services that require a technician visit — and charges for equipment you fail to return, like cable boxes. Pack your modem and router in your essentials load, labelled, not in a random box.
  3. 2 weeks out — submit the account transfers. This is the window the generic advice gets right for simple transfers: schedule the new services to start on your move-in date (or the day before, if you are cleaning and setting up ahead), and the old services to stop about 24 hours after you move out, so the old home still has light, heat, and water while you load and clean. Give every provider a forwarding address for the final bill.
  4. Move week — confirm, don't assume. Re-check each confirmation, verify technician access instructions for the new address, and photograph the meters at the old home as you leave and the new home as you arrive. A dated meter photo is your evidence if a final bill spans usage that was never yours.
  5. Move-in day — test before the truck leaves. Outlets and breakers, hot water, heating or cooling, and internet equipment connected and live. Report a dead service the same day, while "we just moved in" is still a complete explanation and your tracker shows the booking was made properly.
  6. First month — close the loop. Final bills arrive and old balances settle. Pay finals from the tracker, confirm the old accounts read closed, and update the new account numbers anywhere you pay automatically.

Price the safety net: mail forwarding

Forwarding is the overlap problem's cheap fix: it catches the senders you forgot — and there is always one — before a missed bill becomes a late fee or a lost document becomes a replacement fee. Canada Post's residential pricing for a permanent move, checked October 13, 2026: $66.50 for 4 months or $100.75 for 12 months within a province; $82.75 or $126.25 across provinces. Per month, the 12-month term costs roughly $8.40 against the 4-month term's $16.60 — the long term is the value pick unless you are certain every sender has your new address within four months. Up to four residents' names are included in the base fee; extra names are $5.00 each. And forwarding is a safety net, not a substitute: it does not replace telling your bank, your insurer, and the government directly, which the address-change checklist walks through.

Tip: Deliberate overlap is a purchase, and it should be a small one. A day or two of double service at both homes costs a few dollars of basic charges and buys you light and heat in both places on the busiest days of the move. A month of accidental overlap because nobody filed the stop is the same product at ten times the price. Decide the overlap; don't discover it.

What success looks like

You have transferred well when moving day is boring: power, heat, water, and internet live at the new home when you arrive; the old home's services end the day after you leave, on schedule; every line on your tracker carries a confirmation number; and the only bills that reach you at the new address are the ones you expected, including finals that close the old accounts at zero. The test a month later: no service billing at an address you have left, no payment stranded on a closed account number, and a forwarding notice or two from senders you forgot — caught by the net, not by a collections letter. Fold this schedule into your moving-day timeline and the utilities stop being a risk item at all.

FAQ

When should I transfer utilities when moving?

Book appointment-driven services 3–4 weeks out, submit simple account transfers about 2 weeks out, start new service on (or just before) move-in day, and stop old service about 24 hours after you move out. Where a provider lets you schedule earlier — Enbridge Gas takes move requests up to a year ahead — earlier beats the generic window every time.

How much does it cost to transfer utilities?

Usually nothing for the transfer itself. Budget instead for the edges: a one-time new-account charge can appear on a first bill (Enbridge Gas applies one, covering account and meter setup), technician-required work can carry charges, and unreturned rental equipment is billed if you leave it behind. Mail forwarding is the one predictable line item: $66.50–$126.25 for residential terms, per the Canada Post prices above.

What if I'm renting and utilities are in my landlord's name?

Then your job is confirmation, not transfer: verify in writing which services your rent includes, which accounts are yours to open, and the dates the landlord's services run to. The tracker still applies — the account holder column just says "landlord" — because the gap failure does not care whose name was on the account.
