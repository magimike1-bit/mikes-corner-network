Holiday Concert Survival: Festive, Loud in Places, and Very Long in Others

🎄 Concert · 7 min read

Two hours. That is the honest starting number, and almost no official concert sheet will give it to you. Parent guidance that does admit the shape of the evening puts it plainly: with every class getting a moment, most concerts stretch over a couple of hours, while your own child's part lasts a few minutes. A few minutes of yours, a couple of hours of everyone's. That ratio is not a reason to dread the night. It is the reason the night needs a plan — one that treats the loud, festive parts as the entertainment they are, and the long, quiet stretches between them as a pacing problem with known solutions.

The honest shape of the evening

Think of the programme as a watch order rather than a single sitting. Your child's set is the headline act. The sets either side of it are the supporting bill: worth watching properly, because the children in them have parents scanning the room exactly as you will. Further away in the running order sit the stretches where attention is genuinely hard to hold — another class, another key, another seasonal standard — and those stretches are where tired parents are won or lost. The official etiquette sheets never describe any of this. They list behaviour rules, and the rules are sound, but a list of rules is not a map of the evening. The map matters more. Once the shape is clear — headline, supporting bill, long middle — each part gets a different kind of attention, and none of it has to be faked.

Before: arrive early, sit strategically

Run the arrival as a short procedure, because the seat chosen in the first five minutes decides how the next two hours feel:

  1. Arrive early enough to choose, not just to sit. An early arrival picks a seat. A late arrival takes the seat that is left, usually in the middle of a row, with no exit that does not disturb a dozen people.
  2. Take an aisle seat near the back if leaving early is even a possibility. Parents who discuss the leave-early question give this as the core move: sit on an aisle, not in front, so any departure is inconspicuous.
  3. Put younger siblings on the exit side. Parent guidance for siblings is consistent: sit near an exit, feed them beforehand, and bring quiet snacks or a small distraction. The exit side of the row is where that advice becomes physical.
  4. Settle everything before the first number. Coat off, programme read, water located, phone dark. Settling is a one-time cost; fidgeting charges interest all evening.

What success looks like: when the lights drop, the family is seated, fed, briefed and still — and the parent knows exactly which number in the programme belongs to their child.

During your child's set: be the audience they scan for

For those few minutes, no tactics are needed and none should be used. Children look out. The face they are looking for should be upright, awake and visibly pleased. The official guidance frames the wider duty the same way from the other side: remain seated and quiet while a performance is in progress. During your own child's set that rule is not a constraint at all. It is simply what being found looks like from the stage.

During everyone else's: the etiquette that counts

The official sheets reduce, for a tired parent, to three rules that carry nearly all the weight:

  • Remain seated while a performance is in progress, and leave only between numbers. Movement mid-number is the one thing every source agrees disturbs the room.
  • Clap; do not whistle or cheer. The sheets name clapping, not whistling or cheering, as the appropriate appreciation. Applause is also the tired parent's friend: it is active, it is expected, and it briefly wakes the hands and the brain together.
  • Film from your seat, or not at all. When taking pictures or video, remain seated so others can see. A screen raised from a standing position turns one family's recording into another family's view of the evening. Seated, brief and low beats standing, continuous and high — every time, for everyone behind.

None of these rules asks for rapt attention to every note. They ask for stillness, applause in the right currency, and a camera that stays in its seat. A parent can be quietly tired and meet all three without acting at all.

The leave-early question, answered straight

Official sources will not touch this question. Parents, in their own forums, answer it directly — and their answer is more principled than the guilt version. Decide with three checks:

  • Stay if your child will look for you at the end. One reason parents give for staying is that staying teaches a child to support others even when they are no longer performing. If the finale, the bow or the walk out matters to your child, it is part of their set, wherever it falls in the programme.
  • Leave between numbers if you must leave. The forum rule is precise: leave between numbers, ideally at a change-over, never during a number. The aisle seat chosen earlier is what makes this possible without a row-wide apology.
  • Leave before tiredness becomes driving. A parent who is fading in a warm hall has a second journey to make afterwards. Slipping out quietly at a change-over beats staying to the last note and then driving home exhausted. No programme note outranks a safe drive.

What the decision never requires is sneaking out mid-song from the middle of a row. That is the only version of leaving that the etiquette actually forbids, and it is also, happily, the least necessary one.

Younger siblings: the exit-row plan

A younger sibling experiences the couple of hours at full length, with none of the context that makes it bearable for adults. The parent guidance is short and complete: sit near an exit, feed them beforehand, bring quiet snacks or a small distraction. Add one scheduling truth of your own: a sibling who lasts an hour happily and leaves quietly at a change-over has had a successful concert. The finale is for the child on stage. The small child in row nine is allowed a shorter evening — and the parent holding both plans at once is doing the concert properly, all the way to the last, loud, festive number.
