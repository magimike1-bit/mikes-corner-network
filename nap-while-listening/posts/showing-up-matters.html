Why showing up matters even when you're tired (the sincere one)

The honest truth · 6 min read

We've spent four articles joking about strategic seating and the thoughtful nod, so let's put the comedy down for a minute. This one's real.

Ask any adult what they remember about their childhood performances — the school plays, the recitals, the science fair — and the details they recall first are almost never about the event itself. They remember who was there. They remember scanning the audience and finding a face. They remember the drive home, when someone asked how it went and actually listened to the answer.

Your presence is the point. Not your energy level. Not your posture. Not whether you caught every word of the group project about water filtration. The fact that you came — tired, stretched thin, running on fumes — is the thing that sticks.

Kids measure love in attendance

Children, especially younger ones, don't have a sophisticated theory of adult exhaustion. What they do have is a very simple, very accurate accounting system: were you there? That binary question carries more weight than anything else. You showed up on a Tuesday night after a full day of work, sat in an uncomfortable chair, and watched. In their ledger, that's not "barely hung on." That's devotion.

And here's the part worth hearing: they can tell the difference between "asleep in the back row" and "fought through tiredness to be here," and the second one matters enormously to them. Even if you drifted for a stretch, even if you had to deploy every trick in this guide — you were there when the applause started, and you clapped, and you asked about it after. That counts. That counts a lot.

The drive home is the real event

Here's a practical takeaway from all the silliness: the presentation ends, but the memory is still being written on the way home. That's your moment. Ask one real question — "What part were you most nervous about?" or "Which part did you like best?" — and listen to the answer. Genuinely listen. That five-minute conversation does more for your kid than a perfect, wide-awake performance as an audience member ever could.

Know when NOT to nap

All jokes aside, part of showing up is knowing which moments deserve your full, eyes-open attention. Not every event is equal:

  • The firsts. First performance, first presentation, first time on a stage — these are once-in-a-lifetime. Bring the coffee, sit where they can see you, and be fully there.
  • The solos. When your kid is the only one up there — a solo, a speech, a single line in the play — they're looking for you the whole time. Make sure they find you, awake.
  • The things they worked hardest on. You know which ones these are. They talked about it at dinner for a week. They were nervous. Those deserve your best self.
  • The lasts. Last recital before they quit, last year of elementary school, the final game of the season. Endings are heavy. Be present for them.

For everything else — the third class assembly of the semester, the group project where your kid has one line — give yourself grace. You're allowed to be tired. You're allowed to be human.

The long game

Years from now, your kid won't remember whether you nodded off during slide seven. What they'll remember is the pattern: you came. Again and again, you came. And one day they'll be the tired adult in the uncomfortable chair, and they'll understand — really understand — what it cost you to be there, and they'll love you for it in a whole new way.

So keep the nod technique in your back pocket. Use the recovery lines when you need them. But never doubt the main thing: showing up tired is still showing up, and showing up is everything.
