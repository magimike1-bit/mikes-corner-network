Virtual Presentation Survival: Dozing Politely on a School Video Call

💻 Video call · 6 min read

Join with your video on and your microphone off, stay muted unless you are talking, and assume you are visible at all times. Those three rules — drawn from a school board's own video-conferencing guidance and parent meeting etiquette — are the whole survival kit for a school video call, stated in one breath. Everything else here is commentary. The video call looks like the easy option compared with a gymnasium: no drive, no tiny chair, your own sofa. It is, in one respect, the most dangerous format on this site, because the camera changes the contract. In a hall, a tired parent is one face in a crowd. On a call, a tired parent is a named rectangle, and the rectangle is being watched.

The spec, verified: what the school guidance actually says

School-specific rules are easy to miss — one key source is a school board PDF written for classrooms, not a page parents find by searching. So here is the specification, checked and laid out plainly:

RuleWhat the guidance says
Joining stateJoin with video on and microphone off; students turn microphones on before sharing
VisibilityAssume that you are visible at all times
Mute disciplineStay on mute if you are not talking
RecordingParents are not allowed to share photos or videos of students from video conferencing
EatingDo not eat during the meeting — or turn off video and audio until done
PreparationTest settings before the meeting begins; a pre-meeting test is offered

Two lines in that table deserve underlining. The recording rule is not a preference: parents are not allowed to share photos or videos of students from video conferencing, full stop — screenshot the timetable, never the children. And the visibility rule is the one that governs all the others. Assume visible at all times is not paranoia. It is the operating assumption the rest of the plan is built on.

Before anyone joins: the audience member's set-up

Parent audiences split, usefully, into three situations — and the set-up differs for each. Find your row and run its version.

Watching from home, alone. Your risks are comfort risks: the sofa, the low light, the blanket within reach. Test settings before the meeting begins — the etiquette guidance names this step and notes that a pre-meeting test is available — then choose the chair that keeps you upright over the chair that invites a recline. Frame the camera before anyone arrives to be framed at. A parent who has tested audio and settled the picture can spend the call watching instead of troubleshooting, and troubleshooting on a live school call is how a muted parent becomes an unmuted spectacle.

Watching with your child beside you. Your child is a participant in waiting: students turn microphones on before sharing, so the household rule is simple — the microphone stays off until your child's moment, and goes on deliberately, never by a curious hand. Seat the child where the camera already points. A presentation is not the moment to discover that the frame cuts off exactly the person presenting.

Watching from somewhere that is not home. The visibility rule travels with you. Non-verbal behaviour is visible on a call — eye-rolling, texting and playing with a pet all communicate to other participants — and a background you would not choose is communicating too. If you must move locations mid-call, the etiquette guidance gives the correct sequence: turn the camera off first, then move. Camera off, then feet. Never the reverse.

Mute is your resting state

Of the three opening rules, mute does the most protective work. Stay on mute if you are not talking — the parent-meeting guidance states it flatly, and the school guidance builds joining itself around it: microphone off on entry. Mute is what makes a long call survivable for a tired household. A yawn behind a muted microphone and a camera-aware face is a private event. A yawn into a live microphone is a contribution to the meeting. The discipline is mechanical rather than moral: mute on joining, mute after any moment of speaking, and treat the unmute button the way a presenter treats it — a deliberate act, never a resting position for a cursor.

Eating follows the same logic. The guidance offers two lawful options and no third: do not eat during the meeting, or turn off video and audio until done. A parent who needs food during a long call takes the second option completely — both switches, not one — and returns framed, muted and present.

Camera on, attention plausible: the middle path

Between performing enthusiasm and visibly drifting sits the sustainable middle: camera on, face composed, reactions small and genuine. The reason small matters is documented — non-verbals are visible on video, and the catalogue of tells includes eye-rolling, texting and fussing with a pet. Each of those communicates to the whole call, not just to a neighbour. The middle path therefore has a short rule set. Eyes return to the screen after any glance away. The phone lives outside the frame's reach — texting reads as texting, at any angle. Reactions stay at nod scale: a nod survives a grid view, a theatrical expression becomes the grid's main event. None of this requires energy a tired parent does not have. It requires only the decision, made once, that the rectangle with your name on it will look like someone listening — because, under the visibility rule, it is being read as one.

When your child presents: the one decision that matters

Every other choice on the call is etiquette. This one is the point. When your child shares, the household's whole posture changes: microphone on for the sharing, as the school guidance describes, camera steady, and the parent audience doing the one job it joined for — being visibly there for the minutes that are theirs. Decide it in advance, as a framework, because a tired parent should not be designing a response live:

  • Before the moment: camera on, already tested, already framed — nothing to fix while your child is starting.
  • During it: watch the presentation, not the grid. Other rectangles can wait; this one cannot be replayed, and recording it is exactly what the rules do not allow parents to share.
  • After it: back to muted, camera on, resting state — and one specific thing noticed, ready to name afterwards. A parent who can name a specific moment was, by any measure that matters to a child, awake for it.

The call will fade from memory by the weekend — they all do. What remains is simpler and better: your child presented, the rectangle with your name on it was present and composed and clearly watching, and not one rule in the school guidance was bent to achieve it. Dozing politely, it turns out, is mostly a matter of never needing to doze at all: muted by default, visible by assumption, and fully awake for the only minutes that were ever the point.
