Battery and cooling fixes: stop the heat, save the battery

Maintenance · 8 min read

An old laptop that's slow, loud, and hot is often telling you one simple thing: it can't breathe. Dust builds up in vents and fans over the years, airflow drops, temperatures rise, and the processor slows itself down to avoid damage — that's the sluggishness you're feeling. Meanwhile the battery has been quietly degrading since day one. This guide covers the maintenance basics for both: cleaning the cooling system and understanding what's left in the battery.

Cleaning the cooling system

Dust is the enemy. It clogs intake vents, mats itself across fan blades, and insulates the heat sink so it can't shed heat. The symptoms of a clogged cooling system are distinctive: the fan runs loud and constantly, the machine gets hot to the touch, and performance drops after a few minutes of use (that's thermal throttling — the processor slowing down to stay cool).

Cleaning, from least to most involved:

  1. Blow out the vents. With the laptop off and unplugged, use short bursts of compressed air through the intake and exhaust vents. Hold the can upright so no liquid propellant comes out, and don't spin the fan up to absurd speeds with the air blast — a finger or a cotton swab can hold it still.
  2. Open the bottom panel if the design allows easy access, and gently clean the fan blades and visible vents with a soft brush or more compressed air.
  3. Replace the thermal paste — only if you're comfortable going deeper. The paste between the processor and the heat sink dries out over the years and transfers heat less well. Replacing it means removing the heat sink, cleaning off the old paste, and applying a small amount of new paste. This is the most effective cooling fix on very old machines, but it also involves the most disassembly. Watch a teardown video for your exact model first, and if it looks beyond your comfort level, a repair shop will do it for a modest fee.

While you're in there, look for anything that obstructs airflow — sticker residue over vents, a warped panel, a fan that doesn't spin freely. And make sure you're not making things worse in daily use: laptops need their vents clear. Using a laptop on a bed or a couch blocks the intakes on the bottom. A hard, flat surface (or a simple cooling stand) makes a real difference.

Reading your battery's health

All rechargeable batteries wear out — it's chemistry, not a defect. Every charge cycle shaves a little off the maximum capacity. After several years, a battery that once lasted four hours might hold forty minutes. That's normal aging.

You can check how far gone yours is. On Windows, the battery report (generated from a command-line tool) compares the battery's original design capacity with its current full-charge capacity. macOS shows battery health in the power settings. Linux has similar reporting tools. The number to look at is the current full-charge capacity as a percentage of the design capacity — that's your battery's real health.

What the number tells you:

  • Above roughly 80%: the battery is in decent shape. Manage your expectations and your settings (below) and it should serve fine.
  • Noticeably below that: the battery is tired. You can keep using it plugged in most of the time, or replace it — which is often cheap and easy on laptops with removable batteries.
  • Swollen or deformed: stop. A swollen battery is a fire risk. Don't charge it, don't puncture it, don't throw it in the trash. Take it to a battery or electronics recycling drop-off and get the laptop serviced. This is the one battery situation that is genuinely urgent.

Replacing the battery

On laptops with a removable external battery, replacement is trivial: a latch, a slide, done. On laptops with internal batteries, it's a few screws and a connector — check the service manual for your model. Either way, buy from a reputable source. Cheap unbranded batteries are a gamble on capacity and, in rare cases, safety. If the laptop will mostly live on a desk, consider whether you even need a battery at all: many laptops run fine on wall power alone, and removing a dead battery you never use just removes a problem.

Heat management in daily use

Beyond cleaning, a few habits keep an old laptop cooler:

  • Cut the busywork. Fewer background programs means less heat. Trim startup items and close the thirty browser tabs.
  • Use power-saving modes. Your OS has a battery-saver or power-efficiency mode that caps the processor's top speed. On a revived old laptop, you often can't tell the difference in normal use — but the fan can.
  • Elevate it. Even a centimeter of clearance under the laptop improves airflow. A basic stand or even two books at the back edge helps.
  • Mind the environment. A laptop in a hot room with blocked vents is fighting a losing battle. Shade and airflow matter.

The payoff of this maintenance is a laptop that stays quiet, holds its speed under load, and stops crashing or shutting down from overheating — which, on some neglected old machines, it was doing regularly. Cooling maintenance is unglamorous and free, which makes it the best value of any fix in this guide series.
