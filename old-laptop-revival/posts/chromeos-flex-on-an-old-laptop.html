ChromeOS Flex on an old laptop: try it from USB before you commit

Operating systems · 10 min read

ChromeOS Flex is Google's free operating system for computers that were not born as Chromebooks. It replaces Windows or macOS with a light, browser-first system that boots quickly, updates itself in the background, and asks very little of old hardware. For a laptop whose main jobs are email, documents in the browser, video calls, and streaming, it can feel like a new machine for the price of a USB stick.

The timing matters. Microsoft ended support for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025, and no longer provides security updates for it outside the Extended Security Updates (ESU) program, which covers eligible consumer devices until October 12, 2027. A laptop that cannot run Windows 11 is therefore on borrowed time as a Windows machine — which makes a free, still-supported alternative worth a serious look. But Flex is a one-way door if you install it carelessly: installing erases the internal drive. This guide works in the safe order — check the machine, learn the limits, test from USB, and only then install.

Check the machine against Google's minimums first

Google publishes exact minimum requirements, and they are a genuine pass/fail gate, not a suggestion. Before downloading anything, verify each line against your laptop:

  • Processor: an Intel or AMD chip with 64-bit (x86-64) architecture. Flex does not support ARM processors, so Apple Silicon Macs are out entirely.
  • Memory: at least 4 GB of RAM.
  • Storage: at least 16 GB of internal storage.
  • Boot: the laptop must be able to boot from a USB drive, and you need full administrator access to its BIOS settings in case the boot order has to change.
  • Age of components: Google says Intel or AMD processor and graphics components from 2012 and earlier, and Nvidia graphics from 2014 and earlier, are not recommended or supported and are unlikely to work. A laptop from 2013 onward is the realistic starting point.
  • USB stick: 8 GB or larger for the installer — and everything on that stick gets erased when the installer is created, so use one you can spare.

One more distinction the quick guides skip: Google only guarantees that Flex works on models on its certified models list. On a certified model, the guaranteed list is long — audio in and out, the internal display, video output, Wi-Fi or Ethernet, the touchpad and keyboard, sleep and resume, USB, and the webcam. A second group of features is tested but not guaranteed even on certified models: Bluetooth, automatic screen rotation, some keyboard shortcuts and function keys, touchscreens, and SD card slots. A laptop that is not on the list may still run Flex well, but nothing is promised — which is exactly why the USB test drive below matters more than any spec sheet.

What Flex will not do

Flex is not a Chromebook in every respect, and two limits decide most real-world cases. First, there is no Google Play Store: Flex does not run Android apps (Google notes only that some Android VPN apps can be deployed by administrators). If the plan for this laptop involves Android apps, Flex is the wrong tool. Second, it does not run Windows programs — there is no support for Windows virtual machines through Parallels Desktop, and installed Windows software simply has no home here. Work that lives in the browser is fine; work that lives in a specific Windows application is not.

Smaller print worth knowing before you commit: support for the Linux development environment varies by model, and because the laptop keeps its original keyboard, some shortcuts and printed function keys will not behave the way they do on a real Chromebook. None of this is hidden — Google publishes it on its Flex-versus-ChromeOS differences page — but it rarely makes it into install guides, and it is much better read now than discovered after an erase.

A useful pre-check, before any of this: look at what the laptop actually does all day. If nearly everything already happens inside a browser window, Flex fits the machine's real life. If the day depends on installed programs, keep reading the site's other options instead.

Try it from USB before you erase anything

The installer doubles as a test drive. Booting Flex from the USB stick runs the whole system without touching the internal drive, so you can check the Wi-Fi, keyboard, trackpad, sound, and webcam on your exact hardware, then shut down and be back in Windows as if nothing happened. The procedure:

  1. Back up first anyway. Copy anything you cannot afford to lose off the laptop. The test drive does not erase the drive, but the install step later does, and backups made in a hurry get forgotten.
  2. Create the installer. On a working computer with the Chrome browser, add Google's Chromebook Recovery Utility extension, open it, choose Get started, select Google ChromeOS Flex from the model list, insert your 8 GB or larger USB stick, and let the utility write the installer to it. Remember: the stick is erased in the process.
  3. Boot the old laptop from the stick. Insert the USB drive, power on, and press the boot-menu key immediately — commonly F12, F9, Esc, or Del, depending on the maker — then choose the USB device. If Windows starts instead, restart and press the key earlier.
  4. Choose the try-first option on the welcome screen rather than installing, connect to your Wi-Fi, and spend real time in it: open the sites and web apps the laptop is actually used for, join a video call, print something if printing matters, and put it to sleep and wake it.
  5. Judge the hardware, not the speed. Running from a USB stick is slower than running from the internal drive, so a slightly sluggish test drive is normal. What you are checking is whether everything works — a dead Wi-Fi card or a silent speaker in the test drive is a real answer, and it is free.

Install, step by step

If the test drive passes and the backup is done, installing is the shortest part of the whole job — Engadget's walkthrough of the process puts the total at about an hour, most of it spent creating the installer and testing:

  1. Boot from the same USB installer and, on the welcome screen, choose Install ChromeOS Flex instead of the try-first option.
  2. Read the on-screen warning carefully: installing erases everything on the internal drive. Confirm only when the backup is verified.
  3. Let the installer finish. The laptop powers itself off when it is done.
  4. Remove the USB stick, power the laptop back on, connect to Wi-Fi, and sign in with a Google account to complete setup.

Cost check, dated October 13, 2026: Flex itself is a free download from Google, and a spare USB stick is the only hardware involved. Google also sells an optional pre-made installer kit in the US for $3, reported by TechRepublic — a convenience for people who would rather not build the stick themselves, not a requirement.

What success looks like

A successful conversion is boring in the best way: the laptop boots from its internal drive to the Flex sign-in screen without the USB stick, joins your Wi-Fi on its own, and every device you tested — trackpad, keyboard, sound, webcam, sleep and wake — behaves the way it did in the test drive, only faster. Your files are reachable again from your backup or cloud storage, and updates arrive automatically in the background, which is the whole point: this machine is supported again.

Flex, Windows, or Linux: the decision in one table

PathBest whenWatch out for
ChromeOS FlexThe laptop's work is browser-based and it meets Google's minimumsNo Android apps, no Windows programs; full guarantees only on certified models
Stay on WindowsThe machine runs Windows 11, or Windows 10 with ESU cover until October 12, 2027An unsupported Windows 10 machine without ESU receives no security updates
Lightweight LinuxYou need real desktop programs and offline files, and can accept a learning curveCovered in the site's lightweight operating systems guide

Whichever path you choose, the order of operations is the same one this site recommends for every revival: back up, verify against published requirements, test without erasing, and commit last. Flex rewards that order more than most — the test drive is free, and the install is forever.
