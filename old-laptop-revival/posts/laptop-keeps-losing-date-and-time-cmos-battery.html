Laptop keeps losing its date and time: is it really the CMOS battery?

Troubleshooting · 7 min read

Why does an old laptop wake up thinking it is years in the past? The short answer is that the clock is kept alive by a small battery when the machine is off, and that battery is a wear item. The longer answer, the one that saves you opening the case for nothing, is that a wrong clock has three possible owners: Windows time synchronisation, the main battery on newer designs, and the CMOS battery itself. They are checked in that order here, cheapest first.

First, rule out Windows

A clock that is wrong by hours, or wrong only in Windows, is often a settings problem wearing a hardware costume. Two checks settle it:

  1. Open Settings and go to Time & language > Date & time (Time & Language on Windows 10). Turn on Set time automatically and Set time zone automatically, and confirm the time zone shown matches where the laptop actually is. A wrong time zone makes the clock look wrong even when internet sync is working perfectly.
  2. Check the Windows Time service, the component that synchronises the clock with internet time servers. In services.msc, find Windows Time: its startup type should be Automatic or Automatic (Trigger Start) and its status should be Running. If that service is disabled, the PC may never update its clock correctly, however healthy the battery is.

If the clock now holds across restarts while the laptop stays plugged in, the battery was never the problem. The distinguishing test for the battery is what happens with no power at all, which is the next step.

The confirmation test, laptop version

The CMOS battery. CMOS stands for complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor, the chip whose settings and clock it powers, only does its job when every other power source is gone. So the test removes them:

  1. Enter the BIOS or UEFI setup at startup, set the correct date, time, and year, then save and exit.
  2. Shut the laptop down. Unplug the charger and remove the main battery if it is removable.
  3. Leave the machine off and unpowered for 2 to 3 hours.
  4. Reconnect power, start the machine, and check the date and time before Windows can correct them from the internet.

What the result proves: a clock that survived unplugged is being kept alive correctly, and the fault sits in software. A date that has reset, classically to an old default such as 1 January 2009, at 00:00, is the signature of a CMOS battery that can no longer hold the clock. Boot messages point the same way: CMOS Read Error, CMOS Checksum Error, CMOS Battery Failure, and System battery voltage is low are all listed as signs the battery needs replacement.

Three forms the battery takes in a laptop

Most replacement guides are written for desktops and picture one part: a coin cell in a clip. Laptops use that form, but not only that form, and ordering before identifying which one your machine has is how the wrong battery ends up on the desk.

FormHow to recognise itWhat replaces it
Coin cell in a holderA round cell, most commonly a CR2032, held by a metal clip or bar on the boardThe same cell type, printed on the cell itself; slide the old cell out from under the clip without bending the clip
Wrapped two-wire packA small black disc wrapped in heat shrink, connected by a two-wire lead, on one documented model it sits just below the fanA pack with the same connector and part number, unplugged and swapped as a unit
No separate batteryThe model's hardware maintenance manual lists no CMOS or RTC battery at allNothing to swap: these boards take RTC power from the main battery, backed by capacitors with limited capacity, so a weak or removed main battery is the thing to fix

The third row is the one that matters most on recent machines. If the service manual lists no CMOS battery, stop looking for one. A laptop that loses time whenever its main battery is removed or run flat is behaving as designed, and the repair is the main battery, not a coin cell.

One more form deserves a warning rather than a row: a battery that is soldered in place, or buried under the motherboard, may require full disassembly or professional service. That is a stop-and-refer line, not a Saturday job.

Record your settings before you swap

Replacing the battery clears the settings it was keeping. Before the old cell comes out, enter setup and note anything non-default you rely on, boot order is the usual one, along with any drive or security settings you changed when the machine was set up. After the swap, those values are re-entered by hand and saved before exiting setup. Two minutes of notes now is what separates a battery swap from an afternoon of re-diagnosing a machine that suddenly boots in the wrong order.

While the battery is out, inspect its holder. A rarer cause of the same symptom is loose or broken contacts in the battery compartment: clips that no longer grip the cell can interrupt power even with a fresh battery fitted.

The swap, step by step

  1. Shut the laptop down, unplug it, and remove the main battery if it is removable.
  2. Open the bottom cover and locate the battery, using the model's manual if it is not visible, if the manual lists no CMOS battery, stop here and reread the third row of the table above.
  3. For a coin cell: ease the edge of the cell up and out of its socket with your fingers, without bending the retaining clip.
  4. For a wrapped pack: unplug its two-wire connector from the board; do not pull on the wires themselves.
  5. Fit the replacement of the same type, in the same orientation, and confirm it is seated, clipped firmly, or the connector fully home.
  6. Reassemble, reconnect power, and start the machine.

After the swap: set, save, verify

  1. Enter setup at the first boot, re-enter the recorded settings, set the correct date and time, and save before exiting.
  2. Boot into Windows and confirm automatic time is on, so the clock is corrected precisely.
  3. Repeat the confirmation test once: unplug, remove the main battery if it is removable, wait, and restart.

What success looks like: after hours with no power connected, the laptop starts with the correct date and time already showing, no CMOS error messages appear, and the settings recorded before the swap are still in place a week later.
