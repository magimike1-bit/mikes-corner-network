Replace a noisy or dead laptop fan: triage by sound, match the part, swap it safely

Cooling · 7 min read

A loud fan is not a diagnosis. A fan can be loud because it is working hard against clogged vents, loud because its bearings are worn out, or silent because it has stopped altogether, and each of those is a different job with a different bill. Ordering a replacement fan for a dust problem wastes money. Running software fixes on a fan that no longer spins wastes the laptop. Start with what the machine is telling you, in sound and in error messages, and let that decide the work.

Job summary. Difficulty: moderate, the swap itself is screws and one small connector, but access varies sharply by model, and some laptops bury the fan under the whole cooling assembly. Time: an afternoon, most of it spent on careful disassembly and reassembly rather than the fan itself. The hire-a-pro line is stated plainly in the verdict table below: a fan that does not spin is a stop-and-repair job, not a keep-using-it job.

Symptom one: a loud whir that rises and falls with the work

A whir that tracks what the laptop is doing, louder under load, quieter at idle, usually means the fan is working overtime to compensate for heat build-up. Clogged vents and fins are the common cause, and a failing fan is the other possibility the same symptom points to. This is the clean-first case: shut the machine down, clear the exterior vents, and check for obstructions before any part is ordered.

While the machine is open for cleaning, look at the fan itself. Dust packed around the blades and heatsink fins restricts the air the fan can move, so it spins faster to do the same job. Clean first, then retest under the same load that made it loud.

Verdict for this symptom: if the noise settles after cleaning, the fan was compensating, not failing, and no part is needed. If the whir stays loud with clean vents and fins, move to the next symptom's checks, the fan itself is now the suspect.

Symptom two: buzzing, grinding, or rattling

Periodic loud buzzing or grinding points somewhere else: bad or worn-out bearings. A bearing noise does not come and go with cleaning, because the fault is inside the fan's own moving parts. Broken blades sit in the same category, a fan with damaged blades is not practically serviceable.

The rule for this symptom is short. A grinding fan should be replaced rather than repaired. Lubricating or adjusting a worn bearing is not a documented fix in the service guidance this guide is built on, and a fan that has started grinding is announcing the end of its working life.

Verdict for this symptom: replace the fan. Skip the driver and power-settings detours, no software setting rebuilds a bearing.

Symptom three: silence, a boot error, or sudden shutdowns

A fan that does not spin at all points to a failed fan or a connection issue. Some machines say so directly at startup: HP's System Fan Error 90B, for example, indicates that the cooling fan is not operating correctly, with possible causes including a defective fan or loose connection, a fan not spinning properly, high internal temperature from obstructed airflow, and an outdated BIOS. Error codes vary by manufacturer and model, so record the exact wording your machine shows.

This symptom carries the safety line for the whole guide. Continued use without cooling can cause throttling, sudden shutdowns, or hardware damage, and HP's own guidance for the 90B error warns of unexpected shutdown, data loss, or system damage. Power the laptop off and keep it off until the fan spins again or is replaced.

  1. Note the exact error message and code, if one appears.
  2. Run the manufacturer's built-in hardware diagnostics, where offered. HP's route is HP PC Hardware Diagnostics (UEFI), which includes a fan test, and note whether the blades spin and whether the test reports an error.
  3. If the diagnostics clear the fan but the error persists, update the BIOS and drivers from the manufacturer's site, since an outdated BIOS is one of the listed causes of a false fan error.
  4. If the fan still does not spin, the fault is hardware: a failed fan or its connection. That is the replacement case below.

Verdict for this symptom: a confirmed non-spinning fan is replaced, not troubleshot further. Do not force fan-speed controls either, use only fan controls the manufacturer explicitly supports.

Match the part before you order it

The single most useful specification on a laptop fan is printed on the fan itself. Before ordering, open the machine (or find the model's service manual) and record these fields:

FieldWhere it is foundWhy it decides the order
Part numberLabel on the old fan; service manual for the exact modelThe match that guarantees fit; OEM fans are the recommended replacement
Fan only, or fan with heatsinkService manual's parts list for the modelSome models sell the fan only as part of the thermal assembly, which changes both price and the size of the job
ConnectorThe plug on the old fan's cableThe replacement must plug into the same motherboard header; a fan that cannot connect cannot run

Order against the part number, not against the laptop's marketing name. Two machines sold under one name can carry different cooling parts, and the label on the part in your hand outranks any listing title.

Tools and materials

  • The replacement fan or fan assembly, matched as above.
  • The model's service or disassembly guide, open before the first screw comes out.
  • Screwdrivers to match the case screws, and a container for screws removed in order.
  • Compressed air for the vents, fins, and fan cavity while the machine is open.

The swap, step by step

  1. Shut the laptop down fully, unplug it, and remove the battery where it is removable.
  2. Open the machine following the disassembly guide for your specific model, access routes differ too much between models for a generic order to be safe.
  3. Photograph the fan area before touching it: cable routing and screw positions are the two things reassembly gets wrong.
  4. Unplug the fan connector from the motherboard. Pull the plug, never the wires.
  5. Remove the mounting screws and lift the old fan out. Keep its screws separate if they differ from the case screws.
  6. Clear dust from the fan cavity, heatsink fins, and vents while access is open.
  7. Seat the replacement fan, refit its mounting screws, and reconnect the fan cable firmly.
  8. Before closing the case, check the cable run: wires must not be pinched by the cover or trapped against the heatsink during reassembly.
  9. Reassemble in reverse order, reconnect the battery and power, and boot the machine.

What success looks like: the fan spins up at boot, no fan error appears, the manufacturer's diagnostics fan test passes where offered, and under the load that used to trigger the noise or the error, the machine runs without grinding, without a boot warning, and without shutting down.

Common mistakes, and how each is recovered

Ordering by laptop name instead of part number. The recovery is the label check above, and if a wrong fan has already arrived, it goes back unopened rather than being adapted to fit.

Pinching the fan cable on reassembly. A cable trapped under the cover can leave a new fan unpowered, imitating the original fault. Recovery: reopen, reroute the cable along its photographed path, and retest before blaming the part.

Treating a dead fan as a software problem. Driver updates and power settings have their place, after diagnostics clear the hardware. On a fan that does not spin, they only delay the repair while the machine runs uncooled.
