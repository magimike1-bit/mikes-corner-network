Swollen laptop battery: how to spot it and what to do next

Safety & Maintenance · 7 min read

A bulging laptop case is often treated as a cosmetic problem — an old machine getting a little puffy with age, something to press flat and live with until a replacement battery is convenient. That reading is wrong in every particular. A swollen battery cannot be fixed, deflated, or repaired; the only option is removal and replacement. Pressing the case flat is on the list of things never to do to one. And the swelling does not pause while a decision is being made: the battery is physically expanding inside a case that was not built to give. This guide runs the situation in triage order — spot it, stop, then decide — with the safety steps before the money steps, because that is the order the manufacturers' own guidance uses.

Safety note: this article is general information about what official and repair guidance recommends, not instructions to open a laptop. Where the guidance conflicts, the conservative line wins: manufacturer guidance says do not remove a swollen battery yourself, and that advice stands wherever it applies.

The five signs, in the order you will notice them

Manufacturer guidance lists the physical signs of a swollen battery. They tend to arrive in roughly this order, and any one of them on its own is enough to treat the battery as swollen:

  1. The laptop wobbles on a flat surface. The base no longer sits level because the battery compartment is bulging underneath it.
  2. The trackpad or keyboard is raised, or stops clicking properly. The battery on many models sits directly under the trackpad, and its expansion pushes up first.
  3. Gaps or separations appear in the case. Seams that used to meet flush start to part, usually along the front edge or around the battery compartment.
  4. The battery compartment itself is visibly bulging. The bottom panel domes outward instead of lying flat.
  5. The screen no longer closes completely. The distortion through the chassis reaches the hinge line, and the lid meets resistance or a visible gap.

None of these signs needs a second opinion or a battery-health percentage to act on. Swelling is physical failure, and it outranks every capacity figure a diagnostic can report.

Right now: stop, disconnect, and leave it off

Dell's guidance for a swollen battery is a short sequence, and it comes before any decision about repair:

  1. Stop using the battery immediately.
  2. Turn the laptop off.
  3. Disconnect the AC adapter.
  4. Do not attempt to use the battery again.
  5. On modern designs where the battery is not user-removable, do not attempt to remove it yourself — that is the manufacturer's explicit instruction, and the next sections explain the routes that replace it.

Just as explicit is the list of things never to do to a swollen battery or the laptop containing it: never press the case flat, clamp it closed, puncture it, cut it, heat it, freeze it, or throw it in household garbage. If a battery has become stuck in the device because of the swelling, do not try to free it — puncturing, bending, or crushing a battery can be dangerous, and a stuck battery is a technician's problem by definition.

DIY removal: the honest line

Guidance splits here, and an honest guide has to say so. Dell's position is that the owner should not attempt removal and should contact support. Micro Center strongly recommends against removing a swollen battery yourself as well — but, recognising that some owners will attempt it, publishes the precautions that apply if it is attempted. For an in-support machine, the manufacturer's path settles the question. For a discontinued, out-of-warranty revival candidate with no support channel, the realistic options are a repair shop or the precautions below — and the shop remains the recommended default.

Where a removable-battery model is attempted by its owner, the published precautions are consistent: eye protection and gloves, work on a well-ventilated, non-flammable surface, never puncture the battery, and do not slide anything under it to pop it out. After removal, the battery goes alone into a sealed metal container, ideally surrounded by sand or dirt, until it reaches a recycler. A battery that resists, is stuck, or sits in a sealed chassis ends the attempt — that machine belongs to the second row of the table.

Can the laptop be saved?

The battery cannot — removal and replacement is the only option, and no procedure restores a swollen pack. The laptop is a separate question. Once the battery is out, the decision is a revival decision like any other: the machine's age and condition, whether a correct replacement battery exists for the model, and whether the laptop, with a new battery fitted, earns its place. Owners of removable-battery models also commonly ask whether the machine can simply run on AC power with the battery removed and stay in service that way. That depends on the model, and it is a question to settle with the manufacturer's documentation or the technician doing the removal — what is not an option, under any of the guidance, is leaving the swollen battery in place while the laptop keeps working.

Your files: getting data without gambling

The instinct, on realising a laptop may be out of service for a while, is to power it back on and copy the important files off first. Repair guidance addresses this directly: if the laptop contains important files, do not keep using it to back them up — and if the swelling is bad, powering the device may not be worth the risk. The safer route is to ask the technician doing the removal about safe data access: a drive can usually be read by other means once the battery situation is resolved, without running a swelling battery to do it. Files matter. They are not worth a powered-on swollen battery when an unpowered route to the same drive exists.

Disposal and transport: the acceptance trap

The last step is where vague advice does real harm, because a swollen battery is not accepted everywhere a normal battery is. The rules from the guidance:

  • Never household trash or recycling. A swollen battery goes to an authorised battery recycler — full stop.
  • Call first. Before travelling, verify that the recycler accepts swollen batteries specifically. Some collection points refuse damaged batteries, and discovering that at the counter, battery in hand, is the trap this section is named for.
  • Warranty returns travel in an approved shipping container, to comply with transportation regulations — a swollen battery is not something to put in ordinary mail. Out-of-warranty batteries go to an approved recycling centre.
  • Local rules vary. In Ontario and across Canada, disposal rules vary by municipality. The repair shop that removed the battery can often handle disposal afterwards, which closes the transport question entirely — one more reason the shop route is the default for out-of-support machines.

The whole sequence, compressed: notice any one of the five signs, stop and disconnect the same day, let the support status choose between manufacturer, shop, and — only for removable designs, with the precautions — the owner, get the files by an unpowered route, and hand the battery to a recycler who has confirmed, in advance, that swollen batteries are accepted. An old laptop can survive a swollen battery and return to service. What it cannot survive is being treated as a cosmetic problem until the case makes the decision instead.
