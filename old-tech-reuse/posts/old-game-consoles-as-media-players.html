Use a retired game console as a media player

Repurpose · 9 min read

Most advice about using an old game console as a media player was written when the idea worked exactly as advertised: plug in a PlayStation 3, an Xbox 360, or a Wii, open Netflix, and watch. That advice has quietly expired. Checked on October 13, 2026, the streaming picture for these seventh-generation consoles is a sequence of shutdowns — and yet the consoles are not useless as media machines. They have simply changed jobs. Discs still spin, and your own files on your own home network still play. The honest guide is a split one: here is what ended, dated, and here is what still works, with a way to verify it on the console already sitting under the TV before a single cable is bought.

The dates are the story. Nintendo suspended all video streaming services on the Wii after January 31, 2019, alongside the Wii Shop Channel closure, as Engadget reported at the time. Microsoft ended purchases from the Xbox 360 Store on July 29, 2024, and the Microsoft Movies & TV app stopped functioning on the console the same day, so movies bought there are no longer viewable on a 360. The last holdout was the PS3: GameSpot reported Netflix's announcement that the service would no longer be available on the PS3 after March 2, 2026. A console set up as a streaming box today is a console set up to disappoint.

Job one: playing discs still works

Nothing about the shutdowns touches the disc drive. MakeUseOf's media-centre comparison from the consoles' prime describes the hardware plainly: the PlayStation 3 was built with a Blu-ray drive and media streaming in mind, the Xbox 360 carried a DVD drive, and the Wii shipped without any real consideration for media playback. That hierarchy still holds. A PS3 connected to a TV is a completely serviceable Blu-ray and DVD player for a shelf of discs the household already owns — no account, no subscription, no app store required. An Xbox 360 does the same job for DVDs. The Wii does not do this job at all, and no setup changes that.

There is one niche where the PS3 arguably beats the newer boxes in the house. ScreenRant's shutdown coverage points out that the PS3 directly supports analog output and can plug into a CRT television, while the PS4 and PS5 need adapters to do the same. For older shows filmed in 4:3, a PS3 feeding a CRT the way both were designed to work is a genuinely good picture — the kind of setup newer consoles can only imitate through extra hardware.

Job two: your own files over the home network

The second surviving job is playing a personal library — home video, music, photos, and ripped discs stored on a computer — over the home network. This works because it never depended on a streaming company's app store. Polygon's reporting on the PS4 launch confirms the underlying feature: the PlayStation 3 includes DLNA support and lets its owner reach media stored on a PC from the console's dashboard, and DLNA was possible on the Xbox 360 as well, which shipped with native compatibility for data streamed from a Windows PC. A DLNA server program on the computer shares chosen folders; the console finds the server on the local network and plays what it offers. When PS3 owners discussed alternatives after the Netflix shutdown notice, PiunikaWeb recorded the suggestions that came up repeatedly: Universal Media Server, Plex, and Movian, all aimed at streaming personal libraries to the console.

What owners still use a PS3 for

Forum reports fill in the lived version of the spec sheet. In a ResetEra thread asking whether members used a PS3 as a media hub, posters recalled buying the console largely to watch Blu-ray movies, and using DLNA servers such as TVersity to stream from a PC in the years before Plex became the default answer. One poster reported still keeping a working PS3 partly for exactly this media function, praising how many different file types it handles. Treat those as owner reports rather than specifications — but they explain why the PS3, specifically, keeps a place under televisions long after its streaming apps have gone dark: the disc drive and the network player never needed anyone's permission to keep working.

The job that ended: streaming apps

It is worth being precise about what ended, because the consoles still turn on and the menus still look hopeful. What ended is the commercial streaming layer: the Wii's services in 2019, the Xbox 360's store and its Movies & TV app in 2024, and Netflix on the PS3 in March 2026 — the last major app on the last console of that generation still carrying it. Microsoft was explicit that previously purchased Xbox 360 games and DLC remain playable and can be re-downloaded, so a 360 keeps its gaming job; it is specifically the entertainment purchases and store that closed. Likewise, a PS3 keeps every job in the sections above. Only the app-grid vision of these consoles is over.

Set it up and verify it, step by step

  1. Connect the console to the TV it will actually serve and power it on. For a PS3, prefer the best connection the TV offers; for a CRT setup, the PS3's analog output is the feature, not a compromise.
  2. Put a known-good disc in the drive — a Blu-ray for a PS3, a DVD for a PS3 or Xbox 360 — and play ten minutes of it, checking picture and sound. This verifies the drive, the cable, and the TV input in one test.
  3. Connect the console to the home network, wired if it lives near the router, and confirm the connection in its network settings.
  4. On the household computer, install a DLNA media server, point it at one small test folder — a few photos, one music album, one short video — and let it finish indexing before judging the result.
  5. On the PS3, look in the Photo, Music, or Video sections of the dashboard for the media server's name and open the test folder. On an Xbox 360, use its media playback menus to look for the connected Windows PC as a source.
  6. Play one item of each type. If photos and music appear but a video refuses, the likely problem is that file's format, not the setup — change one variable at a time, starting with a different test file.
  7. Only after both jobs pass, tidy the installation: label the input on the TV, leave the console's controller where guests can find it, and write the two-step path to the disc and the media server on a card if other people will use it.

What success looks like

Success is a guest-room or den setup that does two things without help: a disc from the shelf plays with sound within a minute of sitting down, and the household's own photos and music appear from the home network without error messages about stores, accounts, or unsupported services. If the console can only manage one of the two, that is still a real job — a disc-only PS3 in a room with a disc shelf is a finished project, not a failed streamer.

The decision: keep, repurpose, or retire

PlayStation 3: keep as a media player. It is the only one of the three that holds both surviving jobs — Blu-ray and DVD playback plus DLNA network playback — and it owns the CRT niche outright.

Xbox 360: keep for discs and games, not for streaming. DVD playback and a previously downloaded app may still work, and owned games remain playable, but the store and Movies & TV shutdown means it should never be the plan for a streaming room.

Nintendo Wii: retire it as a media player. Its streaming services ended in 2019 and movie-disc playback was never part of its design. It remains a games console for the games already in the house; as a media project, it is the one console in this guide whose honest answer is no.

Whichever verdict a console gets, keep its vents clear, run it in the open rather than in a sealed cabinet, and unplug it before moving or re-cabling anything.

FAQ

Can a PS3 still play Netflix in 2026? No. Netflix announced the PS3 app would no longer be available after March 2, 2026. The console's disc and DLNA jobs are unaffected.

Is a Wii worth setting up for video? No. Nintendo suspended all Wii streaming services after January 31, 2019, and the Wii was never designed for movie-disc playback.

The Xbox 360 store is closed — is the console dead? No. Microsoft confirmed previously purchased games and DLC stay playable and re-downloadable. What closed is buying new content and the Movies & TV app on the console.
