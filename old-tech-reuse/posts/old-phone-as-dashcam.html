Turn an old phone into a dashcam: the honest setup, heat limits and storage math included

Repurpose · 7 min read

A dashcam is a camera, some storage, a GPS receiver and a power source in a small box — and a retired phone already contains all four, usually with a better screen than the dashcam it would replace. That is why the phone-in-a-mount conversion is so popular, and the basic version really is simple: install a dashcam app, mount the phone, keep it plugged in. What the quick versions of this project skip is everything that decides whether the conversion is still working in three months: the temperature limits the phone's own maker publishes, the battery wear from recording for hours at a time, and the storage arithmetic that determines how much of your driving the loop actually keeps. This guide starts with the decision, states the limits honestly, does the math, and then gives one ordered setup procedure with a playback check at the end.

First, the decision: phone dashcam or dedicated dashcam?

Run the candidate phone through five questions before spending anything on a mount. A "no" on the first two ends the project; the rest tell you what kind of dashcam you are getting.

  • Is the battery sound? Batteries are consumables that wear out over time, and continuous recording is hard use — the Droid Dashcam app's own FAQ warns that battery usage is high, the battery drains fast and the device becomes hot. A phone whose battery is swollen, whose case is lifting, or that shuts down unexpectedly is a recycling candidate, not a dashcam.
  • Can it record a readable plate at night? Camera quality is the whole product. A very old phone's camera may not deliver usable video, and quality drops further at night. If a test clip (step 9 below) cannot resolve the plate of a car stopped directly ahead in daylight, the phone fails the only test that matters.
  • Is it Android, or are you prepared to shop the App Store carefully? The best-documented app for this job, Droid Dashcam, is an Android app. On an iPhone the same checklist applies — loop recording, GPS overlay, autostart — but the app names differ, so verify each feature in the listing before relying on it.
  • Will it live in the car, or travel with you? A phone left mounted is more visible to thieves than a small dedicated unit, and a parked car is exactly where the heat problem below bites. The workable pattern for most people is drive-time only: mount it when you drive, take it out when you park for long.
  • Do you need parking surveillance? If the footage you care about is what happens while the car is parked and unattended, a phone is the wrong tool — it will be out of the car, out of battery, or shut down by heat. Buy the dedicated unit instead and let the phone do a different second job.

The heat limit nobody mentions

This is the section most dashcam conversions need and few include. Apple states that iPhone is designed for ambient temperatures between 0 and 35 °C, can be stored between −20 and 45 °C, and says plainly: do not leave the device in a car, because temperatures in parked cars can exceed the storage range. Samsung Canada publishes the same 0 to 35 °C optimal range for Galaxy devices, warns that extended use or storage above 35 °C leads to unexpected shutdowns and shorter battery life, and gives the same hot-car warning. Apple adds the mechanisms: using the camera for an extended period, or GPS tracking in a car, in hot conditions or direct sunlight is on its list of activities to avoid; an overheated phone slows or stops charging, dims or blacks out its display, disables some camera features, and in extreme conditions temporarily disables itself. Hot use can permanently shorten battery life, and Samsung notes continuous use or charging in extreme temperatures accelerates battery deterioration.

Translated into dashcam rules: the phone records while you drive, with the car's ventilation running — and it leaves the car with you whenever the car will sit in summer sun. A phone that shows a temperature warning, stops charging, or goes dark mid-drive is not malfunctioning; it is enforcing the limit its maker published. If your driving life means the phone would bake on the windshield daily, that is a decision-framework answer, not a setup problem: choose the dedicated unit.

Do the storage math before choosing settings

Loop recording deletes the oldest clips when the space cap is reached, so the cap and the recording bitrate together decide how much driving you keep. The conversion is fixed: gigabytes per hour ≈ bitrate in Mbps × 0.45. Worked examples for a 32 GB cap — the size suggested in XDA's account of this setup:

Recording bitrate (example input)Storage per hourDriving kept in a 32 GB loop
5 Mbpsabout 2.25 GBabout 14 hours
10 Mbpsabout 4.5 GBabout 7 hours
15 Mbpsabout 6.75 GBabout 4.7 hours

Read your app's actual bitrate from a test clip's file properties and redo the one multiplication with your number. The practical reading: at a moderate bitrate, a one-hour daily commute is retained for roughly two weeks before the loop overwrites it — ample for incident footage, provided any clip that matters is copied off the phone promptly, because the loop will not keep it forever. Short segments make that copying practical: with 5-minute segments, an incident is one timestamped file, not a position inside an hour-long recording.

The setup procedure

  1. Prep the phone as a single-purpose device. Back up anything worth keeping, charge it fully, install the newest system update it supports, and remove apps the dashcam job does not need — every removal frees storage for the loop. Sign out of banking and shopping accounts; this device will live in a car.
  2. Choose where recordings live. Droid Dashcam can record to the phone's storage or to an external SD card where the phone has a slot. Set the maximum space for all videos to the cap your storage math produced (32 GB is a sensible starting point on a 64 GB phone).
  3. Install the app and grant its permissions. Install Droid Dashcam from Google Play on an Android phone. On first launch it asks for permissions including camera, location and notifications — grant them, because the GPS overlay is the point of the exercise. On an iPhone, install the App Store dashcam app chosen in the decision step and grant the equivalent permissions.
  4. Turn on the evidence overlay. In Settings, enable the hardcoded subtitles: timestamp, GPS coordinates, GPS-based speed, and your vehicle registration plate. Burned into the file, they survive sharing and uploading in a way a separate log file does not.
  5. Set segments, day/night and impact locking. Set the segment length to 5 minutes, leave the automatic day/night mode switch on, and enable the G-shock lock so a detected impact protects that clip from the loop's overwriting.
  6. Set an autostart trigger. Configure recording to start on the trigger that matches your car: on charging, if the car's USB port wakes with the ignition, or on Bluetooth connection, if the phone pairs with the car. Recording that starts by itself is recording that is never forgotten.
  7. Mount it high, central and unobstructed. Fit the mount so the phone sits in landscape orientation with a clear view of the road — typically high on the windshield near the rear-view mirror — with nothing covering the camera lens and the mount itself outside the camera's field of view. Keep the phone out of any airbag deployment zone, and out of your own line of sight as far as the view allows: a phone is bigger and bulkier than a dashcam and intrudes more.
  8. Wire the power for every trip. This is the project the saved-cable drawer was made for — charging leads and power cords from earlier devices have not changed in years, and a sound saved cable is the right cable here. Run it from the car's USB port or charger to the mount, routed so it cannot dangle across controls or fall into the footwell, and leave it in the car permanently. Constant recording drains a battery quickly; an unplugged dashcam phone is a dead dashcam phone.
  9. Take the test drive before trusting it. Drive ten minutes in daylight including a stop behind another vehicle, then a short stretch after dark if you drive at night. Play the clips back on the phone itself — which is, not coincidentally, the conversion's best feature.

What success looks like: the test clips play back with the correct timestamp, GPS coordinates and speed visible on the video; the plate of the vehicle stopped directly ahead is readable in the daylight clip; recording restarted by itself on the second trip with no tap from you; and after a 30-minute drive the phone shows no temperature warning and is still charging. If the plate is not readable, no setting fixes it — that phone's camera has failed the decision test, and the honest outcome is a different second job for the phone.

Living with it

Three habits keep the conversion honest. First, treat the loop as short-term memory: copy any clip that matters to a computer or cloud storage over home Wi-Fi the same day, because loop recording exists to overwrite. Second, do everything with the phone before moving off — settings, playback, uploads. Rules on phone use while driving vary by jurisdiction, but the pattern is consistent: the phone must be mounted, and holding or interacting with it while driving is prohibited in most places. Check your local rules, then set the phone up parked, every time. Third, re-check the mount after big temperature swings and glance at the phone's battery health seasonally; a battery that begins shutting the phone down unexpectedly has reached the end of this job too.

FAQ

Does the phone need a SIM card or a data plan? No. The phone records without one, and recordings upload later over Wi-Fi. GPS positioning for the overlay does not require a mobile plan either — but without one, obviously, the phone cannot upload or call from the car.

Can it watch the car while parked? Not in any way worth relying on. Parked surveillance needs the phone left in the car, powered and recording — precisely the hot-parked-car situation both major makers warn against, with a battery that continuous recording drains fast. That gap is the dedicated dashcam's reason to exist.

Which resolution should be chosen? The highest the phone sustains without overheating or exhausting the loop too quickly — then verify with the storage math and the test-drive playback. A readable plate at a moderate bitrate beats an unreadable one at a spectacular bitrate the loop erases by Thursday.
