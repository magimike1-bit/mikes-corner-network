Give an old router a second job extending Wi-Fi

Repurpose · 6 min read

When you upgrade your home Wi-Fi, the old router doesn't stop working — it just stops being your main router. That old box is still a perfectly good Wi-Fi access point, and with about twenty minutes of configuration it can wipe out a dead spot in the basement, the garage, or the far bedroom. Here's how to do it properly.

The right job: wired access point

The best use for an old router is as a wired access point: you run an Ethernet cable (or use powerline or a MoCA adapter) from your main router to the old router in the dead zone, and the old router broadcasts Wi-Fi there at full speed. This works great because the old router isn't doing any of the hard jobs — your main router still handles the internet connection, device addresses, and routing. The old router just shouts Wi-Fi into a room it couldn't reach before.

Before you start: reset and update it

Dig the old router out, find its power supply (using the wrong adapter is how routers die — check the voltage and plug match the label on the router), and do a factory reset with the little button on the back. Hold it for ten seconds or so with a paperclip while it's powered on. Then, before anything else, update its firmware to the latest version the manufacturer offers. An old router with old firmware is a security liability; a current one is a fine piece of network gear.

Step 1: Give it the same network identity

Connect a laptop to the old router with a cable (not Wi-Fi yet — that gets confusing), open its admin page in a browser, and change three things. First, set its Wi-Fi network name (SSID) and password to exactly match your main network — same name, same password, same security type. That way your devices roam between the two access points without you managing two networks. Second, change the old router's admin password to something unique. Third, turn off its DHCP server: your main router must be the only device handing out addresses, or devices will randomly get broken network settings. Give the old router a static IP address on your network (something outside your main router's automatic range) so you can reach its admin page later.

Step 2: Pick the right channel

Two routers shouting on the same Wi-Fi channel step on each other's toes. Set the old router to a different channel than your main router — on the 2.4 GHz band, channels 1, 6, and 11 are the ones that don't overlap. If your main router picks its channel automatically, you may occasionally want to check and adjust; a free Wi-Fi analyzer app on your phone shows you what's crowded around you.

Step 3: Plug it in and place it

Connect the cable from your main router's LAN port to a LAN port on the old router (not the WAN/Internet port — that's the single most common mistake, and it creates a second, broken network). Place the old router in the dead zone but within cable reach: high up and central beats low and behind furniture. Plug it in, give it a minute, and check from your phone that you're on Wi-Fi with a strong signal.

What about wireless repeating?

Some old routers can also act as wireless repeaters — receiving your Wi-Fi and re-broadcasting it with no cable. It's tempting, but treat it as a last resort: it halves your available speed on that leg and adds lag, because the router spends half its time listening and half talking. If you can't run a cable, it's better than nothing for browsing and email, but don't expect it to fix video calls or gaming.

Know when to let go

Not every old router deserves a second job. If the manufacturer stopped firmware updates years ago, retire it — unpatched routers are one of the easiest targets on a home network. Same if it only supports ancient, slow wireless standards: a router from the flip-phone era will bottleneck everything that connects through it. Those belong in e-waste recycling, which our last guide covers.
