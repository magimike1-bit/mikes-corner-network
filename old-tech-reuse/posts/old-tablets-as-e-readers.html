Turn an old tablet into a dedicated e-reader: what it does well, where a real e-reader still wins

Repurpose · 9 min read

Every comparison of tablets and e-readers ends with the same advice: buy the e-reader. That advice assumes a purchase. A different question gets a different answer: an old tablet is already owned, likely already forgotten in a drawer, and its only competition is the recycling bin. Converted properly — stripped of distractions, loaded with reading and library apps, parked in one charging spot with a reading app as its entire personality — a retired tablet becomes a dedicated reader that costs nothing and reads well. Converted badly, it stays what it was: a general machine with a books app buried under notifications, which is why nobody actually reads on it. The conversion, not the hardware, is the project. This article is honest about the comparison first, because a dedicated e-reader wins most of it, and then gives the conversion anyway — because "good enough, free, and actually used" beats "technically superior and never bought".

The honest verdict first

Start where the e-reader wins, because pretending otherwise insults the reader. The two screens are fundamentally different technologies: a tablet uses a backlit LCD or OLED screen, while an e-reader uses reflective E Ink, which draws significant power only when the page changes. Everything else flows from that. Battery is the most lopsided score in the entire comparison. A tablet runs about 8 to 12 hours of active use; an e-reader runs for weeks to months. Put one concrete benchmark on that range: the Kindle Paperwhite class offers up to 12 weeks of battery at 30 minutes of reading a day, with wireless off and the light set at 13, per Amazon — and its screen is a 7-inch E Ink Carta 1300 panel at 300 ppi that recharges in under 2.5 hours with a 9W adapter. Weight is the second clear loss: a 6-inch e-reader typically weighs roughly 150 to 180 grams, against about 295 grams for an iPad Mini class tablet and about 480 grams for a standard iPad. Two hours into a long novel, that difference is not a specification; it is the whole experience. Light in dim rooms belongs to the e-reader too, where front-lit designs exist for exactly that job, and warm-light features such as Kobo's ComfortLight PRO and Kindle Warm Light are part of the dedicated-reader offer. On battery, weight, and screen comfort, the tablet loses, clearly and permanently. Any conversion that starts by denying that ends in disappointment.

Read that table as a job description rather than a contest. The e-reader is the better pure reading device. The converted tablet is the better use of a device already in the house — and for colour work, library variety, and anything illustrated, it is not even a consolation prize. It is the right tool.

Where the tablet genuinely does well

Three strengths survive the honest verdict. First, colour: a colour LCD or OLED panel shows comics, cookbooks, magazines, children's picture books, and illustrated non-fiction the way they were made, where a monochrome E Ink panel cannot. For households whose reading is heavily illustrated, the converted tablet is not the second-best reader in the house — it is the only suitable one. Second, flexibility of source: a general tablet accepts reading apps from multiple stores and library services on one device, so a reader is not tied to a single bookshop's shelves. Library borrowing through OverDrive and Libby is called out as a Kobo advantage in the comparisons — the same services, reached through apps, are part of what a converted tablet should be set up to do in the procedure below. Third, the economics of the drawer: 8 to 12 hours of active reading between charges is a genuine limitation for travel, but it is an entirely manageable one for a reader that lives beside a chair, a bed, or a kitchen counter with its charger parked beside it. The battery weakness is real, and a fixed charging spot disarms most of it.

The conversion procedure

  1. Reset the tablet to a clean start. Back up anything still wanted from it, then factory-reset it. A dedicated reader begins as an empty device, not as an old machine with a reading app added to the clutter.
  2. Sign in with only what reading needs. Add the accounts the reading and library apps require, and skip the rest. Every account not added is a stream of notifications that will never arrive — which is the design goal.
  3. Install reading apps, and only reading apps. The e-book store app or apps the household actually uses, plus the library app for borrowing — OverDrive and Libby are the services named in the comparisons. Add a PDF reader if illustrated documents are part of the reading pile. Nothing else gets installed at this stage.
  4. Remove the distractions. Delete or disable every remaining non-reading app the reset left behind, turn off notifications globally except for the reading apps, and take email, social, and news off the device entirely. A single-purpose reader is defined by what it refuses to do.
  5. Build a one-screen home. Arrange the home screen so the reading and library apps are the whole screen, with the current book one tap away. Remove widgets, feeds, and anything that scrolls for attention.
  6. Set the comfort defaults. In the reading app, enable the warm or night display mode the app or tablet offers for evening reading, raise the font size and line spacing to genuinely comfortable levels rather than default ones, and set brightness for the room the tablet will live in. Then leave those settings alone — a dedicated reader should open mid-page, not mid-settings-menu.
  7. Give it a home and a charger. Choose the reading spot — chair side, bedside, kitchen — and park the charger there permanently. With 8 to 12 hours of active use per charge, the tablet does not need better battery habits; it needs a place where charging is effortless and automatic.
  8. Load the first three books. Borrow or download the first titles, including at least one library loan, before declaring the conversion done. A reader with no book in progress is a project; a reader mid-chapter is a habit.

What success looks like

Success is behavioural, and it is checkable within a week. The tablet lives in its one spot, on or near its charger, and opens directly into a book. No notifications arrive, because there is nothing installed that sends them. Borrowed library titles appear through the library app and reading happens in the evening comfort settings without adjustment. Most tellingly, the tablet gets picked up for reading instead of a phone — the conversion worked when the device's job is so clear that reaching for it and reaching for a book are the same motion. If the tablet drifts back to general use, the fix is rarely a new device: it is step 4 again, distractions removed a second time, because single-purpose is a state that has to be defended, not achieved once.

FAQ

Is a converted tablet as good as a Kindle? No, and this article does not claim otherwise. On battery life, weight, and screen comfort, a dedicated e-reader wins clearly: weeks to months per charge against 8 to 12 hours, and roughly 150 to 180 grams for a 6-inch reader against tablet weights well above that. The converted tablet's case is different: free, already owned, in colour, and good enough to actually be used.

What reading is the tablet actually better for? Colour work: comics, magazines, cookbooks, picture books, and illustrated or PDF-heavy reading. A backlit colour LCD or OLED panel presents that material as intended, and that row of the table belongs to the tablet outright.

How is the battery problem handled day to day? By location, not willpower. At 8 to 12 hours of active use, a reading session will not strand the tablet — but a week on a coffee table might. A permanent charger at the reading spot makes charging part of putting the book down.

Should a dedicated e-reader still be bought later? For a heavy plain-text novel reader, the sourced numbers make the case on their own — battery measured in weeks, a far lighter device, warm-light features such as Kobo ComfortLight PRO and Kindle Warm Light. The converted tablet is how a household finds out whether that reading habit exists before spending anything at all.
