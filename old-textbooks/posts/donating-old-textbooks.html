Donating Old Textbooks: Where They Actually Go, and the Books Nobody Can Take

The exit chapter · 7 min read

Donating a textbook feels like handing it to a grateful student. The reality, documented by the organisations that take the books, is more interesting and more honest: most donated textbooks are resold to fund literacy work, placed on free shelves for whoever passes, or shipped to schools abroad — and only rarely shelved in a library collection. None of that makes donation a lesser exit. It makes it a logistics decision, and logistics decisions go better with the actual rules on the table.

What actually happens to a donated textbook

Trent University Library publishes its routing in plain language, and it is the clearest window into the whole system. Used textbooks dropped in the designated bin at the entrance to Bata Library are either routed to Better World Books — a social enterprise that funds literacy initiatives worldwide — or placed on the library's Free Shelf, where anyone in the Trent community can simply take them. Occasionally, at the discretion of the Scholarly Resources Librarian, a donated textbook is added to the library's collection. Read that again with fresh eyes: the shelved-in-the-collection outcome is the exception, labelled as occasional and discretionary. The standard outcomes are resale-for-a-cause and free-to-a-good-home. Bookroo's donation guide confirms the resale model at Better World Books directly: donated books are resold, and the proceeds fund global literacy initiatives. The book's value is converted to money for literacy work rather than preserved as a book — which, for a superseded edition nobody will adopt again, is arguably the most useful thing it can still do.

The comparison carries one lesson: every channel is a filter, not a bin. Each has an age, condition, or format rule, and the rules are not cruelty — a charity that accepts unsellable books pays to dispose of them, which spends donation money on landfill fees. Sorting before the trip is part of the donation.

The triage: three piles before any driving

Pile one — current and clean. Recent editions in good condition have the most routes open: campus bins and free shelves, Textbooks for Change, Books for Africa (inside its 15-year window), and library Friends sales all take them. If the book also carries a live buyback quote, the honest fork in the road is sell-versus-give — the companion chapter on selling runs the numbers side; this chapter simply notes that a book worth money to a student is also the book charities can do the most with.

Pile two — older but sound. Outside the 15-year window or a superseded edition, but intact and readable: free shelves, little free libraries, and thrift channels are the realistic homes. Libraries are the weakest bet here — Denver Public Library's published list accepts modern textbooks, which is a polite word for a cutoff — and calling ahead before loading the car is the documented etiquette everywhere: confirm the branch is accepting, bring the boxes to the desk, and never use the book return drop, which Denver calls out explicitly.

Pile three — damaged. Broken spines, missing pages, water damage, heavy marking: no channel on record accepts them, and Denver's refusal of broken or damaged items is typical rather than harsh. The honest exit for pile three is paper recycling where local rules allow (hard covers usually need removing first, per municipal guidance — check yours), or the creative chapters of this site, which have never once asked a textbook to be readable.

The procedure, such as it is

  1. Sort into the three piles by age and condition, using the channel rules above — not by guilt.
  2. Match pile one to a channel and check its stated rules (age window, drop location, box limits) on the channel's own page.
  3. Call or check ahead for libraries and Friends groups; donation intake pauses happen and are posted.
  4. Deliver to a person or a designated bin — never a book return, never a doorstep after hours.
  5. Recycle pile three without ceremony. A book that cannot be read, sold, or shelved has one dignified exit left, and it is fibre recovery.

What success looks like: every book leaves in a load its channel actually accepts, nothing is refused at a desk, and nothing boomerangs home to resume its career as a shelf ornament. The donor's reward is an empty shelf and a clear conscience, in that order.

Donation as a calendar event, not a purge

The smoothest version of donating is boringly scheduled. Textbooks accumulate at predictable moments — the end of each term, the end of a degree, the shelf-clearing that follows both — and the channels in this chapter are at their most receptive at exactly those moments, because campus bins, free shelves, and redistribution programs are built around the academic calendar's rhythm. A household that sorts its pile in the week the term ends donates into open doors; the same pile discovered three years later donates into the pile-two and pile-three rules, because editions age whether or not anyone opens them. There is also a courtesy dimension the channels' own rules hint at: a sorted, honestly-graded donation, delivered inside the stated limits, costs the receiving organisation nothing to process, while an unsorted carload transfers the donor's sorting problem onto volunteers. The triage in this chapter is therefore not fussiness. It is the difference between giving books and giving work — and only one of those is a donation.

A last word for the sentimental case. Some textbooks carry a course, a teacher, or a year of a life that mattered, and the triage table has no row for that, correctly. Keep that one book, on purpose, with its name spoken aloud. A kept book is a memento; forty kept books are a storage problem wearing sentiment as a disguise. The shelf that results — one deliberate keeper, and space where the rest used to lean — is the most honest outcome this chapter can offer.

FAQ

Will the library put my textbook in its collection? Occasionally, at a librarian's discretion — Trent says so in nearly those words. Expect resale or a free shelf; treat collection placement as a pleasant surprise, not the plan.

Is shipping books to Books for Africa worth it? It is a donation with a shipping bill attached: up to 50 lbs, about $0.50 per book plus shipping, for books no older than 15 years. For a heavy box of current textbooks it converts freight money into books in students' hands; for three old paperbacks, a local free shelf achieves the same end without the postage.

What about textbooks with writing in them? Light notes rarely block the free-shelf and thrift routes; heavy marking closes most doors, and it closes buyback doors too. When in doubt, the free shelf is the forgiving channel — the next reader can judge the margins for themselves.
