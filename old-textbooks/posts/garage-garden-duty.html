Garage and Garden Duty

Outdoor service · 6 min read

Every old textbook eventually reaches the same career stage: the shelf is full, the guilt is fading, and the garage is beckoning. This is not a demotion. The garage is where the book finally gets used — genuinely, physically, unglamorously used. The shelf years were the internship. This is the real job.

Doorstop duty (the garage door edition)

The side door of the garage, the one that slams in the wind while you're carrying groceries, meets its match: a thick textbook, wedged with intent. The book is too heavy to blow over and too boring to steal. Note: this is the one role where rain is a risk. A soaked textbook becomes papier-mâché, which is a demotion even by our generous standards. Keep it under the eave.

The wheel chock

On a genuinely flat driveway, a textbook wedged behind a tire will keep a parked car politely in place while you work on it. On any slope whatsoever, skip this and buy an actual wheel chock — they're cheap, and gravity has a perfect record against paper products. The book's job here is "assist," not "miracle."

The kneeling pad

Garden weeding, driveway repairs, crawling under the deck to find the thing the dog buried: all of these are knee-destroying tasks, and a thin textbook is a surprisingly decent cushion. It's exactly the right size, it doesn't slide much, and — best of all — you will never feel bad about getting it dirty. A $200 textbook turned into a kneeling pad is the book's way of finally paying you back.

Plant risers

Seedlings need light, and windowsills need height. Short textbooks under pots lift leaves into the sun like tiny library pedestals. Arrange them by height for a tiered effect that makes your windowsill look curated instead of chaotic. Water carefully — plant risers are the one outdoor job where the book actually gets seen, and a swollen, warped textbook says less "curated garden" and more "recent flood."

Cold-weather helpers

A textbook under each front tire can provide just enough traction texture to coax a car out of a shallow icy rut. Sometimes. Maybe. Once. We're not promising anything — ice laughs at paper. But on the morning you're late and the car is stuck and the book is right there, you will try it, and you'll feel a brief, shining kinship with everyone who has ever improvised with whatever was in the trunk. Keep a real traction aid in the trunk too. The book is the backup plan; the traction aid is the plan.

The parts label press

Disassembled something and need to keep small parts from rolling away? Lay an open textbook flat and use the pages as a parts tray. The text gives you a grid-like background, and a dropped screw shows up clearly against the words. Organize parts by chapter if you want to feel organized. Future you, reassembling things, will thank past you and never once wonder why the screws are sitting on "Chapter 7: Fluid Dynamics."

The oil-drip sentinel

Slip an open, face-down textbook under the engine bay and it becomes an oil-drip detector: spots show clearly against the pages, and you'll know exactly how bad the leak is by how many chapters it covers. A one-chapter leak is a watch-it. A three-chapter leak is a mechanic. A whole-book leak is how you learn the word "catastrophic." Remove and recycle the pages when the job is done — oil-soaked paper is not recyclable, but knowing your car's condition is priceless.

The farewell

Garage duty is the textbook's last honest chapter — after this, the honest move is to let them go (see our one sincere article). But while they serve: doorstops, chocks, cushions, risers. They hold things, lift things, and cushion things. For a book that once claimed it would change your mind, this is arguably a more successful record: every garage assignment completed, no exams, no exceptions.
