Scanning Your Old Textbooks and Notes: What Is Actually Allowed

The sincere shelf · 8 min read

Ask the internet whether it is legal to scan a textbook you own and the internet will argue with itself. One Q&A answer calls personal scanning "entirely permissible." Lawyer answers elsewhere call scanning a purchased book infringement. A third calls it a case-by-case question. They cannot all be the whole story, and the honest answer is less satisfying than any of them: there is no scanning-specific rule, the law runs on balancing tests, and where a scan lands depends on what is being scanned and what happens to the file afterward. This article sorts it into piles, using university copyright guidance rather than comment-section confidence — and then covers the part the legal answers never mention: how to scan a thick textbook without destroying it.

Disclaimer: This is general information about how copyright frameworks are described by university libraries, not legal advice, and it is not a verdict on any specific scan. Copyright law differs by country, and anyone facing a real dispute or a commercial project should consult a lawyer in their own jurisdiction — which is also what the official guidance itself advises when there is doubt.

First: scanning has no special rules

Simon Fraser University's copyright FAQ answers the scanning question directly: there are no special rules for scanning. Scanning is treated like any other method of copying, so a scan is allowed on the same terms as a photocopy — where an exception in the Copyright Act applies, such as fair dealing, or where no permission is needed because the work is in the public domain. Outside those cases, the copyright owner's permission is the route. Two corollaries from the same guidance do heavy lifting. Owning a copy of a book does not mean owning the copyright in it — the purchase buys the object, not the rights. And being out of print does not mean out of copyright.

The three piles inside one textbook

A textbook on a scanner bed is not one legal thing; it is three. Pile one: your own work. Copyright guidance notes that the creator of a work is usually the first owner of its copyright. The handwritten notes, margin arguments, and highlighted battle plans in a used textbook were created by their writer — scanning one's own notes is copying one's own work. Pile two: facts and data. Copyright does not protect factual information or data; it protects the expression of facts. A formula, a date, a measurement is not owned the way the paragraph explaining it is. Pile three: the publisher's expression. The text, diagrams, and layout of the book itself — this is the copyrighted work, and everything cautious in this article is about pile three.

Why there is no magic percentage

In the United States, fair use under Section 107 weighs four factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and the effect on the potential market for the work. The US Copyright Office's own guidance stresses that the line between fair use and infringement is not easily defined, that there is no specific number of words or amount that may safely be taken without permission, and that crediting the source does not substitute for permission. Lawyer answers arguing against whole-book scans lean on the same factors: copying all or substantially all of a work weighs against the use, and a copy that replaces buying the original weighs against it again under market effect. The first sale doctrine, those answers note, covers selling the physical book you bought — it is not a scanning license.

Canada runs a different test with a similar shape. SFU's guidance is explicit that the US fair use exception is not the equivalent of Canadian fair dealing, and that US information has no jurisdiction in Canada. Canadian readers sorting a scan should be reading Canadian guidance; American readers, American guidance; and nobody should be reading a Q&A site's certainty as either.

How to scan a thick textbook without wrecking it

Assume the scan is sorted. The physical problem remains: textbooks are the worst scanning subjects in the house — thick, tightly bound, and heavy. Digitization guidance from TownsWeb Archiving explains the core issue: even moderately tight binding causes curvature around the gutter, distorting the text nearest the spine, and the curvature is worse in thicker volumes and can defeat OCR. The method below keeps the book — and the text layer — intact.

Step 1: Choose the non-destructive route. Method comparisons rank a flatbed scanner as gentle on spines and best for delicate books, and an overhead scanner as the fit for bound volumes that should not be unbound. A document feeder is fast but requires unbinding — cutting the spine off a textbook to feed it is a decision that cannot be un-cut, and it converts the book into a stack of loose leaves permanently.

Step 2: Support, never force. Rest the book in a cradle or on a cushion so the cover opens at a comfortable angle, and use a low-pressure glass sheet or a gentle hand at the fore-edge to flatten the page being scanned. Archival guidance is clear that cradles and low-pressure glass flatten pages without stressing the book, and that the spine is the most fragile part. A textbook forced flat on a copier platen is a binding repair waiting to happen.

Step 3: Mind the gutter. If the inner margin comes out shadowed or curved, rotate the book so the light runs along the gutter instead of across it — the archival fix for gutter shadow. Check the first few scans at full zoom; gutter problems found on page 4 are a settings fix, and found on page 340 are a tragedy.

Step 4: Set resolution and OCR. Scan at 300 to 600 DPI, and turn on the scanner's OCR or "searchable PDF" setting, which produces a searchable, higher-quality PDF with a text layer. One honest caution from OCR guidance: handwriting on the page — underlining, highlighting, margin notes — degrades OCR. Pile-one material scans fine as an image; expect its text layer to be approximate.

Step 5: Name, tag, back up. Save the file with the book's title, author, ISBN, and publication date in the metadata or filename, and keep a second copy in a different place. A scan that cannot be found or identified in three years was shelf space saved and information lost.

What success looks like

Success has three checks. The file opens and a search for a known term from a middle chapter lands on the right page — the OCR worked. The book closes flat afterward with its binding unstressed and no new gutter cracks — the method worked. And the file lives in exactly the places the framework says it should: with the scanner's owner, unshared — the part that makes the whole project defensible worked.

Honesty corner

The honest summary is uncomfortable in both directions. "I bought the book" settles ownership of the paper, not the rights, and no percentage, page count, or credit line converts a scan into a safe one — the Copyright Office says so itself. At the same time, the guidance is equally clear that exceptions for private study and research are real, that facts are not owned, and that one's own notes are one's own. The genuinely dangerous move is the one that feels most generous: passing the file along. Distribution is the right holder's right, and even access given on request is the step legal answers single out as riskier than the scanning itself. Scan like a librarian, share like the file is radioactive, and the textbook can go back to its day job holding up a monitor.

FAQ

Can a copy shop or scanning service do it instead? Online answers conflict and no authoritative source settles it, so this article does not pretend to. It is exactly the kind of specific situation the official guidance says to take to a lawyer.

Does crediting the author make a shared scan fine? No. The Copyright Office guidance is explicit that acknowledging the source does not substitute for obtaining permission.

The textbook is out of print. Does that change things? No. University guidance states plainly that out of print does not mean out of copyright; in Canada, the term generally runs for the life of the creator plus 70 years.

What about selling the physical book after scanning it? Selling the physical copy is the first sale doctrine working as intended. Keeping and using a full scan afterward is precisely the substitute-for-a-copy fact pattern the amount and market factors weigh against — the framework's least comfortable row, and the reason this article will not bless it.
