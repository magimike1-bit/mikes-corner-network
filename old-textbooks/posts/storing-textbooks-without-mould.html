Storing Textbooks Without Mould: Humidity, Shelving and the Inspection Routine

Book hospital · 9 min read

This site spends most of its time finding ridiculous second careers for old textbooks. This article is about the opposite decision: the textbooks that are staying. A set of course books kept for reference, a child's future courses, or simple refusal to throw away a book that cost that much money — all fair reasons. The problem is that the usual storage plan, a cardboard box in the basement, is close to a mould incubator with a silverfish nursery attached, and the covers warp while nobody is looking. Mould, mildew, silverfish and warped covers look like four problems. Preservation guidance treats them as one: an environment that is too damp, too changeable, and never inspected. Fix the environment, support the books properly, and check them on a schedule, and all four problems mostly stay away.

One cause, three symptoms

Start with why the basement box fails. The Library of Congress is direct about location: do not store objects of value in the basement, attic, or garage, because those areas have poor temperature and relative humidity control and a higher risk of leaks and floods. A university preservation guide adds the mechanism — places where heat and humidity fluctuate wildly, including spots near radiators and air vents, speed up the degradation of acidic paper, encourage mould growth, harbour insects and pests, and encourage adhesives to fail. Bathrooms and kitchens are on the same list, for humidity and splashes. The pests are a humidity signal, not a separate invasion: the Northeast Document Conservation Center (NEDCC) notes that the presence of silverfish and booklice often indicates a humidity problem in the storage area, and that over time silverfish can eat holes in paper collections. Silverfish also feed on the starch in old bindings, while booklice feed on the mould that moves in when moisture does. Control the moisture and the menu closes.

Warping is the quieter symptom, and it is mostly mechanical. A heavy textbook leaning on a part-filled shelf is being bent, slowly, by its own weight. Preservation guidance is explicit that leaning books are subject to distortion and damage, that books shelved on their fore-edge with spines up sag and pull away from their covers, and that a book too large to stand upright belongs flat, on its side, with similarly sized books — not forced onto a shelf at an angle.

The numbers that matter: temperature and relative humidity

Different preservation sources give slightly different target bands, and the honest reading is that they agree on the shape: cool, dry, and above all stable. An archival guide gives a best environment of a stable 68–72 degrees Fahrenheit and 38–50% relative humidity, warns against daily fluctuations, and notes that very low humidity — below 15% — can cause brittleness. The Library of Congress, answering a question about book pests, gives a preventive relative humidity band of 35–50%, and notes that musty odours fade when books are stored with air circulation in cool environments at 35–55% relative humidity. Reporting from the Huntington Library's preservation programme quotes a wider working target of 40–60% relative humidity and 60 to 77 degrees. Read together: aim for the middle of the overlap — roughly 40–50% relative humidity in a room that feels like a normal living space — and treat stability as the real goal. A room that holds a steady 55% beats a room that swings between 35% and 70% every time the weather changes, because the swings are what attics, basements and garages do.

One practical consequence: a cheap hygrometer in the storage room is not optional equipment for long-term storage. Without a reading, every decision below is a guess, and the first sign of a problem becomes the smell when a book is opened years later.

Shelving material matters at the margins. Both the Library of Congress and NEDCC prefer metal shelving for collections: wood can give off damaging pollutants, and the Library of Congress notes that acids present in wood can migrate into paper and books. Existing wooden shelves do not need to be thrown out — they can be lined with a barrier such as polyester film, corrugated board, metal foil laminate, acrylic sheet or glass, so books are not in direct contact with the wood. A custom archival box has a second use beyond boxing: conservator Jeff Peachey, quoted in preservation reporting, recommends one for a book that matters because it blocks light, softens humidity swings and protects the cover.

The storage procedure: 9 steps

Step 1: Quarantine anything new or suspect. Before a second-hand textbook joins the shelf, smell it and look at it. The preservation advice reported from conservator Jeff Peachey is that a musty smell paired with spotting or water damage means the book stays away from the rest of the shelf. A musty smell on its own often means a book was exposed to high relative humidity and may have been mouldy or mildewed in the past. Suspect books live apart until they are dry, clean and odour-free — one damp book is a problem; a damp book pressed between ten healthy ones is a colony.

Step 2: Pick a living-space room. A bedroom, study or hallway in the main part of the house, where temperature and humidity are relatively stable. Not the basement, attic or garage, not a bathroom or kitchen, and not against a radiator, air vent or other source of heat or cold swings.

Step 3: Measure before moving anything in. Put a hygrometer in the room and read it over several days. The working target from the sources above is roughly 40–50% relative humidity, stable. A room that sits well above that needs ventilation or a dehumidifier before it needs books.

Step 4: Clean the space, then keep food out of it. Library of Congress guidance for book pests starts with housekeeping: keep collection areas clean, tidy and free from food and drink residues. Its cleaning guidance adds keeping books off the floor and dusting with magnetic dusters that contain no fragrances or other chemicals.

Step 5: Shelf the everyday books upright and supported. Average-sized textbooks stand upright alongside similarly sized volumes. Fill each shelf, or use bookends, so every book is supported upright rather than leaning. Never shelve a book on its fore-edge with the spine up. Oversize volumes that cannot stand upright go flat on their side, stacked only with similarly sized books.

Step 6: Box the long-term books the archival way. For books being stored for a prolonged period, the Library of Congress says to choose acid- and lignin-free boxes, in a size that will not become too heavy to handle safely. Pack books flat with heavier and larger volumes at the bottom, or vertically, spine down. Centre the weight, pad out empty space, and avoid overpacking. Notre Dame's guidance adds: do not stack flat books on top of vertically packed ones in the same box.

Step 7: Raise everything off the floor. Shelves and boxes sit above floor level, so a small leak is an inconvenience rather than a soaked collection. Off the floor is also the Library of Congress's housekeeping starting point for cleaning.

Step 8: Label and list what went where. A box labelled with its contents does not need to be opened for a look — and every unnecessary opening, in a marginal room, is an inspection nobody recorded. A simple list, on the box and in a note, is enough.

Step 9: Put the inspection dates in a calendar now. Storage that depends on remembering will not happen. The routine below is short; its only real requirement is that it exists on specific dates.

What success looks like

Success is checkable, not a feeling. The hygrometer reads in the target band — roughly 40–50% relative humidity — on ordinary days, not just on the day the room was set up. Every book on the shelf stands upright with support on both sides, or lies flat in a small stack of similar-sized books; no cover leans, bows or opens on its own. Boxes are acid-free, labelled, off the floor, and firm when lifted — not sagging, not overpacked. An inspection finds no musty smell, no spotting, no live or dead insects, and no new warping. A room and a shelf that pass those checks, twice a year, are a storage system. Anything less is a hope.

The inspection routine

Twice a year is a workable cadence for a home collection — once a year is the minimum that still counts as a routine. Work shelf by shelf. Read the hygrometer record first: a room that spent a wet month above the target band explains anything found next. Take each textbook down, fan the pages once, and look at the spine, the page edges and the inside covers — the places mould spotting and insect damage show first. NEDCC describes the insects to know: booklice are extremely small, about 1–2 mm long, while silverfish are larger, up to 12.5 mm. Smell the book: mustiness is information, not just an odour. Check the covers against a flat surface for new bowing. Dust the shelf before the books go back, and put sticky traps at the shelf ends if insects have ever been seen — NEDCC's suggested monitor for exactly that situation. Anything suspect comes out and stays out, in quarantine, until it is resolved; it does not go back between healthy books while a decision is pending.

When a book is already mouldy or infested

Two escalations, both from preservation sources, and neither is routine storage. For wet or mouldy books, the Library of Congress says to set them out to air dry immediately and control the ambient temperature and relative humidity — and if newly wet or mouldy books cannot be air dried within two days, to prepare and freeze them. Where only mould stains remain, keeping ambient relative humidity between 40–65% prevents regrowth, with regular checks. For an insect infestation, NEDCC's sequence is inspect to find whether the problem is isolated or widespread, reduce the humidity, isolate and vacuum the affected materials, and monitor with sticky traps; non-chemical measures are preferable, and a widespread problem is a job for a preservation professional. Preservation reporting from the Huntington adds the home freezing method for bugs — seal the book in a bag first, so condensation does not form as it thaws, freeze for three days, six if being careful, and expect that eggs can survive a first freeze, so a bad case may need a second round — with the warning that freezing treats the bugs, not the cause. The cause is always the room.

FAQ

Are plastic bins really that bad? The bin is not the villain; the room is. The Library of Congress treats polyethylene and polypropylene bins as useful secondary protection against water damage, and warns that a plastic container in a high-humidity area increases mould risk. A bin in a dry living-space room is reasonable. The same bin in a damp basement is a sealed mould chamber.

What about silica gel packets in each box? They are not covered by the preservation sources used for this article, and no packet can hold a bad room at 45% for a year. Measure the room first; a desiccant is, at best, a supplement to a room that already passes the hygrometer check, never the system itself.

The books are already in the basement. How urgent is this? Urgency follows the readings, not the address. Move a hygrometer down for a week. A basement that somehow holds a stable, dry reading is a lesser emergency than its reputation — but leaks and floods are the second half of the Library of Congress warning, and no reading predicts a burst pipe. Living-space storage remains the recommendation.

Can a warped cover be flattened back? Prevention is the subject here, deliberately: the sources above establish that leaning and unsupported storage cause the distortion, and a book that still matters deserves conservation advice before weight, moisture or heat is applied to its cover at home. What can be done today is to stop the cause — shelve it flat, fully supported, in a stable room — so the warp does not get worse.
