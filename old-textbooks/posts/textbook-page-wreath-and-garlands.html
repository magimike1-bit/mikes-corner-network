Textbook Page Wreaths and Garlands: Two Ways to Hang a Dead Textbook

Wall decor, budgeted · 8 min read

A wreath is a textbook that learned to be round. A garland is a textbook that learned to be long. The craft internet teaches both, in total isolation from each other: wreath tutorials never mention garlands, garland tutorials never mention wreaths, and almost none of them say the one thing a textbook owner needs to know before the first page is torn — how many pages the project is going to eat. A textbook is a finite resource with a page count printed right in it. This article treats it like one: four hanging projects compared by pages consumed, tools, and display space, then a full procedure for the wreath and the garland, with the page budget settled before any demolition begins.

The decision writes itself once the space is named. A door or a blank wall wants a circle, and the pinned wreath is the circle with the least cutting and the most forgiving technique — every fold is allowed to be slightly different, and the differences are what give it dimension. A mantel or a doorway wants length, and the leaf garland delivers it for about a quarter of the pinned wreath's page budget. The cone wreath is the heavy industry option — beautiful, and built like it. The sewn garland is what happens to the pages left over from everything else.

Before a page moves: the ownership rule and the page budget

The ownership rule is the same one every craft on this site starts from: only a textbook owned outright, never a borrowed book, a library book, or a book with resale or sentimental value — and preferably one whose edition is long superseded. Then, the budget, which no tutorial bothers with. Count the book's pages first. A roughly 200-page book funds the pinned wreath on its own. A fatter textbook funds the wreath and still leaves garland money: reserve about 50 pages for the leaf garland before the wreath build starts, and tear from the back of the book for one project and the front for the other so the accounting stays visible. One warning travels with the budget: a book that is too old and fragile will tear too easily for the pinned wreath's folding, so brittle textbooks go to the garland column, where cutting does the work that folding cannot.

Project 1: the pinned-page wreath

The pinned wreath is a foam form wearing several hundred small paper fans, and its great virtue is that nothing is glued until it misbehaves. Supplies: an 18-inch styrofoam wreath form, the textbook, pins, about 4 feet of 4-inch burlap wired ribbon for a hanger and bow, and a glue gun on standby.

Step 1: Build the page stack. Tear pages out of the book carefully until there is a generous stack. Tear, do not cut — the wreath's texture comes from pages that left the book the honest way, and tearing is faster than any blade.

Step 2: Fold the fans. Fold a page slightly in half lengthwise and then crosswise, pinch it in the center, and bring it together at the top. Make each one slightly different on purpose — some smaller, some bigger, with varying folds. Uniformity is the enemy; dimension is the product.

Step 3: Pin the base of each fan. Push a pin through the bottom of the folded page, making sure it catches all the layers. Fingers get sore doing this a few hundred times; a rubber jar opener between thumb and pin top is the field remedy the tutorial tradition hands down.

Step 4: Fill the form from the inside out. Push the pins into the foam starting on the inside ring and working around the circumference, then add layer after layer on top. An 18-inch form takes about six or seven layers before it reads as a wreath instead of a foam ring with ambitions.

Step 5: Patch and secure. Look for sparse spots and fill them with extra folded pages pushed between the layers. Any pin that pops out gets a dab of hot glue to hold it — the only glue the whole build officially needs.

Step 6: Hang it. Pin the burlap ribbon to the underside of the form, centered, with the ends hanging down, and use the wire in the ribbon's edges to give it shape. The wreath hangs beside a fireplace, on a door, or anywhere a circle of equations improves the architecture — which, the record shows, is most places.

Project 2: the leaf garland (and the sewn shortcut)

The garland trades the wreath's depth for length, and its unit is the leaf: one page, one shape, repeated about fifty times until a mantel surrenders. Supplies: the textbook, scissors or a craft knife, a pencil, a leaf template, tape, twine, and cup hooks to hang it from. Pine cones, floral wire, and two large wooden beads are optional dressing.

Step 1: Trace the leaves. Print or draw a leaf template, tear out a section of pages, lay the template on the top page, and trace around it in pencil. Textbook diagram pages make excellent leaves — a garland with the occasional labelled cross-section is a garland with credentials.

Step 2: Cut the stack. Cut out the traced leaves, working through the stack until there are about 50 book page leaves. Fifty is the mantel-tested quantity; a doorway wants more, a bookshelf wants fewer, and the page budget from the planning section already decided which.

Step 3: Build the clusters. Tape three leaves together into a cluster, fanned slightly. Clusters are the garland's load-bearing units; single leaves are filler.

Step 4: String and swag the twine. Tie the twine onto the cup hooks and let it swag between them. Tape the clusters to the twine on the backs of the leaves so the tape never shows from the front, then fill in between the clusters with single leaves until the swag reads as foliage rather than stationery.

Step 5: Finish the ends. Tie a large wooden bead at each end of the garland. Pine cones hung over the cup hooks on floral wire are optional, but they do sell the season.

The sewn shortcut. Shorter on time than pages? The sewn garland needs only burlap strips, a needle, thread, and scissors. Fold a burlap strip like an accordion and string it on the threaded needle; fold book pages in half — cutting larger textbook pages in half lengthwise first, if needed — stack them, and string the stack onto the same thread. Alternate burlap and page stacks until the garland is the length wanted, then tie loops at the ends. The worked example ran about 1½ to 2 feet, which is exactly mantel-corner sized.

What success looks like

Success for the wreath: the foam form is invisible from every angle, six or seven layers deep, with no sparse patches and no pins showing; it hangs flat against the door and reads as texture first and textbook second. Success for the garland: clusters evenly spaced along a twine that swags in a smooth curve, tape nowhere visible from the front, leaves lying flat rather than curling into tubes, and a bead holding each end. Success for the budget: both decorations hanging, and a page count left over that matches the plan — proof the book was spent, not just spent time on.

Honesty corner

Three honest notes. First, these are paper decorations: the same flammability and fragility that made the pages cheap craft material does not retire when they reach the wall, so hang them as decor, not as lampshades. Second, pins and glue guns are the build's only real hazards — pins are sharp several hundred times in a row, and the sore-thumb warning in Step 3 is earned, not decorative. Third, the copyright note that applies to every project on this site: these are personal, handmade uses of a physical copy already owned, which is a very different thing from reproducing or selling the textbook's content.

FAQ

Can one textbook really fund both projects? Yes, if the page budget is planned first: a book of around 200 pages covers the pinned wreath alone, and a fatter textbook covers the wreath plus about 50 reserved pages for the leaf garland.

Which project suits a brittle, fragile textbook? The garland. A book too old and fragile tears too easily under the wreath's repeated folding; cutting leaves asks much less of the paper.

What is the cheapest project here in pages? The sewn garland — pages folded in half and strung in stacks, with the length set by the maker, starting from a worked example of about 1½ to 2 feet.
