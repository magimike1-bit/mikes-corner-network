1. Sector overview

Full-service restaurants — dine-in with table service, from casual bistros to fine dining — run on coordination between the floor and the kitchen. What makes this sector different:

  • Courses and timing matter. Appetizers, mains, desserts, and fired/hold commands mean orders have routing logic, not just item lists.
  • Tipping is part of checkout. Pre-auth, tip adjust, and tip pooling make card handling more complex than retail.
  • The kitchen is a real-time system. Kitchen display screens (KDS) or kitchen printers decide whether the line cooks in the right order.
  • Reservations and walk-ins coexist. Floor plans, table turns, and waitlists are operational tools, not nice-to-haves.
  • Margins are thin. A published 2025 industry figure put full-service margins around 2.8% — every fee line item matters more here than in most sectors.

Bundled platforms — a separate category

All-in-one platforms such as Toast and TouchBistro bundle the POS software, hardware, KDS, and payment processing into one package. The upside is a system designed to work together out of the box. The trade-off: the processing cost is hidden inside the bundle, and changing to a cheaper processor may require replacing the whole stack. Bundled platforms are kept in their own category on this site and are never mixed into standalone provider/POS comparisons.

Layout notes

  • KDS screens mount where the line can see them without turning — expo screen faces the pass, kitchen screen faces the hot line.
  • Kitchen printers need heat-rated paper and a spot away from direct steam; mount high enough that tickets don't get splashed.
  • Run ethernet to the kitchen before opening — retrofitting cable through a working kitchen is miserable.
  • Handheld docks live at the host stand on chargers; a dead handheld at 7pm is a staffing problem, not a tech problem.
  • Keep the switch and router in a locked office; label every cable at both ends.

Daily workflow

  1. Pre-service: KDS screens on and showing the right dayparts, printers loaded, handhelds charged, one test fire to the kitchen.
  2. Service: orders fired by course; expo bumps completed tickets; handhelds take payment at the table with tip adjust.
  3. If the KDS dies: switch routing to the kitchen printer (pre-configured fallback) — don't re-enter tickets by hand mid-rush.
  4. Close: settle card batches before the processor cutoff, reconcile tips, run the end-of-day report, charge everything.

4. Setup guide

Restaurants fail POS go-lives on the menu, not the hardware. Do these in order.

  1. Build the menu with modifiers first. Every item, every modifier (add chicken, no onion, dressing on side), every forced question — the menu is the database everything else hangs off.
  2. Set up taxes. Dine-in vs. takeout can tax differently depending on province and product — confirm with your accountant.
  3. Draw the floor and table map. Tables, sections, bar seats, patio — servers can't run a floor the system doesn't understand.
  4. Configure printer and KDS routing. Which items fire to the hot line, which to the bar, which to expo. Test every category, not just the common ones.
  5. Set up tipping. Tip prompts, tip adjust on pre-auths, tip pooling or tip-out rules if you use them.
  6. Define staff roles and permissions. Who can void, comp, discount, open the drawer without a sale — and an audit trail for all of it.
  7. Run live tests. Real orders, real payments, real voids and comps, on a quiet afternoon — with the kitchen actually cooking.
  8. Drill the offline scenario. Unplug the internet: what still works? Offline card behaviour varies by platform — confirm yours in writing with the vendor rather than assuming.
  9. Set the printer fallback. If a KDS screen dies, tickets must route to the kitchen printer automatically or with one tap — decided and tested before opening.
  10. Run a mock service. Staff as guests, full menu, real timing. You'll find three routing mistakes and one menu error — that's the point.
  11. Cut over on a slow day. Tuesday lunch, not Friday dinner. Keep the old system accessible for one week as a reference, not a crutch.

5. Install guides

Network first — especially the kitchen

Wire ethernet to every fixed point: KDS screens, kitchen/bar printers, server stations. The kitchen is hot, greasy, and full of metal — the worst environment for Wi-Fi. Use a dedicated POS Wi-Fi network for handhelds, physically or logically separate from guest Wi-Fi. Label every drop.

KDS install

Mount screens at line-of-sight for the cooks, connect via ethernet, and add them in the POS as kitchen stations. Configure ticket columns (new / working / done), bump behaviour, and all-day timers. Fire a test ticket from every menu category and confirm it lands on the right screen.

Kitchen / bar printer install

Assign a static IP (or DHCP reservation), add by IP in the POS, and set it as the fallback route for its KDS screen. Load heat-rated paper, test the cutter, and confirm the chime/bell is audible over the line noise.

Payment device install

Pair handhelds through the POS, confirm tip-adjust works on a pre-auth, and test a split bill across two cards. Record the settlement cutoff and the support number for payment issues — it differs from POS software support.

Offline drill

Unplug the internet during a quiet period and run the full flow: fire an order, check the KDS, take a card payment. Document exactly what degrades — some platforms queue card payments offline, some don't. Confirm your platform's behaviour with the vendor; don't generalize from another restaurant's setup.

8. PCI scope callout

The concept, in two sentences: if card data flows through your POS system (the POS sees, stores, or transmits the card number — including tip-adjust data tied to the card), your POS is in scope for PCI security requirements. If you take cards on a standalone terminal — or a terminal that encrypts card data in a validated tunnel straight to the processor so the POS never sees it — your scope is reduced.

This is a conceptual overview only, not a compliance determination. Your actual obligations must be confirmed with your processor/acquirer or a qualified assessor. Read the full plain-English version: PCI scope guide.

9. Integrations you may also need

Each integration is separated on purpose: what it is, why you'd want it, what it costs (real published pricing or "quote-based"), and an illustrative ROI sketch — a hypothetical example to show the math, not a promise. Prices below were observed in research during September 2026 and change often — verify current pricing before buying.

Online ordering

What it is: your own pickup/delivery ordering (website, QR code) feeding straight into the POS and KDS — no re-keying from a tablet.

Benefits: one order stream, no tablet farm, you keep the customer data and the margin that marketplaces take.

Cost: first-party online ordering is often bundled with the POS platform; GloriaFood offers a free tier (verify current terms). Marketplace commissions are separate and much higher.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): a restaurant shifting 50 orders/week from a 25%-commission marketplace to its own $0-commission ordering at a $40 average ticket keeps roughly $500/week in commission — minus any platform fee. Your volume and mix will differ.

Delivery middleware

What it is: one hub (e.g., Deliverect, Chowly, ItsaCheckmate) that merges Uber Eats, DoorDash, and Skip orders into your POS/KDS.

Benefits: kills the tablet farm; orders fire to the kitchen like any other ticket; fewer missed or duplicated orders.

Cost: quote-based — priced by locations and order volume.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): if tablet chaos costs 30 minutes of manager time daily and 2–3 missed orders a week, the payback is usually measured in weeks for busy delivery locations. Model it with your own labour cost and miss rate.

Reservations & waitlist

What it is: online reservations, table management, and waitlist tied to your floor plan.

Benefits: fewer no-shows, faster table turns, a guest database for slow nights.

Cost: OpenTable around $249/month plus per-cover fees (third-party figure observed September 2026 — verify current pricing). Simpler waitlist tools cost less; many POS platforms bundle basic versions.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): if reservations cut no-shows from 8% to 3% on 200 covers/week at a $45 average check, that's roughly $450/week in recovered revenue against the subscription. Your no-show rate decides this one.

Inventory & food-costing

What it is: recipe costing, invoice capture, and theoretical-vs-actual food cost (e.g., MarketMan, MarginEdge).

Benefits: you see which menu items actually make money and where waste or over-portioning leaks margin.

Cost: MarketMan around $199/month starter (observed September 2026 — verify current pricing); MarginEdge is quote-based.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): a restaurant doing $80K/month in food sales that trims food cost by 2 points through portion and waste control saves ~$1,600/month. The software only pays if someone acts on the numbers weekly.

Staff scheduling

What it is: shift scheduling with labour-cost forecasting (e.g., 7shifts).

Benefits: schedules matched to forecasted covers; less overstaffing on dead Tuesdays.

Cost: 7shifts roughly $40/month per location (observed September 2026 — verify current pricing).

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): cutting 5 unnecessary labour hours a week at $20/hour saves ~$400/month against a ~$40 subscription. Requires a manager who actually trims the schedule.

Accounting connector (QuickBooks / Xero)

What it is: daily sales, tips, and payouts pushed into bookkeeping automatically.

Benefits: clean month-end, tip reporting that reconciles, no spreadsheet archaeology.

Cost: QuickBooks Online / Xero roughly $30–80 CAD/month (observed September 2026), connectors often extra. Pricing changes — verify before buying.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): if your bookkeeper spends 6 hours a month untangling POS exports at $60/hour, halving that is ~$180/month against ~$60 in software. Your rates will differ.
