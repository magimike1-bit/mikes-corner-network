1. Sector overview

Retail covers independent shops and specialty stores — apparel, gifts, electronics, hobby, home goods, and similar single- or few-location businesses. What makes retail different from other sectors:

  • Inventory is the business. SKUs, variants (size/colour), suppliers, purchase orders, stock counts, and shrink drive almost every POS decision.
  • Checkout is lane-based. One or more fixed lanes, each with a POS station, payment terminal, printer, drawer, and often a barcode scanner.
  • Multi-channel is normal. Many shops sell in-store and online; the two stock pools must stay in sync or you oversell.
  • Debit matters in Canada. Interac debit is a large share of in-person transactions, so per-transaction fee structure matters as much as the percentage rate.
  • Seasonality is real. Holiday rushes stress lanes, returns, and stock counts — your setup should be tested before December, not during it.

Bundled platforms — a separate category

All-in-one platforms such as Square and Shopify POS bundle the POS software, hardware, and payment processing into one package with one monthly (or free) price. That simplicity has a cost: the processing cost is hidden inside the bundle, and you cannot see — or change — the interchange and markup breakdown. Changing to a cheaper processor later may require replacing the whole stack. Bundled platforms are never mixed into standalone provider/POS comparisons on this site; they are evaluated as a single decision: the bundle, or a best-of-breed standalone stack where you can see every fee.

Layout notes

  • Printer sits under the counter, out of the customer's way; the drawer cable is short, so plan the printer-to-drawer distance first.
  • Payment terminal goes on the customer side of the lane, reachable for tap without handing the card over the counter.
  • Scanner lives on a stand at elbow height; a holstered Bluetooth scanner works better if staff roam the floor.
  • Lanes should face the flow of foot traffic so queues don't block the door or the aisles.
  • The network switch lives in a locked office or cabinet, not under a lane where it can be kicked or unplugged.
  • Keep one spare of every cable (USB, ethernet, RJ11 kick, power) in the office — cables fail more often than hardware.

Daily workflow

  1. Open: count the float, verify yesterday's Z-report matches the deposit, power-check printers and terminals, run one small test card sale.
  2. Sell: scan, tender, hand or email the receipt; price overrides need a manager PIN so discounts stay visible.
  3. Mid-day: if a lane misbehaves, move the queue to the other lane and troubleshoot off to the side — never hold up a line debugging.
  4. Close: run the Z-report, reconcile cash to the report, batch/settle card transactions per your processor's cutoff, and put terminals and tablets on charge.

4. Setup guide

Do these in order. Each step prevents a specific week-one disaster.

  1. Clean your SKUs and variants before import. Decide your naming for products, sizes, colours, and barcodes now. Garbage in the import becomes garbage at the till for years.
  2. Test the import with a small CSV batch. Import 20–50 items first, check prices, taxes, and barcodes at the lane, then import the rest.
  3. Set up Canadian taxes correctly. GST/HST/PST rules vary by province and product type — confirm your setup with your accountant, not the POS vendor's defaults.
  4. Build the supplier and purchase-order workflow. Enter suppliers, cost prices, and reorder points before you need to reorder; your first PO should be creatable on day one.
  5. Decide the barcode strategy. Manufacturer barcodes where they exist, generated labels where they don't — and one label printer workflow staff actually follow.
  6. Set up locations and transfers. If you have a back room, a second store, or a warehouse location in the system, test a stock transfer before cutover.
  7. Sync ecommerce stock if you sell online. One stock pool shared between store and web, with a sync test on a low-stock item so you see oversell protection work.
  8. Configure gift cards and loyalty. Sell a gift card, redeem it, refund part of it — all before a customer is standing in front of you.
  9. Test payments, refunds, and exchanges. Run a real card sale, void it, refund it, and exchange an item. Know what each looks like on the statement.
  10. Do a physical count before cutover. Your opening stock numbers should be counted, not guessed. Everything downstream — margins, reorders, shrink — depends on them.

5. Install guides

Network first

Install and test the network before any POS hardware arrives. Wired ethernet to fixed devices (terminals, ethernet printers), strong Wi-Fi for tablets. Put POS devices on their own network segment if your router supports it, and keep guest Wi-Fi completely separate. Run a speed and stability test at the busiest physical spot in the store.

Receipt printer install

USB: connect directly to the POS station, install the driver or select the model in the POS app, print a test receipt. Ethernet: assign a static IP (or a DHCP reservation so the address never changes), add the printer by IP in the POS settings, print a test receipt. Load paper, check the cutter, and confirm the drawer kick fires on a cash sale.

Payment terminal install

Pair the terminal through your POS app or processor's pairing flow. Confirm the integration mode with your processor (integrated with the POS vs. standalone — see the PCI callout below). Run test transactions for chip, tap, and Interac debit, then confirm the test batch settles. Write down the settlement cutoff time.

Cash drawer install

Connect the RJ11/RJ12 cable from the drawer to the printer's kick port — not to the POS station. Enable the drawer kick in the POS settings and test it with a cash sale. Confirm the drawer locks and that only manager roles can open it without a sale.

Barcode scanner install

USB scanners are usually plug-and-play: connect, open the POS, scan a product barcode, confirm the right item appears. For Bluetooth scanners, pair once, then confirm the scanner reconnects after a power cycle. Test on your smallest and most reflective barcodes.

Offline drill

Unplug the internet and run a full sale. Know exactly what still works (usually: cash sales; sometimes: card sales queued) and what doesn't (refunds, gift-card balance checks, online stock sync). Write the drill result on a card taped inside the office — future-you will thank present-you.

8. PCI scope callout

The concept, in two sentences: if card data flows through your POS system (the POS sees, stores, or transmits the card number), your POS is in scope for PCI security requirements. If you take cards on a standalone terminal — or a terminal that encrypts card data in a validated tunnel straight to the processor so the POS never sees it — your scope is reduced.

This is a conceptual overview only, not a compliance determination. Your actual obligations must be confirmed with your processor/acquirer or a qualified assessor. Read the full plain-English version: PCI scope guide.

9. Integrations you may also need

Each integration below is separated on purpose: what it is, why you'd want it, what it costs (real published pricing or "quote-based"), and an illustrative ROI sketch — a hypothetical example to show the math, not a promise.

Ecommerce / POS inventory connector

What it is: a connector that keeps your online store's stock in sync with your POS inventory — one stock pool, two sales channels.

Benefits: stops overselling when an item sells in-store and online the same hour; one place to update prices and product info.

Cost: third-party estimates observed September 2026 put platforms like Shopify around $39/month and Lightspeed around $89/month, with dedicated connectors typically $89–199/month. Pricing changes — verify current pricing before buying.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): a shop doing $30,000/month online that cuts oversell cancellations from 4% to 1% of online orders saves roughly $900/month in recovered margin and support time against ~$150/month in connector cost. Your mix will differ.

Accounting connector (QuickBooks / Xero)

What it is: daily sales, refunds, and payouts pushed from your POS into your bookkeeping automatically.

Benefits: no manual re-keying, cleaner month-end, your accountant gets clean data instead of spreadsheets.

Cost: QuickBooks Online / Xero roughly $30–80 CAD/month (observed September 2026), with POS connectors often $20–50/month. Pricing changes — verify current pricing before buying.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): if manual bookkeeping takes your bookkeeper 4 hours a month at $60/hour, automation saving half of that is ~$120/month against ~$60/month in software. Your rates will differ.

Warehouse / WMS integration

What it is: a warehouse management link for shops that outgrow the stockroom — barcode-directed receiving, bin locations, multi-location transfers.

Benefits: fewer receiving errors, accurate multi-location stock, less dead stock hiding in the wrong place.

Cost: quote-based — depends on locations, users, and whether you need EDI with suppliers.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): for a growing shop, cutting stock-count labour from two full days to half a day per month is the usual payback story. Model it with your own labour cost.

Loyalty program

What it is: points or visit-based rewards tied to the customer record at checkout.

Benefits: repeat visits, a customer list you own, measurable promotion results instead of guesswork.

Cost: often bundled with the POS platform; standalone loyalty tools are typically bundled or quote-based. Verify current pricing before buying.

Illustrative ROI (example only — not a promise): if loyalty members visit 20% more often and represent a third of revenue, even a modest lift compounds fast — but acquisition cost and discount depth decide whether it pays. Run it on your own basket data.

12. How AI is changing this sector

Real products only — no vapourware, no "AI-powered" marketing fluff presented as fact.

  • Prediko — demand forecasting for Shopify-based retailers; quote-based pricing (vendor site, observed September 2026). Vendor performance claims should be treated as marketing until proven on your data.
  • Cin7 — established inventory and order-management platform adding forecasting features; third-party pricing observed around $349–$999/month (September 2026 — verify current pricing).
  • Lightspeed analytics — "smart reporting" dashboards on top of your sales data: useful descriptive analytics, not deep AI. Good for spotting trends; not a forecaster.
  • Review-response drafts — several tools now draft replies to Google reviews. Practical rule: require human approval before anything posts. One bad auto-reply costs more than the time saved.
