Does Pet Insurance Cover Prescription Food in Canada? One Partial Yes, and the 60 Day Limit

Coverage · 6 min read

Published 2026-11-08 · Last updated 2026-11-08

Disclaimer: This is general information about how prescription food coverage typically works, not financial or insurance advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Food coverage, percentages and time limits vary by provider and can change, and the policy wording controls over any FAQ page. The monthly diet figure in the cost example is a labelled assumption used for arithmetic, checked against provider material on November 8, 2026. It is not a retail price or a quote. Confirm food coverage in a current sample policy before buying.

50 percent, for 60 days. Those two numbers are the strongest prescription food offer in the Canadian material checked for this guide, and they are worth reading slowly, because they describe a benefit that helps with the first two months of a diet and then stops, on a cost that usually runs for the rest of the pet life. Other major providers do not offer a smaller version of the same benefit. They exclude food completely. So the answer to the title question is not yes or no. It is one partial yes, two clear noes, and a conditions list that decides whether the partial yes pays anything at all.

The three positions in Canada, compared

Fetch (Canada)NoFetch Canada FAQ states it does not cover prescription food for a dog or cat. Prescription medications and supplements recommended by a vet to treat a new injury or illness are covered, which makes food the specific gap in an otherwise broad policy.
PetsecureNoThe policy wording lists any food, including prescription or therapeutic diets, under what it will not pay for. The exclusion is for food as a category, not for one brand or diet type.

Notice what the table does to a common shopping habit. Comparing policies by a single covered or not covered tick makes Trupanion and a hypothetical full food benefit look identical, and makes Fetch and Petsecure look identical to each other, which they are on this one item. The useful comparison is the shape: included but halved and time limited, versus excluded as a category. A buyer whose pet already eats a therapeutic diet, or whose breed and age make one likely, should weight that shape heavily. A buyer whose pet eats ordinary food and has no condition on the horizon should weight it lightly, because a benefit tied to a diagnosis they may never receive is worth less than core accident and illness terms that apply to every claim.

One year of prescription food, costed

Assumption, labelled as an assumption: a therapeutic diet costs $110 a month, every month, for a full year. That is $1,320 a year. The figure is an arithmetic input, not a price check; real diets vary by pet size, brand and condition. Now run that year through the three positions above.

That table is the honest version of the marketing line. Half the cost covered is true, for two months. Across the first year it is $110 of a $1,320 cost. Across a chronic condition managed by diet for five years, at the same assumed monthly figure, the diet costs $6,600 and the same benefit still pays the same $110 once, under the terms as published. None of that makes the benefit worthless; $110 is $110, and it arrives in the expensive first months after a diagnosis, when diagnostics and medication changes are also landing. It does mean prescription food coverage should almost never be the reason a policy is chosen. Choose the policy for its accident and illness core, and treat any food benefit as a small, early discount on a long cost you will mostly carry yourself.

There is a budgeting consequence worth stating plainly. A pet prescribed a long term therapeutic diet has acquired a fixed monthly cost that behaves like a second, smaller premium, except it never resets, never gets reimbursed at a percentage after the window, and continues for as long as the condition does. Households comparing policies for a pet already on such a diet should add the full diet cost to the premium when they compare monthly outgo, not the diet cost minus an assumed food benefit. Under two of the three positions above there is no benefit to subtract, and under the third it expires in 60 days.

The conditions that decide a food claim

Under the one partial yes, four conditions do most of the work, all of them stated on Trupanion Canada published FAQ material.

  1. An eligible condition comes first. Trupanion describes prescription food as covered for eligible conditions, with diets listed as helping digestive issues, urinary and kidney conditions, joint problems and arthritis, diabetes, food allergies and heart disease. Food for a condition the policy does not cover has no route to payment, however therapeutic the diet is.
  2. The veterinarian must prescribe and dispense it. Coverage only includes food the veterinarian prescribes and dispenses. The same diet bought from a pet store or an online retailer, on a vet verbal suggestion, sits outside that wording. Where the food is bought can decide the claim before the food itself is considered.
  3. The food must meet the therapeutic standard. Trupanion limits coverage to food formulated, tested and manufactured with guaranteed analysis and safety standards to aid treatment of specific medical conditions. It names established veterinary diet lines among its examples, including Hill's Prescription Diet, Royal Canin Veterinary and Purina Pro Plan Veterinary Diets.
  4. Whole diet categories are excluded even when prescribed. General maintenance diets, puppy or kitten diets, homemade diets and raw food diets are not covered, even if prescribed and dispensed by a veterinarian. A prescription alone does not rescue a diet type on that list.

What success looks like when checking a policy for food cover: you can point to the sentence that includes or excludes food, state the percentage and the day limit if it is included, and name where the food must be bought for a claim to stand. If the answer came from a comparison site summary rather than the provider own wording or FAQ, it is not verified yet.

One related distinction prevents a common disappointment. Fetch covers prescription medications and supplements a vet recommends for a new injury or illness, while excluding prescription food. A household reading a covered list quickly can easily carry away the impression that prescribed items in general are covered. They are not a single category. Medication, supplements and food are three separate lines in policy wording, and each can sit on a different side of the covered boundary within the same policy.

Should prescription food decide which policy you buy

Use three tests, in this order. First, is a therapeutic diet a present cost or a plausible near term one for this pet? If neither, food coverage is a tiebreaker at most. Second, if a diet is likely, does the policy you are otherwise choosing include food, and have you run the full year arithmetic above with your real diet figure in place of the $110 assumption? A benefit worth $110 once should not outweigh a weaker annual limit or a worse deductible on the core policy. Third, can you buy the diet through the channel the wording requires, at a price you can sustain after any window expires? A diet that only works financially while half of it is reimbursed is a diet with a month three problem.

For most households the answer lands in the same place: budget for prescription food as an owner cost, in full, from the month it is prescribed. If the chosen policy happens to return half of the first two months, treat that as a small rebate on a hard month, not as coverage in any lasting sense. The policies are at their most honest on this item when their wording is read literally, and the literal reading is short enough to check before any premium is paid.
