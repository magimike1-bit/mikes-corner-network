How Pet Insurance Handles Chronic Conditions Year After Year

Chronic conditions · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-28 · Last updated 2026-10-28

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance policy structures typically work — not financial advice and not a summary of any specific policy. The worked example uses figures from the sources cited, labelled as an example. Always read your own policy wording before relying on it.

$3,000 a year. That is the diabetes spending figure in one worked example (s1), and it behaves three completely different ways depending on a single clause in a policy. Under an annual limit, $3,000 is a yearly charge against a pot that refills. Under a lifetime per-condition maximum, that same $3,000 consumes 20% of a $15,000 cap in one year (s1). Under a renewal-reset model, a condition claimed in one policy year can become pre-existing at renewal (s4). Same pet, same diagnosis, same spend — three different futures. The comparison belongs up top, before any detail:

Five years of diabetes, run through the structures

The arithmetic is simple enough to redo on an envelope, and every number in it comes from the sources. Assume $3,000 a year in diabetes spending (s1). Over five years that is $15,000 in total spending. Under a $15,000 lifetime per-condition maximum, the cumulative draw runs $3,000, $6,000, $9,000, $12,000, $15,000 — the cap is fully consumed at the end of year five, and year six starts with nothing left for that condition, however long the pet lives.

Under an annual-limit structure, the same five years never accumulate. Diabetes diagnosed after enrolment and the waiting period is covered — insulin, testing supplies, veterinary visits, prescription diets and complications such as cataract surgery — and coverage continues for the pet's lifetime subject to annual policy limits and renewal terms (s3). What changes at renewal under some annual-limit policies is the structure covered in the reset section below, not the arithmetic.

Under the lifetime per-condition deductible model, the shape changes again. That model pays 90% of the invoice once the deductible is met, with unlimited lifetime payouts and no payout limits, and once the deductible is met for an eligible condition the owner never meets it again — the condition is covered for life, a design the source describes as suited to chronic conditions such as allergies or cancer (s2). There is no cap counting down. The figure to scrutinise before buying is the deductible itself, because it is paid once per condition rather than once per year.

The renewal reset: when last year's claim becomes this year's exclusion

The fourth structure is the one to search for by name. One Canadian provider's New Condition Coverage model makes conditions claimed in one policy year pre-existing at renewal — a trade-off that lowers premiums but can leave gaps compared with lifetime per-condition policies (s4).

For a one-off accident, that trade may never bite. For a chronic condition, it bites by definition: chronic means the condition is still there next year. A policy that reclassifies claimed conditions at renewal is, for diabetes or arthritis, close to a one-year benefit with a premium attached. Neither the deductible nor the reimbursement rate reveals this. Only the renewal wording does.

A note on labels: much of the chronic-conditions advice online imports a UK taxonomy of lifetime, time-limited and maximum-benefit cover. The Canadian mechanics in the sources behind this piece — a lifetime per-condition deductible, annual limits that continue, a renewal reset — use none of those product labels. Read Canadian policies in their own terms, not through imported categories.

What never resets

Some exclusions do not refill, deplete or renew. Exclusions for pre-existing lameness or arthritis carry indefinitely (s1). Conditions diagnosed after enrolment generally stay covered year after year, subject to policy limits (s1) — but the ones that predated enrolment stay outside.

Remission does not reliably change that. Most policies still classify feline diabetes as pre-existing even after remission, so a cat whose diabetes goes quiet has not necessarily reopened coverage if it returns (s3). The stakes of the timing are not small: industry data cited by one source puts diabetes at 1 in 300 dogs and 1 in 200 cats, with peak diagnosis in senior pets (s3). This is a common condition arriving late in life, which is precisely when the policy structure has the least time left to forgive a bad one.

Renewal-year checklist: read the policy before the anniversary

  1. Find which structure you own. Search the policy wording for the limit type: annual limit, per-condition lifetime maximum, per-condition deductible, or a renewal clause that reclassifies claimed conditions. The table at the top of this page is the sorting key.
  2. If you hold a per-condition cap, do the depletion sum. Divide the cap by last year's spending on that condition. On the worked figures — $15,000 divided by $3,000 — the answer is five years (s1). Your numbers will differ; the division is the point.
  3. Check the renewal clause before claiming patterns set. If claimed conditions become pre-existing at renewal, price that honestly against a lifetime per-condition alternative before the diagnosis anniversary, not after (s4).
  4. List every pre-existing exclusion separately. Those do not reset under any structure, and remission does not reliably remove them (s1, s3). Budget for them as out-of-pocket costs.
  5. Confirm the chronic basics are inside cover — medication, testing supplies, monitoring visits and complications, the items listed for diabetes cover in the sources (s3) — rather than assuming a diagnosis being "covered" includes all of them.

What success looks like: you can state, for your pet's most likely chronic condition, which of the four structures would pay in year three — and the year the money runs out, if it does.
