How to appeal a denied pet insurance claim in Canada

Claims · 8 min read

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance typically works, not financial advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Coverage, limits, and definitions vary by provider and province. Always read your own policy wording before relying on it.

14 to 30 days. That is the appeal window many insurers allow from the date of a denial letter, and it starts running before most owners have finished being angry about the letter itself. A denial is a decision made on a file. Your job in an appeal is to improve the file, quickly, in the order below.

First, get the denial in writing

If the denial arrived by phone, it is not yet something you can appeal effectively. Request the denial in writing, stating the specific reason and the policy provision relied on. Ontario guidance for insurance complaints makes the same point in general terms: ask for the decision in writing, quoting the specific policy wording it relies on, because a denial that names a clause can be checked against your own policy document and one given only over the phone cannot. Keep the letter, the date on it, and your full policy wording together from this point on. The appeal is won or lost on exact words.

Map the clause to the record

Denial reason on the letterRecord that answers it
Pre existing conditionComplete vet history showing when signs were first noted, and a vet letter on timing if the record is ambiguous
Waiting periodPolicy start date, the waiting period end date for that coverage type, and the treatment date, on one page
Missing documentationThe exact missing item, supplied in full, with a cover note naming the claim number
Policy exclusionThe exclusion wording itself, checked word by word against what was actually treated

Ask your clinic for the complete medical records early. Clinics are busy, and records requests are the step most likely to eat your window.

The appeal sequence, day by day

  1. Days 1 to 3: read the full policy document against the denial clause. Mark the sentence you believe supports coverage.
  2. Days 1 to 5: request complete medical records from your vet, and ask whether the vet will add a short clarifying letter.
  3. Days 5 to 10: write the appeal. One page of facts, the clause quoted, the record attached, and the outcome you are asking for. No anger, no history of premiums paid, neither changes a clause.
  4. Around day 10: submit to the insurer's appeals process and keep proof of submission and the date.
  5. After submission: insurers commonly acknowledge an appeal within 3 to 5 business days, and a review can take 15 to 45 business days. If no response arrives within about 15 business days of submission, follow up in writing.
  6. If the appeal fails: ask for the second internal level of review. Many insurers offer one, often involving a more senior team or an appeals committee.

What success looks like: a written decision on a complete file, either a reversed denial with a payment date, or a final position that names the clause clearly enough for you to judge the next step.

Escalation in Canada, honestly stated

Every insurer licensed in Ontario must maintain an internal complaints process with a designated complaints officer, and using it is free. That internal process is the reliable next step after a failed appeal. Beyond the insurer, be careful about promises you read online. The General Insurance OmbudService, for example, describes its work as covering home, auto, and business insurance disputes, so do not assume a pet claim fits a given external body's mandate without checking that body directly. Your provincial insurance regulator can explain which route, if any, covers a pet policy complaint in your province. For a large disputed amount, independent legal advice is the honest recommendation, and this article is general information, not legal or financial advice.

When an appeal is unlikely to work

Skip the effort, or spend it knowing the odds, when the treatment clearly falls inside a named exclusion, the condition was documented before the policy started, or the claim was filed outside a clear deadline with no explanation. In those cases the better use of the same hour is reviewing the policy itself, because the next claim is the one you can still protect.
