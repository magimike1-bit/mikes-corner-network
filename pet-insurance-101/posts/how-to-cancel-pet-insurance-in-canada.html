How to Cancel Pet Insurance in Canada: Refund Windows, End Dates, and the Reset Nobody Mentions

Money mechanics · 6 min read

Published 2026-11-08 · Last updated 2026-11-08

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance cancellation typically works, not financial or insurance advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Refund windows, notice methods and end dates vary by provider and province, and the policy wording controls over any FAQ page. Dollar figures in the worked example are labelled illustrative arithmetic, checked against provider material on November 8, 2026. They are not quotes. Read your own cancellation clause before you send anything.

Most people assume the hard part of cancelling pet insurance is getting money back. It usually is not. The refund, where one exists, is often the smallest number in the decision. The larger cost is quieter: once a policy ends, the coverage history ends with it, and starting again later means new waiting periods and a new look at everything your pet has ever been treated for. Cancel for the right reason and that cost may be worth paying. Cancel in a hurry over one renewal notice and it is an expensive way to save a monthly premium.

Three cancellation models, compared

Canadian facing providers do not run one shared cancellation rule. Provider material checked on November 8, 2026 shows three distinct shapes, and which one you hold decides both the refund and the last day of cover.

Two details in that table change real outcomes. First, the no claim condition: both Fetch and Spot tie the full early refund to not having used the policy. A claim filed in week two can close the full refund window even though the calendar window is still open. Second, the end date: a Sonnet policy runs to the end of the current monthly invoice period, while a Spot cancellation takes the future date you name in your notice. Assuming your cover ends the day you call is how a pet ends up uninsured for the gap you thought was still covered, or still insured, and still billed, for weeks you thought were finished.

Run the same cancellation as a monthly payer and the refund column mostly disappears. That is not a penalty; it is the shape of paying month by month. The practical lesson is narrower than most cancellation guides admit: the refund question matters mainly to annual prepay households inside, or just past, an early window. For everyone else, the decision turns on the next section, not on money coming back.

What cancelling resets

Embrace states the restart cost plainly on its cancellation help page, and it is worth reading as a warning about the product category, not only about one company. If you cancel and purchase a new policy later, waiting periods are reset and the new policy is subject to new underwriting. Conditions your pet has been treated for or diagnosed with are considered pre-existing at the new policy, whether the new policy is with the same company or another one, and may not be covered.

That single paragraph reframes the whole decision. A healthy two year old with an empty vet record loses little by cancelling; there is almost nothing to reclassify. A seven year old with a treated ear infection history, a dental extraction and a limp that was investigated last spring loses a great deal, because each of those records can follow the pet into the next policy as an exclusion. The premium saved is monthly and certain. The coverage lost is conditional and permanent, and it is lost at exactly the age where claims become more likely.

Insurers hold cancellation rights too, which is a separate matter from your choice to leave. Sonnet lists its grounds as failure to pay premiums, a case of animal abuse, or a false or exaggerated claim. Spot sample wording allows cancellation for nonpayment with at least 10 days notice, and for other stated reasons with at least 30 days notice. A policy that lapses for nonpayment is not the same event as a planned cancellation: the end date, the refund treatment and the record of why cover ended can all differ, so a household that has decided to leave should cancel deliberately, in writing, rather than simply stopping payment.

Cancel in the right order

  1. Read your own cancellation clause first. Find three things: the early refund window and its no claim condition, the notice method your insurer accepts, and the end date rule. Those three lines, in your wording, outrank every general guide, including this one.
  2. Decide the end date before you make contact. If a replacement policy is part of the plan, its start date and waiting periods belong in this decision. A cancellation date chosen before the replacement cover is understood creates the gap the reset warning is about.
  3. Send the cancellation the way your insurer specifies. Where a written request is required, include the items the insurer names. Sonnet, for example, asks for your name, your pet name and your policy number in a written request sent by email, fax or regular mail.
  4. State the future cancellation date in the notice. Spot wording is built around the policyholder advising the future date the policy is to cancel. Naming the date removes the guesswork about whether cover ends on receipt, at period end, or on the date you intended.
  5. Confirm the refund treatment in the reply. Ask which model applies to you: full early refund, refund of premium paid for periods after the last coverage date, or refund of prepaid premiums. Get the answer with the cancellation confirmation, not after the money fails to arrive.
  6. Keep the confirmation with the vet records. The end date of previous cover is a fact a future insurer, or a future claim dispute, may ask about. A dated confirmation is the proof.

What success looks like: you can state, from your own policy documents, the last day your pet is covered, the exact refund you are owed and why, and what a restart would reclassify. If any one of the three is a guess, the cancellation is not ready to send.

When reducing coverage beats cancelling

Cancellation is not the only response to a premium that has grown uncomfortable. Embrace, for one, offers a formal alternative on the same help page: log into the customer account and use the Change Coverage section to adjust the policy to better fit the budget, rather than ending it. The specific levers differ by provider, and any change is a question for your own insurer, but the principle travels: a reduced policy keeps the coverage history alive, while a cancelled one does not.

So the honest order of decisions is this. If the pet is young, healthy and lightly recorded at the vet, and the premium saving is real money in a tight budget, cancelling can be a clean, defensible choice, especially inside an early full refund window with no claim filed. If the pet carries a treatment history, price the reduced coverage option first and cancel only if the reduced premium still fails the budget. And if the trigger is a single renewal increase, treat the renewal audit as its own task before treating cancellation as the answer. The refund is a one time amount. The reset lasts for the rest of the pet life.
