How to File a Pet Insurance Claim: Steps, Documents and Deadlines

Claims · 8 min read

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance claims typically work — not financial or legal advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Deadlines, document requirements, and appeal routes vary by provider, province, and state. The deadlines and timeframes below are attributed to the providers named in the text, checked on October 17, 2026. Always check your own policy's claim deadline first — it is the one that applies to you.

Most pet insurance is reimbursement insurance: the vet is paid first, by the owner, and the insurer pays the owner back for the covered share. Spot describes exactly that flow — pay the vet bill as normal, then submit a claim for reimbursement based on the plan chosen. That makes a claim a paperwork exercise, and paperwork exercises are won on two things: gathering the right documents while they are easy to get, and filing well inside a deadline that is shorter, and less standard, than most owners assume.

Before leaving the vet: the paper that decides the claim

Spot's claim guidance starts with one document: an itemized, paid invoice from the vet showing each service, its cost, and the diagnosis, noting that complete documentation speeds processing. That invoice is different from a card receipt. A card receipt proves money moved; the itemized invoice shows what the money bought, which is what the claims team is deciding on.

The second document set is the medical record — the doctor's notes, laboratory results, and other visit documentation the vet keeps. Spot notes that these records are not typically sent home with the owner, and that the vet may need to submit them to the insurer. ManyPets makes the first-claim consequence concrete: with a first claim, it asks for the pet's medical records from the prior 18 months. That lookback is how an insurer checks whether a condition is new or pre-existing, and it is the most common reason a first claim moves slowly — the claim waits on records moving between a vet clinic and the insurer, not on the claim form itself.

While still at the clinic, do four things: confirm the invoice is itemized and shows the diagnosis; ask the clinic to note that records may be requested and who there handles insurer requests; photograph or scan the invoice the same day; and check that the pet's name and the treatment date on the invoice are correct, since those are the fields the claim will be matched against.

The filing procedure, step by step

  1. Pay the vet and keep the itemized, paid invoice. Under the reimbursement model described by Spot and ManyPets, treatment is paid at checkout as usual; the claim comes after.
  2. Check the policy basics before filing. Confirm the treatment date falls after any waiting period, note the deductible remaining for the year, and find the claim deadline in the policy — the table below shows why assuming one is risky.
  3. Submit through the channel the insurer actually tracks. Spot lists the member portal, mobile app, or email; Fetch accepts claims through its member portal on a computer or its app on a phone; ManyPets uses the "Make a Claim" button in the online account. Enter the visit details and upload the itemized bill — and, on a first claim, the medical record if it can be supplied directly.
  4. File even small claims. ManyPets advises submitting claims even when the invoice is less than the deductible, so eligible amounts count toward meeting the yearly deductible. A skipped small claim is deductible credit left unclaimed.
  5. Save the confirmation and the claim number. Spot emails when a claim is received, when more information is needed, and when it is complete, and status can be checked in the member center or app. File that confirmation with the invoice copy.
  6. Respond to record requests immediately. During review, the claims team verifies eligibility, applies the deductible and reimbursement rate, and calculates the amount, and reviewers may request medical records for new conditions, per Spot. A request sitting unanswered is the claim's clock standing still.
  7. Confirm how payment will arrive. ManyPets reimburses approved claims by direct deposit or check, at the owner's choice; Spot describes payment by check or direct deposit, typically within a few business days of complete documentation.

What success looks like: a confirmation with a claim number on the day of filing, a complete document set (itemized invoice, plus records on a first claim) already in the insurer's hands, and a calendar note for the follow-up date if no update arrives within the processing window below. A claim that needs nothing further from the owner is a finished task; a claim waiting on records is not, even if it was "submitted."

Deadlines are not standard: 90, 180, and 270 days

The spread between the shortest and longest of those deadlines is six months. An owner who changes insurers and keeps filing on the old insurer's rhythm can miss the new one by a wide margin — under Fetch's 90-day rule, a claim filed on a comfortable Spot-style schedule would already be late. Two habits remove the risk entirely: file within days of treatment, while the invoice and the visit are easy to reconstruct, and write the actual deadline from the actual policy on the claim folder, not a remembered industry average. Unless the policy documents state a different payment arrangement for a specific visit, the safe assumption is the reimbursement flow above.

After submission: review, timing, and payment

Review is verification, not a second application. Per Spot, the team checks that the condition is covered, that treatment happened after waiting periods, and what deductible remains, then applies the reimbursement rate. Timing varies with completeness. ManyPets says it aims to process most accident and illness claims in less than 8 to 10 business days, while warning that cases needing more information from the vet take longer — and that a bank may then take up to ten further days before reimbursement funds become available. Plan cash flow on the slower end: the vet bill leaves the account on day one, and the reimbursement can legitimately land weeks later even on a clean claim.

Filing itself is not a penalty. ManyPets states in its claims FAQ that owners can submit as many claims as they like online with no impact on the monthly premium. That is one provider's stated position rather than an industry guarantee, but it underlines the step-4 advice above: small, eligible claims belong in the system, counting toward the deductible, not in a drawer.

If the claim is denied: the written appeal

A denial letter or explanation of benefits is a document to work from, not just a result. Read the stated reason and sort it into one of three kinds: a missing-document denial, which is fixed by supplying the record named; a timing denial, involving waiting periods or the filing deadline, which turns on dates that can be evidenced; and a coverage denial, involving an exclusion or a pre-existing classification, which turns on the medical record and the policy wording.

Spot's appeal route shows the standard shape: appeals are sent in writing — in Spot's case by email to its service address — must clearly state why the owner or the vet disagrees with the initial determination, and should include supporting documentation; Spot says appeals can take around 30 days to be reviewed and confirms receipt by email. Whatever the provider, an effective appeal does three things: quotes the denial reason it answers, attaches the specific record that answers it (a vet's clarified note is worth more than a longer letter), and goes in promptly, while clinic staff still remember the visit. For background on why claims are refused in the first place, see why pet insurance claims get denied.

Our take: Treat every vet visit as the start of a claim, not the claim form as the start. Get the itemized invoice and the clinic's records contact before leaving, file within days regardless of which deadline the policy allows, file small claims toward the deductible, and keep the confirmation and claim number together. That routine makes the deadline table above almost irrelevant — which is exactly the point of having it.
