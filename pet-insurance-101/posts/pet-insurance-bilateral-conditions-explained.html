How Pet Insurance Treats Bilateral Conditions (Both Sides of the Body)

Coverage · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-29 · Last updated 2026-10-29

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance bilateral-condition clauses typically work — not financial or insurance advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Definitions, look-back periods and exceptions vary by provider and province or state. Always read your own policy wording before relying on it.

Two knees do not make two separate chances. That is the misconception bilateral clauses correct, expensively, for owners who learn it at claim time. A bilateral condition is a medical issue that can affect both sides of the body, and these conditions typically affect both sides eventually (s1). Insurers therefore read the body as one unit for these conditions: the history of the left side is evidence about the right.

Two legs, one condition: how the either-side rule works

The comparison that matters is not between insurers' marketing pages. It is between the two ways a second-side problem can be classified:

SituationHow it is typically treatedSource
Condition first diagnosed after enrolment, waiting period over, no sign it manifested before coverage — even on the other sideDiagnostics and treatment may be covered(s1)
Condition first appeared on one side before coverage began; second side shows symptoms laterThe second side may be classified pre-existing and not covered(s1)
Cruciate problem on the same or the opposite leg in the 18 months before the policy effective dateConsidered pre-existing and not coverable under that insurer's stated rule(s2)
Both sides affected at different times under one policyMany insurers treat bilateral conditions as a single condition, so the cover limit is not doubled(s4)
A curable bilateral condition recurs years later, unrelated to the earlier incidentMay be covered if a vet clarifies the incidents are unrelated(s1)

The mechanism is not arbitrary. Take hip dysplasia starting on the right side: it can change how a pet distributes weight, increasing the likelihood of the condition on the opposite side — which is why an insurer may classify the later side as pre-existing even when its symptoms appear after coverage begins (s1). Agree or disagree, that is the logic written into the clause, and it is the logic a claim will be judged by.

The named list: wording to search for in a policy

Spec verification, in this case, means searching real policy wording rather than a comparison site's summary. One insurer's sample policy states the either-side rule as a named list: no cover for the listed conditions if the pet showed signs or evidence on either side of the body before the effective date. The list runs to glaucoma; keratoconjunctivitis sicca (KCS, or dry eye); lens luxation; entropion; ectropion; cataracts; prolapse of the tear gland of the third eyelid (cherry eye); elbow dysplasia; hip dysplasia; epiphyseal dysplasia; luxating patella; disease, weakness, instability, tear, partial tear or rupture of the cranial cruciate ligament; and intervertebral disc disease (s3).

Other insurers describe the same territory without a single list. The common examples across the sources checked are elbow and hip dysplasia, cruciate ligament injuries, cataracts, luxating patella and glaucoma (s1), with ear infections also named among bilateral examples in UK guidance (s4). The practical point is the search itself: find the policy's definition of a bilateral or related condition, and find whether a named list exists, before enrolment. After a diagnosis, the wording is no longer a shopping question.

When the second side can still be covered

The either-side rule has two genuine openings. First, timing: a condition diagnosed after enrolment, after the waiting period, with no evidence it manifested before coverage, may be covered even though it is bilateral in nature (s1). A clean record before enrolment is what keeps that opening open.

Second, the curable-and-unrelated exception. Certain bilateral conditions considered curable may be covered when the second incident is unrelated to the previous one — the example given is glaucoma arising years after it previously affected one side, covered if the vet is able to clarify that the incidents are unrelated (s1). Note what that exception requires: a veterinary clarification, in the record, that two episodes are unrelated. It is not an owner assertion, and it will not help conditions whose whole nature is a shared underlying process, such as dysplasia progressing from one hip to the other (s1).

A decision framework for a pet with any one-sided history follows from those two openings. If the condition is on a named bilateral list and the first side predates enrolment, assume the second side is excluded until the insurer says otherwise in writing. If the condition is curable and the episodes are genuinely separate, ask whether a vet clarification can be documented. If there is no history on either side, the bilateral clause is a reason to enrol now, while the record is clean — not a reason to delay.

The attributed cost case, read carefully

Costs in this area are often quoted without a source. Only one case is used here, because it is attributed: in an insurer's own published examples, a 7-year-old dog named Wendy was treated for a bilateral cruciate rupture, the vet bill came to almost $6,150, and the owners received over $5,500 back (s1). Read it for what it is — one policyholder case published by that insurer, in US dollars, under that policy's terms — not a typical price, not a Canadian price, and not a promise about any other policy. It shows the scale a bilateral claim can reach when both sides are treated; it does not price a surgery.

Pre-enrolment audit: check the record before the look-back does

  1. Request the full vet record, not a summary. Include every clinic visited. A pre-existing condition, under one Canadian insurer's definition, is a condition that first occurred or displayed signs before the coverage effective date (s2) — signs in any clinic's notes count.
  2. Search the record side by side. For knees, hips, elbows, eyes and ears, note every mention of lameness, stiffness, luxation, cloudiness or infection, and which side it names. One-sided notes are exactly what an either-side clause acts on (s3).
  3. Check the dates against the look-back. For cruciate cover under the rule cited here, a problem on either leg in the 18 months before the effective date is pre-existing (s2). Other conditions and insurers use their own windows — find each one in the wording.
  4. Ask the bilateral question by condition name. Put each listed condition found in the record to the insurer and ask, in writing, whether the opposite side will be covered. Keep the answer with the policy.
  5. If a curable episode is in the record, ask the vet about unrelatedness now. The exception depends on a vet being able to clarify that incidents are unrelated (s1). That clarification is far easier to document before a second episode than during a claim.

What success looks like: before paying a premium, you can name which of your pet's paired structures carry a history, which side it was on, and what the insurer has said — in writing — about the other side.
