Pet Insurance Exam Fees Explained: When the Vet Visit Fee Is Covered — and When It Is 100% Yours

Money mechanics · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-31 · Last updated 2026-10-31

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance exam fees typically work — not financial or insurance advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Coverage, exclusions and reimbursement vary by provider, pet and province. Always read your own policy wording and confirm the exam-fee rule in a current quote before buying.

A reimbursement rate only applies to charges the policy counts as eligible. That sounds obvious until the first sick visit: the bill is split into the examination fee — the charge for the veterinarian examining your pet and working out what is wrong — and the tests and treatment that follow. Some Canadian policies put the exam fee inside the eligible total. Others leave it outside entirely, which means the advertised 80% or 90% is 0% on that line, every visit, no matter how large the rest of the claim grows.

The trap: a fee outside the claim never reaches the deductible either

Where an exam fee is excluded, it does not merely miss reimbursement. It also does not count toward the deductible, because only eligible costs move that meter. Fetch's published reimbursement example shows the included side of the line: at an 80% rate, a $500 approved claim can pay up to $400 once the deductible has been reached. Move part of that $500 into an excluded exam fee and the approved claim shrinks first — the percentage is then applied to a smaller number. Two policies with the same rate, the same deductible and the same premium can therefore pay different amounts on the identical vet bill, and the entire difference can sit on this one line.

Tax treatment can travel with the fee. One Canadian review of Petsecure notes its reimbursement — up to 80%, after the deductible — includes taxes and exam fees. Trupanion's published exclusions list, by contrast, covers examination fees and sales tax where applicable. Check both lines, not just the fee.

Three fees that share one name

The sick-visit exam fee. This is the examination charge on an unexpected visit for an accident or illness. Fetch Canada puts this fee at around $50 to $250 per visit, in addition to tests and treatment, and states that it covers the complete sick visit, exam fee included.

The routine wellness exam. This is the scheduled annual examination. Fetch classifies annual exams with vaccinations, heartworm prevention and dental cleaning as preventive care, offered through an optional wellness add-on rather than the core accident-and-illness policy. A policy can cover the sick-visit fee and still pay nothing toward the annual check-up.

The accident-only exam fee. Spot's Canada guidance lists vet exam fees among the costs an accident-only plan can help cover — where the visit relates to an accident. The same plan pays nothing for an illness visit, fee included. The question is never just "are exam fees covered?" It is "for which kind of visit, under which part of the policy?"

Read the Trupanion row fairly. Excluding the fee is a design choice, not a defect: the policy concentrates its percentage on the larger treatment costs. Whether that trade suits a household depends on how often the pet is examined and how large the treatment bills are likely to be — which is what the arithmetic below is for.

At the top of the published range the picture sharpens: at $250 a visit, the same three-visit year puts $750 of fees outside the claim. At the bottom, $50 a visit puts $150 outside it. A pet seen once a year for minor problems barely feels the exclusion. A pet with a managed condition, seen for rechecks through the year, can pay more in excluded fees than the premium difference between two quotes — which is why this line belongs in the comparison before price, not after the first claim.

One limit on the included side deserves stating plainly. Inclusion does not mean the fee is free. Where the fee is eligible, it still passes through the deductible first and is then reimbursed at the policy's rate, so the owner keeps the remaining share. Inclusion changes the fee from 100% yours to partly shared; exclusion keeps it entirely yours.

Check a quote in five steps

  1. Find the exam-fee sentence. In the sample policy or quote summary, search for "exam" and "examination". Note whether the wording covers the fee, excludes it, or covers it only for certain visit types.
  2. Separate the three fees. Ask, in writing, how the policy treats a sick-visit fee, an annual wellness exam, and an accident-only visit fee. Three answers, not one.
  3. Ask whether an eligible fee counts toward the deductible. If it is excluded, confirm that it also sits outside the deductible — that is the usual consequence, and it changes the worked math.
  4. Check tax alongside the fee. One policy can include taxes in reimbursement while another excludes sales tax. On a large bill, that line is not small.
  5. Run your own visit count. Take the exam fee from your last invoice, multiply by the number of sick visits in your pet's last twelve months, and compare that annual figure with the premium gap between the quotes you are weighing.

What success looks like: you can state, for the policy you are about to buy, exactly what happens to the exam fee on a sick visit, a wellness visit and an accident visit — and you have an annual dollar figure for the excluded case built from your own invoice, not a brochure.

When paying the fee yourself is still the right trade

Skip the worry over this line if your pet is young, is typically seen only when something is plainly wrong, and the fee-excluding quote buys something you value more — a higher limit, a per-condition deductible structure, or a lower premium that exceeds your realistic annual fees. The worked example cuts both ways: at one $50 visit a year, the exclusion costs $50, and paying a much higher premium to insure a $50 line is poor arithmetic. Pay close attention instead when visits are frequent, when the pet is managed for an ongoing condition, or when the fee sits at the top of the range at an emergency or specialist clinic. In those households the exam fee is not a footnote to the claim. Repeated often enough, it is one of the largest costs the policy never touches.
