Pet Insurance Exclusions for Flat-Faced and Other High-Risk Breeds

Breeds · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-28 · Last updated 2026-10-28

Disclaimer: This is general information about how breed-related exclusions typically work — not financial advice and not a summary of any specific policy. Cost figures are attributed to their sources, with currencies as published. Always read your own policy wording before relying on it.

An exclusion pays $0. That is the direct answer, and it outranks every other number in a policy. A breed exclusion overrides the deductible, the reimbursement rate and the cap: the owner pays 100% of an excluded condition (s2). A generous 90% reimbursement rate on a policy that excludes your dog's breathing problems is 90% of nothing, for the condition your dog is most likely to need treated.

Teardown: what a denial actually costs

Start with the condition at the centre of the flat-faced debate. BOAS — brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome, the breathing condition tied to shortened skulls — costs affected pets $1,400–$2,900 per year in one insurer's figures (s3). Surgery sits above that annual range: BOAS surgery can cost up to US$5,000 (s1; price checked 2026-10-25 — a US-published figure, shown as published, not a Canadian price).

Now run an excluded claim through the teardown. Annual care at the top of the published range, $2,900, with an exclusion in force: deductible applied — irrelevant; reimbursement rate applied — irrelevant; annual cap checked — irrelevant. The exclusion is applied first and ends the calculation, and the owner pays the full amount (s2). Owners of the highest-risk breeds face this arithmetic more often than most: Pugs and French Bulldogs are 8–10 times as likely to claim for BOAS, according to one insurer's 2022–2024 claims data (s3).

The clause to search for: conformational defect

The denial mechanism has a name worth memorising before any quote comparison: the conformational-defect exclusion, named as the clause to watch for in French Bulldog policies (s4). Wording of this kind treats a condition arising from the breed's built shape — the shortened airway a flat-faced dog is born with — as a defect of conformation rather than an insurable illness.

That is why breed lists alone mislead. A policy can welcome French Bulldogs, quote a premium for one, and still exclude the respiratory conditions the breed is known for, through a clause that never mentions the breed by name. The search to run on any specimen policy is not the breed name. It is the exclusion wording: conformational, hereditary respiratory, brachycephalic, breed-specific. One insurer, for contrast, states that it covers brachycephalic conditions if they are not pre-existing (s3) — proof that cover exists, and that the clause, not the breed, is the variable.

Waiting periods that stretch to a year

Standard timing first: the typical illness waiting period is 14 days (s1). For brachycephalic respiratory conditions, some carriers impose waiting periods of 6–12 months instead (s4). A puppy enrolled at eight weeks can therefore be most of the way through its first year before breathing cover — if it exists in the policy at all — actually begins.

The longer wait interacts with the symptom rule, and the interaction is the trap. A symptom, not a diagnosis, before enrolment makes BOAS pre-existing (s1). Flat-faced breeds can show noisy, laboured breathing early, as a normal-looking feature of the breed. If that breathing is noted at a vet visit before enrolment, or inside a long waiting period, the condition can be classified pre-existing before the cover ever had a chance to attach. The breeds named at highest risk are English and French Bulldogs, Pugs, Boston Terriers, Shih Tzus, Boxers and Persian cats (s1) — for those animals, enrolment timing is not admin. It is the claim.

Questions owners of high-risk breeds ask

Does pet insurance cover BOAS surgery? It can — at least one insurer covers brachycephalic conditions that are not pre-existing (s3) — but a conformational-defect or respiratory exclusion in the wording reduces the payout to $0 regardless of the plan's other numbers (s2, s4). The specimen policy answers the question; the marketing page does not.

Are French Bulldogs excluded from pet insurance? The sources do not describe a blanket breed ban. They describe exclusion clauses aimed at the breed's characteristic conditions (s4), and claims data showing French Bulldogs claim for BOAS at 8–10 times the usual rate alongside Pugs (s3). Being insurable and being covered for the likely claim are different things.

What is a conformational defect exclusion? An exclusion aimed at conditions arising from an animal's bred structure, named in the sources as the denial mechanism to search French Bulldog policy wording for (s4).

Beyond flat faces: hips, size and the mixed-breed surprise

Flat faces get the attention, but the same clause-hunting applies elsewhere. Exclusion patterns include hip dysplasia in Shepherds, Labs, Goldens, Rottweilers and Danes (s2). Large-breed owners should also check for a cruder instrument: some insurers exclude all dogs over 90 lb by size (s2).

That size threshold is the mixed-breed surprise. A rescue cross with no pedigree and no breed label can still be caught by a weight rule that no breed list mentions. Breed-by-breed research, however careful, misses an exclusion written in pounds.

Questions to put to an insurer in writing

  1. Does this policy contain a conformational-defect, brachycephalic or breed-specific respiratory exclusion? Ask for the clause number, not a verbal summary — it is the named denial mechanism for these breeds (s4).
  2. What waiting period applies to respiratory conditions for this breed? Compare the answer against the typical 14-day illness wait and the 6–12 month respiratory waits some carriers impose (s1, s4).
  3. Has anything in my pet's vet record already made breathing a pre-existing condition? A symptom before enrolment is enough under the sources' rule, with no diagnosis required (s1).
  4. For large or mixed-breed dogs: is there a weight-based exclusion? Name the threshold — some insurers exclude all dogs over 90 lb (s2).
  5. If the likely condition is excluded, what is left? Price the policy on the conditions that remain covered. An excluded BOAS claim leaves the owner paying 100%, up to US$5,000 for surgery on the published figure (s1, s2).

What success looks like: you hold the exclusion clauses in writing, the respiratory waiting period for your breed in writing, and a clear answer on whether any recorded symptom has already closed the door — before the first premium is paid.
