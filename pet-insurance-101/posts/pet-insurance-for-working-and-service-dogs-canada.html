Pet Insurance for Working, Service, and Therapy Dogs in Canada

Working dogs · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-29 · Last updated 2026-10-29

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance and related rules typically work in Canada — not financial, insurance, legal or tax advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Exclusions vary by provider and province. Check your own policy wording, and for tax questions check the Canada Revenue Agency's current guidance or a qualified tax professional.

A working dog can be insured. The direct answer is also the trap: there is no single product called working-dog insurance, and buying the wrong one of three different products leaves the actual risk uncovered. A pet health policy pays the dog's vet bills. Liability cover responds when other people or property are involved. A tax credit, for eligible service animals, returns part of qualifying costs at filing time. Handlers who conflate them end up with a policy that was never designed to pay the bill in front of them.

Three products, three different jobs

One boundary is absolute. Human health insurance does not cover veterinary care for a service dog, because insurers classify animals as personal property rather than medical equipment (s5). However essential the dog is to the handler's daily life, the vet bill belongs to the pet side of the ledger — a pet health policy, an eligible tax claim, or out of pocket.

What a pet health policy does for a working dog

For the dog's own health, the standard Canadian product is accident and illness cover. It can include emergency care, hospital stays, diagnostic tests, surgeries, medications and specialized care for accidents and illnesses, generally on a reimbursement model where the owner pays the veterinarian first and submits a claim (s1). Premiums are influenced by the dog's age, breed, location and the coverage level chosen (s1).

The standard limits apply with full force to working dogs. Pre-existing conditions and elective procedures, including spaying and neutering, are commonly excluded, and waiting periods apply — conditions arising during a waiting period may be treated as pre-existing (s1). For a dog whose work is physical, that combination argues for enrolling early in the dog's career, before a working injury or a repetitive-strain note exists in the record to be judged against a start date.

The working-dog exclusion: read it against the actual job

Here is the fine print where a working-dog claim goes to die. One major insurer's exclusions page lists as not covered any illness or injury that arises from racing, coursing, commercial guarding, law enforcement, or organized fighting — alongside pre-existing conditions, obedience training, grooming, breeding and elective or cosmetic procedures (s2).

Read that list against the dog in front of you. A police or commercial guard dog sits squarely inside the named activities. A personally owned service dog assisting its own handler — navigating public areas, alerting to sounds, opening doors and performing similar tasks (s4) — is not named in that list, but the wording was not written with service dogs in mind either, and policies differ. A therapy dog visiting a care home sits in a third position again: the visit work is neither guarding nor racing, yet it is organized activity with the public, which is precisely why the liability product in the table above exists separately.

The honest conclusion is narrow: the exclusion turns on the activity that caused the injury and on the exact wording of the policy bought. No general guide can settle it for a specific dog. The procedure at the end of this article — a written answer from the insurer, naming the dog's role, before the premium is paid — is how it gets settled.

Canada: the tax credit and certification are separate from insurance

Two Canadian systems run alongside insurance and are often confused with it. First, tax. The medical expense tax credit provides relief for certain expenses for an animal specially trained to assist a person coping with blindness, profound deafness, severe autism, severe diabetes, severe epilepsy, or a severe and prolonged impairment that markedly restricts the use of the arms or legs — provided the animal was supplied by a person or organization one of whose main purposes is that training (s3). Eligible expenses include the cost of the animal, its care and maintenance including food and veterinary care, reasonable travel to a facility that trains patients in handling such animals, and reasonable board and lodging for full-time attendance there (s3). Expenses for an animal specially trained to perform specific tasks for a person with a severe mental impairment — a psychiatric service dog is the given example — were proposed to be included for expenses after 2017, and providing comfort or emotional support alone is not a qualifying task (s3). The Canada Revenue Agency may ask for a signed statement from a medical practitioner and for receipts, which are kept rather than sent with the return (s3). Therapy dogs, whose role is comfort for other people, do not meet that specially-trained-task test on the wording given.

Second, certification — which governs public access, not claims. In British Columbia, the Guide Dog and Service Dog Act governs certification of guide and service dog teams, by two routes: receiving a trained dog from an accredited school, or passing a public safety test (s4). Therapy dogs are not covered under that Act and are not permitted in all public places (s4). Other provinces run their own rules. A certificate can establish a team's access rights; it does not change what a pet health policy will pay, and a policy does not confer access rights. Keep the two files separate.

Before you pay: the written-confirmation procedure

  1. Name the dog's actual job in one sentence. Service tasks for its own handler, therapy visits to facilities, or commercial work such as guarding or law enforcement. The exclusion list is activity-based (s2), so the sentence has to be activity-accurate.
  2. Ask the insurer two questions, in writing. Is an injury arising during that named activity covered? Is the dog itself eligible to enrol while doing that work? Keep the reply with the policy documents.
  3. Check the training line. Obedience training is excluded in the wording cited here (s2), and behaviour training appears only inside an optional preventive-care add-on list — so do not budget a base policy as a training fund.
  4. Sort the tax file separately. If the dog is a specially trained service animal for a listed impairment, keep receipts for its cost, food, veterinary care and any training-facility travel, plus the medical practitioner's statement the CRA may request (s3).
  5. Buy liability separately if the public is involved. Therapy visits and any paid work with other people's animals or property are a liability question, not a pet health question — the table at the top of this page is the sorting test.

What success looks like: you hold three separate answers — a pet health policy whose insurer has confirmed the dog's role in writing, a liability position you have chosen deliberately rather than assumed, and a receipt file that would support a tax claim if the dog qualifies.
