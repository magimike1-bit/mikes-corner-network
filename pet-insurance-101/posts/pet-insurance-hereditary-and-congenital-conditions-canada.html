Pet Insurance for Hereditary and Congenital Conditions in Canada: The Signs-Before-Enrolment Rule

Coverage · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-31 · Last updated 2026-10-31

Disclaimer: This is general information about how pet insurance hereditary and congenital coverage typically works — not financial or insurance advice, and not a summary of any specific policy. Definitions, waiting periods and exclusions vary by provider and province. Always read your own policy wording and get answers on named conditions in writing before enrolling.

Yes, pet insurance in Canada can cover hereditary and congenital conditions — and the deciding fact is usually not the condition's name. It is timing. A condition in either category is generally covered only if the pet showed no signs or symptoms of it before enrolment and none during the waiting period. A condition the pet was born with can still be covered if it had never shown itself; a condition diagnosed years later can still be excluded if an early sign sits in the vet record from before the policy started. Two definitions and one date rule do almost all of the work.

Two words that sound alike and behave differently

A hereditary condition is genetically inherited. Fetch Canada describes it as an issue a pet is predisposed to at birth, often linked to breed, which can show up at any point during the pet's lifetime — or not at all. French Bulldogs being genetically more prone to breathing problems is its example. The predisposition is present from the start; the illness may arrive at age two, age eight, or never.

A congenital condition is an abnormality the pet is born with, caused by a developmental disruption during pregnancy — Fetch lists a drug, a virus or another environmental factor. The range runs from an obvious cleft palate to a heart murmur that may go undetected for some time. MetLife's explanation adds the overlap honestly: congenital conditions may or may not be hereditary, because some are inherited and some arise in the womb for other reasons.

The practical difference is detection, not importance. A hereditary risk can be named in advance from the breed alone, with a completely clean record. A congenital condition is already physically present at enrolment, even when nobody has found it yet — so the entire question becomes whether anything in the record shows it had begun to surface.

The signs-before-enrolment rule, stated carefully

Fetch's congenital wording is the clearest version: if a pet is enrolled before showing signs or symptoms of a congenital condition, treatment can be covered even though the pet was born with the condition. If the pet was already showing signs before enrolment, the condition is pre-existing and is not covered. MetLife's version draws the second boundary: a pet not exhibiting symptoms before enrolling or during its stated 14-day waiting period can be covered for necessary treatment; symptoms inside that window make the condition likely pre-existing.

Notice what neither version requires: a diagnosis. A note in the record — a murmur heard at a puppy check, a gait comment, a "monitor" flag — can be a sign, even where no condition was named and no treatment was given. That is why enrolment timing interacts with the first vet visit. A thorough first examination is good medicine and should never be delayed for insurance reasons; it simply means the record created at that visit is the record the insurer will later read. Enrolling before the first signs are documented, where a family has already decided to insure, preserves options that a later enrolment cannot restore.

One related rule can surprise owners of larger dogs. Under a bilateral rule — covered in a separate guide on this site — a problem documented on one side of the body can affect cover for the matching part on the other side. Petsecure's reviewed material applies that logic to pre-existing conditions: an issue on one side can exclude the opposite side. For joint conditions with a hereditary component, ask about both legs, not just the sore one.

Breed examples: a prompt for questions, not a prediction

Fetch lists hip dysplasia — the pelvis and femur developing abnormally so the joint fits loosely — as more likely in German Shepherds, Golden Retrievers and St. Bernards; cherry eye in Beagles, French Bulldogs and Pugs; and, in cats, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy as more common in Maine Coons and Ragdolls, alongside congenital examples such as cerebellar hypoplasia and hernia. Its covered list also names intervertebral disc disease and progressive retinal atrophy.

Treat any such list as a shopping list for questions, nothing more. A breed on a list does not mean an individual pet will develop the condition — Fetch's own definition notes a hereditary condition may never show up at all. And a breed absent from a marketing list is not cleared; mixed-breed pets can inherit risks from their parent breeds. The list earns its place when it sends an owner to the sample policy to check whether that specific condition is named, capped, or given a longer wait.

Verify a named condition before you enrol: five steps

  1. Name your pet's top two or three breed risks. Use the breed examples in provider material and your veterinarian's knowledge of the individual pet. Write condition names, not categories — "hip dysplasia", not "joint problems".
  2. Search the sample policy for each name. Note for each one: covered, excluded, sub-limited, or subject to a longer waiting period. The Petsecure pattern — 6 months for cruciate and IVDD against 14 days for general illness — shows why a single "waiting period" answer is not enough.
  3. Read the full vet record once, as an insurer would. Look for signs, symptoms and "monitor" notes, not only diagnoses. Anything recorded before enrolment is what the signs rule will be applied to.
  4. Ask the bilateral question. For paired parts — hips, knees, eyes, elbows — ask in writing whether a sign on one side affects the other.
  5. Get the answers in writing before paying. Ask specifically whether each named condition will be treated as pre-existing for your pet, given the record as it stands today. Keep the reply with the policy.

What success looks like: for each named condition, you hold a written line stating covered or excluded, the waiting period that applies to it, and whether the existing vet record changes that answer. If a provider will only answer in general terms, that is information too — price it as uncertainty.

Who should move quickly, and who should read twice

Enrol early, after the check, if the pet is young, the record is clean, and the breed carries named hereditary risks. Early enrolment is the one move that keeps both categories open; no later shopping restores a sign that has already been recorded. Read twice before paying if the record already contains a murmur, a gait note, or a monitored symptom: the premium may still be worth paying for everything else the policy covers, but it should not be paid in the expectation that the named condition is among them. Ask about the curable route if the condition has been treated and is now symptom-free — at least two providers publish a review or return-to-cover path with a symptom-free period, and the exclusions on that path, such as knee and ligament conditions, are exactly the detail to confirm in writing.
