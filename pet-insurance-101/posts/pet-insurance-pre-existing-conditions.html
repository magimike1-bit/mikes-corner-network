Pet Insurance and Pre-Existing Conditions: Curable, Chronic, and Bilateral, Explained

Coverage · 9 min read

"Pre-existing conditions aren't covered" is the sentence everyone knows about pet insurance. It is true, and it is incomplete in ways that cost owners money in both directions: some owners never buy a policy that would have covered a cured condition's recurrence, and others buy one assuming a chronic condition will somehow be included. The working definition is broader than most people expect: a pre-existing condition is any illness, injury, or symptom that existed before the policy started or during its waiting periods — a formal diagnosis is not required (ConsumerAffairs; GoodRx for Pets). A limp noted in a vet record can matter as much as a named disease.

The two buckets: curable and incurable

Many insurers sort pre-existing conditions into two categories, and the category decides whether exclusion is temporary or permanent (Nasdaq / Motley Fool Money; ConsumerAffairs).

The free-period length is not standardised — sources in this packet alone cite 180 days, six months, and twelve months from different insurers, and the definition of "curable" itself varies by company. A condition one insurer treats as curable can be a permanent exclusion at another. That variability is precisely why the sample policy's definitions section, not a comparison site's summary, is the document that decides your case.

The bilateral trap

The least-known exclusion in the category is the bilateral one. A bilateral condition affects paired body parts — hips, knees, eyes, ears. Because a problem on one side predicts a problem on the other, many policies exclude the second side if the first side was treated or showed symptoms before coverage began (Nasdaq / Motley Fool Money). The concrete version: your dog tore a cruciate ligament in the left knee two years before you enrolled. The right knee tears next year, in an otherwise covered accident-and-illness policy. At an insurer with a bilateral exclusion, that second knee can be denied as pre-existing — even though it had never been injured. Hip dysplasia is the other common case. If your pet has any history on one side of a paired structure, the bilateral clause is the first thing to check in any policy you consider, and a reason the "curable" reset often explicitly carves out knee and ligament conditions, as ASPCA's does.

Symptoms count, records decide

Two practical consequences follow from the symptom-based definition. First, timing of vet visits around enrollment matters: a problem that appears during a waiting period is treated as pre-existing even if it was genuinely new, because the policy cannot distinguish new from concealed — that is what waiting periods are for. Second, insurers decide claims from the medical record. At claim time the insurer reviews the veterinary history, and what the record documents — dates, symptoms, side of the body — is what the exclusion analysis runs on. Owners who understand this do two things: they keep their own copy of the pet's records, and they read any exclusion letter against the record to check the insurer matched the right condition, date, and side. Errors happen, and an exclusion applied to the wrong condition is appealable with the record in hand.

A pre-purchase audit you can do in one evening

  1. List everything documented. Request your pet's full record from every clinic used. Write down each diagnosed condition, each recorded symptom, and each side-specific note (left/right knee, hip scores).
  2. Sort each item into a bucket. Curable-resolved, curable-recent, or chronic — using the definitions above. Be pessimistic; the insurer will be.
  3. Ask each insurer in writing. For your two or three shortlisted providers, ask how each listed item would be treated, and whether their curable reset and bilateral clauses apply to it. Keep the answers.
  4. Price what remains. A policy that excludes a chronic condition still covers everything else — accidents, cancer (if not the excluded item), and new illnesses. Judge the premium against the coverage that survives your list, not against an imaginary full-coverage policy.
  5. Calendar the resets. For curable items, note the date each becomes coverable again under your chosen policy's free-period rule, so a recurrence claim is filed with the timeline already documented.

What success looks like: before paying a premium, you hold a written list of what is excluded permanently, what is excluded until a specific date, and what is covered from day one after waiting periods. No surprises are left for claim time.

Worked example: reading one pet's record against the rules

Make the categories concrete with a composite example built from the rule types above. A five-year-old cat's record shows: a urinary tract infection treated fourteen months ago with no recurrence since; mild dental disease noted at the last two check-ups; and a one-line note of a limp in the right hind leg, never diagnosed, eight months ago. Sorted against the framework: the urinary infection is the classic curable case — at an insurer with a 180-day symptom-free rule it has long since stopped being pre-existing, while at a 12-month-rule insurer it has also now cleared; check which clock your shortlisted policy uses. The dental disease is chronic and documented at consecutive visits: expect it excluded as pre-existing, permanently. The limp is the dangerous line — undiagnosed, hind-leg, recorded. If that leg later develops a cruciate or joint problem, an insurer can argue the symptom predates coverage, and a bilateral clause could extend the argument to the other leg. The pre-purchase audit exists to surface exactly this line while you can still ask insurers, in writing, how they would treat it — rather than discovering their answer attached to a denied claim.

FAQ

Can any insurance cover a chronic pre-existing condition? Mainstream accident-and-illness policies generally do not. Accident-only policies still cover new injuries, and some wellness products reimburse routine care regardless — partial tools, not substitutes.

Does switching insurers reset everything? Yes, in the bad direction: conditions covered at your old insurer become pre-existing at the new one. Continuous coverage has real value once a condition exists.

My pet's condition resolved years ago. Is it still pre-existing? For a curable condition, possibly not — depending on the insurer's free-period rule and whether the record shows a clean interval. For knee, ligament, and chronic conditions, usually yes.

Our take: "Pre-existing" is three different rules wearing one label: a temporary exclusion with a reset clock for curable problems, a permanent exclusion for chronic ones, and a bilateral clause that can reach the healthy side of the body. The pet's record plus the policy's definitions section tell you which rule applies to your pet — get both before you price anything.
