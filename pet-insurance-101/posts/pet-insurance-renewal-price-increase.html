Your pet insurance renewal jumped: what to check before you pay or switch

Renewals · 10 min read

A renewal notice that jumps 20, 30 or even 50 percent feels like a penalty — especially in a year when you barely claimed. It usually is not a penalty in that simple sense. Premiums typically rise about 10 to 25 percent a year as a pet ages, with the largest jumps arriving around senior status at roughly age seven, and the drivers are a mix of your pet's age, veterinary-cost inflation across the insurer's whole pool, conditions in your area and, for some insurers, your own claims history (MoneyGeek). Understanding which of those levers moved your price is the difference between overpaying quietly and making one of several cheaper moves that keep the coverage that matters. This guide works through the renewal letter line by line, then through the options in the order most owners should try them.

Read the renewal notice like an auditor, not a bill

Start with three comparisons against last year's declarations page — the summary that lists your deductible, reimbursement percentage, limit and premium. First, the percentage increase itself: a 12 percent rise on a middle-aged pet with no claims is a different story from a 60 percent rise, and you cannot judge either until it is a number rather than a feeling. Second, check whether discounts you previously had are still applied; a quietly dropped multi-pet or loyalty discount can look exactly like a rate increase. Third, check whether the coverage terms changed alongside the price — a lower annual limit or a new co-payment percentage sometimes travels with the new premium, which means you are not comparing the same product at two prices (LegalClarity, renewal review guidance). If the increase still looks larger than age and inflation explain, call the insurer and ask for the breakdown in writing: how much is age, how much is pool-wide veterinary inflation, and how much, if any, is your claims history. An insurer that cannot explain its own increase has told you something useful.

Why switching is more expensive than it looks

The obvious response to a price jump is to shop around — and for a young, healthy pet with a clean record, that can be right. For most pets who have been insured a while, switching carries a hidden cost that quote pages do not show: anything your pet has been treated for, and in many insurers' reading anything that has merely shown symptoms, becomes a pre-existing condition at the new insurer and is excluded. A new policy also restarts waiting periods. The unbroken run of cover is often the asset most worth protecting, particularly once a pet has an ongoing condition, because even a short break can mean starting again with that condition left out (WSFP renewal guidance). None of this means paying whatever the renewal says. It means the first negotiations happen with the insurer you already have, where your pet's history is an asset instead of an exclusion list.

Work the levers one at a time and get a quote for each change separately. Bundling two changes at once makes the renewal impossible to compare next year, and the whole point of this exercise is a policy you can still audit in twelve months.

A decision framework for renewal week

  1. Price the increase honestly. New premium minus old premium, times twelve. A $15 monthly rise is $180 a year — real money, but compare it with the cost of one excluded chronic condition before treating it as outrageous.
  2. List what your current policy covers that a new one would exclude. Every diagnosis, symptom and vet note from the policy's life goes on this list. If the list is empty and your pet is young, shopping around is a genuine option. If it is not empty, your current insurer is mostly competing with itself.
  3. Pull one lever, not three. Raise the deductible or trim the reimbursement rate, sized to bring the premium back inside your budget while keeping the annual limit intact — the limit is what stands between you and the one bill that actually matters.
  4. Check your cash position before raising the deductible. A $1,000 deductible only saves premium if $1,000 is genuinely available on a bad Saturday night. If it is not, the cheaper premium is borrowed risk, not savings.
  5. Diarise the next renewal now. Review the notice 30 to 60 days before the renewal date, not the week it lands — deductible changes at some insurers need notice, and every option in this guide is easier with a month in hand (MoneyGeek).

If you do let it lapse

Understand exactly what ends. Coverage stops, and any condition your pet develops while uninsured becomes pre-existing if you later buy a new policy; the covered history you built — the arthritis, the diabetes, the heart condition the current policy pays toward — is gone with it (MoneyGeek). The main alternative for a pet whose premiums have climbed past the value of the remaining cover is a dedicated savings account: the same monthly amount, kept by you, with no exclusions and no claims process. Its weakness is timing — it cannot fund a major emergency in its first year the way insurance can — so it suits owners who have already built a balance or whose pet's insurable conditions are mostly excluded anyway (LegalClarity). What success looks like at the end of renewal week: a premium you chose on purpose, a declarations page you have actually read, a written note of anything the insurer promised about existing conditions, and a calendar reminder for next year. That is a better outcome than either blind renewal or a hasty switch — and it takes about an hour.

FAQ

Does claiming make my premium go up? It depends on the insurer. Age and pool-wide veterinary inflation are the documented main drivers; some insurers also factor in individual claims history. That is precisely why the written breakdown request matters — it replaces guessing with your insurer's own answer.

Can I change my deductible mid-year? Typically changes happen at renewal, and some insurers require notice ahead of the renewal date, so ask before the deadline rather than after it (MoneyGeek).

My pet is now a senior. Is renewal still worth it? Often yes, and for a specific reason: a new illness in an older pet is frequently the kind that needs treatment for the rest of its life, so a policy that keeps paying for an ongoing condition can be worth more at nine than it was at three — provided the premium still fits and the limit has not been quietly cut.

Our take: Treat the renewal as a negotiation with paperwork, not a verdict. Audit the notice, protect the unbroken cover, pull one lever at a time, and only shop around with your eyes open about what a new policy will exclude.
