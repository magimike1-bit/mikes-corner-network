Price lock and grandfathering

Definition: Grandfathering means existing customers keep their old price when rates rise; a price lock is a contractual guarantee the price will not change for a set period.

Why it matters when comparing prices

Introductory discounts expire, so the price that matters is the renewal price — get it in writing before you sign. Ask whether the vendor has a history of grandfathering existing customers through increases, and treat a first-year quote as a sales tool, not a promise.

What to watch for in Canadian pricing

Canadian buyers should check which currency the lock is denominated in. A price lock quoted in USD protects you from the vendor's price increases but not from the exchange rate — if the loonie weakens 8%, your CAD cost rises 8% while the "locked" USD price sits perfectly still. The lock worth asking for is a CAD-denominated price for the contract term; get the currency named in the clause, not just the number.

Grandfathering is a pattern, not a promise, so ask for the vendor's actual history: how many price increases in the last three years, and were existing customers grandfathered through each one? Also ask what ends grandfathering — adding seats, changing tiers, or switching from monthly to annual billing can silently reprice you to current rates. If the vendor will not put the renewal price in writing, treat the year-one quote as a sales tool and model year two at the current list price plus a margin.

A concrete example

Illustrative arithmetic, not a real vendor quote: say a vendor locks a plan at CAD 49 a month for two years. In month 25 the lock expires and the plan reprices to CAD 65 a month — a 33% jump. Year three costs CAD 780 instead of CAD 588, a CAD 192 increase on the identical product that the buyer never approved. Anyone who only compared year-one totals never saw it coming.
