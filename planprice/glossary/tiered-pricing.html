Tiered pricing

Definition: Plans stacked in levels — Starter, Pro, Enterprise — where each tier unlocks more features, higher limits, or better support. Most B2B software is sold this way.

Why it matters when comparing prices

The middle tier is engineered to look like the sensible choice, so ignore the framing and map your must-have features to tiers directly. The expensive mistake is buying the top tier for one feature — check whether that feature is available as an add-on, or whether the tier below plus a workaround covers you.

What to watch for in Canadian pricing

Tier names and prices do not always travel well across the border. Some vendors show Canadian visitors USD pricing by default — check the currency code next to the price, because a bare $ sign is not enough — and the feature set can differ by market: data residency, CAD payment methods, or Canadian tax features sometimes sit only in the top tier. Ask for the Canadian tier sheet in CAD and check which tier holds the Canada-specific features you need, not just the US-marketed headline features.

The expensive mistake on tiered plans is buying a whole tier for one feature. Before you jump, ask whether that feature is sold as an add-on, whether the tier below plus an integration covers it, and whether the vendor will discount the higher tier on an annual CAD commitment. Also ask what happens at renewal: vendors move features between tiers, and a feature you bought the top tier for can slide down to a cheaper tier — or up out of your reach — at the next price change.

A concrete example

Illustrative arithmetic, not a real vendor quote: say a 12-person team needs an audit-log feature available only on the top tier at CAD 60 per seat per month, versus CAD 30 on the middle tier. The jump costs 12 × 30 = CAD 360 a month, or CAD 4,320 a year, for one feature. If the vendor sells audit logs as a CAD 100-a-month add-on to the middle tier, that is CAD 1,200 a year — a CAD 3,120 saving for the same capability.
