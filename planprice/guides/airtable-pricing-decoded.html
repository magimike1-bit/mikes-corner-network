Airtable Pricing Decoded: When the Expensive Tier Is the Cheaper Bill

Published 2026-10-15 · Last updated 2026-10-15

Airtable's pricing page lists two paid rates, and neither of them is the number that decides what a team pays. The deciding number is who counts as a billable collaborator — and, unusually, that definition changes between the two paid tiers, so a team with many commenters can pay less on the expensive tier than on the cheap one. Layer on per-base record limits that force upgrades independently of headcount, plus a portals add-on priced per block of seats, and Airtable becomes a pricing model that has to be modelled, not read. This guide does that modelling for Canadian teams, with prices in US dollars as Airtable publishes them, checked on 2026-10-15.

The four tiers: records are the second price list

Free costs US$0, with unlimited bases capped at 1,000 records per base, 1 GB of attachments per base, 100 automation runs a month, and up to five editors. Team costs US$20 per user per month billed annually, or US$24 billed monthly, raising the caps to 50,000 records per base, 20 GB of attachments and 25,000 automation runs. Business costs US$45 per user per month billed annually, or US$54 billed monthly, raising them further to 125,000 records per base, 100 GB of attachments and 100,000 automation runs, and adding premium sync, an admin panel and SAML single sign-on. Enterprise Scale is quote-only, at 500,000 records per base. Every plan also carries a monthly AI credit allowance per paid user — 500 credits per eligible editor on Free, 15,000 on Team, 20,000 on Business and 25,000 on Enterprise Scale — pooled across the workspace, with additional packs available on paid plans.

Read those record caps as a second price list. A team can have exactly the right number of seats and still be forced upward because a single base outgrows 50,000 records — three years of tasks, entries and history in one base will do it. Splitting bases to stay under a cap breaks the cross-base reporting that justified Airtable in the first place, so the honest budget assumes the tier the largest base needs, not the tier the feature list suggests. Inventory the biggest bases before pricing anything; record growth, not headcount, is the usual upgrade trigger here.

Who counts as a seat: the rule that flips between tiers

Airtable charges per collaborator with edit-level access, and read-only collaborators, form submissions and share links are free on every plan. That much is simple. The complication is the commenter. On the Team plan, Creator, Editor and Commenter roles are all billable. On the Business plan, only Owner, Creator and Editor roles are billed — commenters stop counting. Work the example that follows from those rules: four people who edit and six who only comment, on annual billing. On Team, all ten are seats: 10 × US$20 = US$200 a month. On Business, only the four editors are seats: 4 × US$45 = US$180 a month — less money, for the higher tier, with larger record caps included. If those six people only need to read rather than comment, they are free on both plans and Team costs 4 × US$20 = US$80 a month. The same team, the same product, three defensible bills ranging from US$80 to US$200 a month depending entirely on a role definition. No per-seat comparison table captures this; the team's actual role mix has to be counted person by person before any quote means anything.

The monthly-billing penalty and the portals add-on

Monthly billing costs 20% more than annual on both paid tiers — US$24 against US$20 on Team, US$54 against US$45 on Business. For a 10-editor Business team that is US$90 a month, or US$1,080 a year, paid purely for flexibility. Monthly billing still has one legitimate use: a short evaluation at real scale, with real bases, before committing a year at the higher tier. Beyond a month or two it is simply the most expensive way to buy the same product.

The other line to price separately is Airtable Portals, an add-on for giving external users a structured view into a base. It starts at US$120 per month for 15 portal seats on Team and US$150 per month for 15 portal seats on Business, with custom pricing at Enterprise Scale. A team whose use case is client-facing — an agency sharing project data, a firm giving customers a window into their records — should treat Portals as part of the base price of the solution, not an optional extra discovered later. Fifteen external seats at US$120 a month can exceed the cost of the internal Team seats it sits beside, which is precisely why add-ons belong in the first budget draft, not the renewal surprise list.

Year-one math and the comparison that matters

For a 10-editor team on annual billing, Team costs 10 × US$20 × 12 = US$2,400 a year and Business costs 10 × US$45 × 12 = US$5,400 a year. For a 30-person agency where everyone edits, Team is US$7,200 a year and Business is US$16,200 a year. Those are database-platform prices, and they should be compared against what Airtable replaces: if one Airtable workspace genuinely displaces a separate CRM, a project tracker and an inventory tool, the consolidated bill can be the cheapest of the four. If it displaces only a spreadsheet, it is an expensive spreadsheet. The commenter-heavy pattern cuts the other way and is Airtable's best-kept pricing advantage: broad read-only and comment access for clients costs little or nothing, where per-seat project tools charge for every participant. Count editors, commenters and readers separately, price the role mix on both paid tiers, and add Portals if external access is part of the plan — that three-step count is the whole Airtable pricing decision.

The decision framework

This is a buying-decision guide, not a setup procedure — the framework below is the process.

  1. Count roles, not people. Editors, commenters and readers price differently, and commenters are billable on Team but not on Business.
  2. Price both paid tiers for a commenter-heavy team. Business can be the cheaper bill despite costing more than double per seat.
  3. Size the largest base before choosing a tier. Record caps — 50,000 on Team, 125,000 on Business — force upgrades independently of features.
  4. Add Portals to the first budget if clients need access. US$120–150 per month per 15 seats is core cost for client-facing use, not an extra.
  5. Use monthly billing only as a paid evaluation. At a 20% premium, it stops making sense after a month or two.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Airtable cost per user?

Team is US$20 per user per month billed annually (US$24 monthly) and Business is US$45 annually (US$54 monthly). Enterprise Scale is quote-only. Read-only collaborators are free on every plan.

Do commenters cost money on Airtable?

On Team, yes — Commenter is a billable role. On Business, commenters are not billed; only Owner, Creator and Editor roles count. A commenter-heavy team can therefore pay less in total on Business than on Team.

What forces an Airtable upgrade?

Most often the per-base record cap: 1,000 records on Free, 50,000 on Team and 125,000 on Business. A growing base triggers the upgrade even when the feature set and headcount have not changed.
