Should You Accept Amex? The Acceptance-Cost Math for Small Tickets (Canada, 2026)

Published 2026-11-03 · Last updated 2026-11-03

The common belief is that accepting American Express always costs a small business more. On a flat rate plan in Canada, it costs exactly the same as Visa or Mastercard. On interchange plus pricing, it usually costs more, and the size of that gap, not the principle, is what should decide the question. Both cases are worked below with rates checked November 3, 2026.

Square Canada's point of sale pricing page states the flat rate plainly: every major credit card, including American Express, at 2.5% per tap or insert transaction, with Interac debit at 0.75% plus 7 cents. If your business is on Square, the acceptance question is already answered. Refusing Amex there saves nothing.

Where the Amex premium actually lives

On interchange plus pricing, the processor passes through the card network's own cost and adds a markup, so brand differences reach your statement. Helcim publishes the Amex OptBlue wholesale discount rates for Canada: 1.60% for retail and services tickets up to $500, 2.00% above $500, and 2.40% for restaurant tickets over $200. On top of wholesale, Amex charges a network fee of 0.1356% on all transactions, and Helcim's acquiring bank adds 0.03% plus 3 cents. The processor's own margin goes on top of that again.

Clearly Payments' 2025 Canadian report puts the resulting totals in context: Visa and Mastercard typically cost 1.4% to 2.0% in person, while Amex typically costs 2.0% to 3.0% in person. The same report notes that domestic consumer interchange for eligible small merchants averaged 0.95% for in-person Visa and Mastercard transactions under the federal small business programme, which is part of why the gap opens up. Amex is not part of that average.

A $30 ticket, worked both ways

Assumptions, labelled: a retail sale of $30, in person, on a shop with a 60% gross margin, so $18 of margin in the sale. On Square flat rate, the Amex fee is $0.75, identical to the Visa fee. Extra cost of accepting Amex: $0.00.

On interchange plus, use a scenario gap of 0.8 percentage points, the difference between the midpoints of the Clearly Payments ranges above. The extra cost on a $30 Amex sale is $0.24. That is the whole premium at small ticket size. Set it against the alternative: the customer whose only card, or preferred card, is Amex, and who walks. Losing one $18 margin sale wipes out the premium saved on 75 Amex sales at that ticket. A seller would need an extraordinary walk away rate, close to zero, before refusing Amex paid for itself.

When the math flips

Three situations change the answer. First, large tickets: at $1,000, a 0.8 point gap is $8, and the OptBlue wholesale rate itself steps up to 2.00% for retail tickets over $500, so the premium grows with the ticket while the margin percentage often shrinks. Second, thin margins: a business running a 10% gross margin feels a percentage point of processing very differently from the 60% example above. Third, a customer base that would pay another way anyway: if Amex holders at your counter happily switch to debit or Visa when asked, the premium buys nothing. That is a fact to establish from your own till, not an assumption.

How to decide, step by step

  1. Find your pricing model. Flat rate means brand does not change your fee. Interchange plus means it does. Your statement or pricing page says which.
  2. Pull one month of Amex volume. Total Amex dollars and the average Amex ticket. Note whether tickets sit under or over the $500 OptBlue step for retail and services.
  3. Price the premium. Multiply Amex volume by a scenario gap, 0.5 to 1.0 percentage points is a reasonable test band from the ranges above, and compare the result with the margin on the Amex sales themselves.
  4. Ask the walk away question honestly. If even a small share of Amex customers would not complete the purchase on another card, the margin on those sales usually covers the premium many times over.
  5. Revisit at renewal, not at the till. Acceptance is a pricing model decision. The cheaper fix for a real Amex premium is usually negotiating the processor markup, which applies to every card, rather than refusing one brand.

For most small ticket Canadian sellers, the answer the math gives is to accept Amex, and to spend the negotiating energy on the markup line instead. The premium is measured in cents per sale. The lost sale is measured in whole margins.

, Penny Hughes, Money Writer. Saving Desk
