beehiiv Pricing Decoded: Free, Lite, Pro and the 0% Take Rate

Published 2026-10-21 · Last updated 2026-10-21

beehiiv rebuilt its pricing page in 2026, and most comparison content did not notice. The plan names changed — Launch, Scale and Max gave way to Free, Lite and Pro — the entry paid price moved, and the subscriber ceilings were redrawn, yet ranking “beehiiv pricing” pages still quote the older ladder in both directions: some overstate the entry cost with stale $39 figures, others understate the Pro tier by carrying the old Max price forward. This guide decodes the current structure from beehiiv's own pricing page (checked October 21, 2026), cross-checked against October 2026 third-party pricing reporting, with the two mechanics that decide real cost: pricing is set by subscriber band, and beehiiv's platform take on paid subscriptions is 0% — the comparison that matters against Substack's 10% is arithmetic, and it is worked below. The four plans as beehiiv states them

beehiiv's current page lists four plans. Free is $0, free forever with no credit card required: up to 2,500 subscribers, unlimited email sends, email templates, landing pages, the recommendation network, API and integrations — and, per beehiiv's FAQ, access to its core newsletter, website and podcast tools, with every plan including website, podcast, community and digital products. Lite is shown at $49/month on yearly billing for the 1,000-subscriber band (beehiiv's FAQ states paid plans start at $59/month, the month-to-month figure), adding paid subscriptions at a 0% take rate, ad monetisation, automations, the beehiiv MCP, audience polls and A/B testing. Pro is shown at $95/month on yearly billing at the same band, adding branding removal, webinars and coaching, advanced analytics, AI credits, webhooks, dynamic content and 24/7 priority support. Enterprise is custom-priced, adding SSO/SAML, dedicated IPs, a customer success manager, concierge onboarding, workspace templates and custom rate limits.

The band ladder: what growth actually costs

The starting prices above apply to small lists; the bill grows with the subscriber band. October 2026 third-party pricing reporting (EmailToolTester, following beehiiv's October 2026 price increases — reported as high as 52% on some large-list tiers) publishes the month-to-month ladder for the renamed plans: at 1,000 subscribers, Lite $59 and Pro $109; at 2,500, $79 and $149; at 5,000, $109 and $199; at 10,000, $139 and $239; at 25,000, $199 and $349; at 50,000, $299 and $469; at 100,000, $469 and $699. Treat that ladder as reported, not official — beehiiv's page shows band pricing through its subscriber selector rather than a static table — but the shape is the decision-relevant fact: the steep increases sit above 50,000 subscribers, so a newsletter's beehiiv bill is predictable and modest through the growth stage and becomes a real line item only at genuine scale. A second dated datapoint cross-checks the entry: a September 2026 platform comparison records Scale (now Lite) starting at $49/month monthly (~$43 annually) — the pre-increase ladder — which is exactly why older comparisons now understate current month-to-month entry pricing by about ten dollars.

The fee math that actually decides beehiiv vs Substack

beehiiv takes 0% of paid subscription and digital product revenue; the creator keeps everything minus Stripe's standard processing fee of 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction. Substack takes 10% off the top of every subscription, on top of the same Stripe processing. The crossover arithmetic is simple and worth doing with your own numbers: at $500/month of paid subscription revenue, Substack's platform cut is $50/month — roughly the cost of beehiiv's Lite entry — so above that revenue level the flat-fee platform is cheaper on fees alone, before counting beehiiv's ad network and Boosts as additional monetisation surface. At $5,000/month of paid revenue, the 10% cut is $500/month, $6,000 a year — against a Lite bill that, even at the 10,000-subscriber band, is reported at $139/month. Below the crossover, the honest answer reverses: a free Substack with no paid revenue costs nothing at all, and beehiiv's Free tier (2,500 subscribers, unlimited sends, but no paid subscriptions or ad monetisation per the plan structure) is the like-for-like starting point. The decision framework is therefore three questions: Is paid revenue live or planned within a year? Is the list past, or nearing, 2,500 subscribers (the Free ceiling)? And does the operation need more than one publication or branding removal (Pro's distinguishing features)? Two yeses put most small publishers on Lite; Pro is justified by publications, seats and support, not by sending volume, which is unlimited everywhere.

Traps and fine print

Four cautions complete the decode. First, stale comparisons are everywhere right now because the rename and repricing are recent — any “beehiiv pricing” page using Launch/Scale/Max names or $39/$99 figures predates the current structure. Second, the trial: Free is free forever with no card; Lite and Pro include a free trial with no card required, ending at upgrade. Third, outgrowing a plan is an upgrade conversation, not a penalty — beehiiv states plans can be upgraded at any time, with Enterprise as the route for custom volume, concierge onboarding or enterprise security. Fourth, the 0% take rate is a platform-fee statement, not a total-cost statement: Stripe's 2.9% + $0.30 still applies to every paid transaction on every plan, and budgeting that ignores it understates real cost by roughly three percent of revenue.

How the ceilings interact with the Free plan's real job

One more structural point decides many small publishers' timing. The Free plan's 2,500-subscriber ceiling is generous as a starting runway — unlimited sends at that size is a genuinely usable product, not a demo — but the monetisation features that make a newsletter a business (paid subscriptions, the ad network, Boosts) sit on the paid tiers, so the Free plan's job is validation, not revenue. The practical consequence: a newsletter that intends to charge should budget the move to Lite as part of launch economics rather than treating it as a growth milestone, because enabling paid subscriptions mid-flight, after an audience has been trained to expect everything free, is a harder conversion than launching paid from the start. Conversely, a publication with no monetisation intent can rationally stay on Free until the 2,500 ceiling forces the question, and the ceiling itself — unique addresses, per beehiiv's counting — arrives sooner than list owners expect once recommendations and imports are counted.
