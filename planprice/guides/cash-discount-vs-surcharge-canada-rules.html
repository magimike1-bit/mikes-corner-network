Cash Discounting vs Surcharging in Canada: Rules, Math and Risks

Published 2026-10-29 · Last updated 2026-10-29

Calling it a cash discount does not make it one. If a customer sees one price and then pays more because they chose a credit card, the card networks treat that as a surcharge — with a cap, a notice period and a disclosure regime attached — whatever the sign at the till calls it. The two programs can land at the same dollars. They do not carry the same rules, and mixing them up is the expensive way to find out. General information only, not financial or legal advice. Confirm any program with your acquirer and, for Quebec sales, with legal advice before starting.

The tripwire is mechanical, not verbal. Dual pricing stays pricing while both prices are displayed up front — on the shelf, the menu, the quote, the checkout screen — and the register charges exactly the displayed amount. The moment a separate fee line appears on the receipt, or a fee is added at the till to the displayed price, the program is a surcharge under the networks' logic, and the surcharge rules apply to it retroactively in spirit: cap, notice and disclosure were all due before the first transaction.

The surcharge rulebook in Canada

Canadian merchants outside Quebec have been able to surcharge credit card transactions since October 6, 2022, following the Visa and Mastercard class-action settlement. The conditions, from the federal and network sources checked October 29, 2026:

  • Credit only. No surcharge on debit — Visa's Canadian guidance excludes Visa debit and prepaid transactions, and Mastercard's Canadian FAQ states plainly: “We do not surcharge Debit Mastercard cards.” Interac debit is not surcharged.
  • Capped twice. The surcharge may not exceed 2.4%, and may not exceed your actual cost of accepting that card. A merchant whose effective cost is 1.9% has a 1.9% ceiling, not a 2.4% one.
  • Disclosed three ways. At the store entrance, at the point of sale (in store and online), and on every receipt, with the dollar amount on the receipt. Federal tax guidance adds the consumer side of the same rule: the surcharge must be disclosed before the transaction is completed, and the customer must have the opportunity to opt out of using the credit card on that disclosure.
  • Notice before the first sale. Advance written notice to the acquirer and the networks is required before surcharging begins; the network process described in Canadian merchant guides runs 30 days ahead.
  • Refunds include the surcharge. If a surcharged transaction is refunded, the surcharge is refunded with it — in full for a full refund, pro-rated for a partial one.
  • Not in Quebec. Quebec's consumer protection rules prohibit demanding a price higher than the advertised price, which blocks the surcharge model there.

Helcim's published Canadian material also shows how processors package this: its Fee Saver is an optional surcharge program for eligible businesses, subject to the card networks' surcharge rules. A processor program does not transfer the merchant's obligations — the cap is still tied to that merchant's own cost of acceptance, and the disclosure is still on that merchant's door, till and receipts.

Two honest wrinkles fall out of that table. First, the dollars are near-identical, which is the point: choosing between the programs is a rules and customer-reaction decision, not a math trick. Second, a 2.0% surcharge on a $100 price does not quite recover a 2.0% cost charged on the $102 total — the merchant is a few cents short on every card sale, forever. Programs sold as “eliminate your fees” rarely mention that the fee applies to the surcharge too.

A $20,000 month — and the sale that wipes it out

Scale the same assumption: $20,000 a month in credit card sales at a 2.0% effective cost is $400 a month in processing fees. A compliant 2.0% surcharge recovers roughly that $400 from customers who stay on credit. Now price the risk side. At a 30% gross margin — an assumption, stated as one — losing $1,333 of sales costs $400 of margin. In other words, if the program drives away about 6.7% of that credit volume entirely, the recovered fees and the lost margin cancel out, before counting signage, staff explanations, or the customers who switch to debit (a better outcome — the sale survives at a lower acceptance cost).

That reaction risk is not theoretical. A Canadian Federation of Independent Business survey reported by Queen's University's Smith School of Business found about 19% of businesses planned to surcharge, 40% were unsure, and 26% said they would follow what others in their industry did. The same analysis notes the framing effect that makes the cash-discount route attractive: a discount is perceived as a small separate gain, while a surcharge registers as a loss — and reframing the same dollars as a discount is less likely to be judged unfair by card users. The numbers can match while the queue's mood does not.

Before either program starts

  1. Compute your actual cost first. Total card acceptance costs divided by card volume, from real statements. That number is the surcharge ceiling (up to 2.4%) and the honest size of any cash discount. Check for minimums or monthly fees in the current processor agreement that a surcharge program does not remove — contract terms, including termination conditions, survive a pricing change at the till.
  2. Pick the mechanism, then follow its rulebook — not the other program's. Surcharge: notice, entrance and point-of-sale disclosure, receipt line, credit only, no Quebec. Dual pricing: both prices displayed everywhere a price appears, no fee line on the receipt, consistently applied.
  3. Configure the terminal to match the paperwork. The surcharge must never land on a debit transaction, and a refund must return its surcharge share automatically.
  4. Brief the counter. Staff need one sentence for “why is this more on card?” that matches the signage word for word. A program explained three different ways reads as a trick even when it is compliant.

Success looks like this: a customer can learn the full price of paying by card before paying, from the door, the shelf or the checkout screen — and the amount on the receipt is exactly the amount that disclosure promised.
