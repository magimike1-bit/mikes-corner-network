Chargeback Fees in Canada: What Processors Charge and How Disputes Really Cost

Published 2026-10-26 · Last updated 2026-10-26

The $15 fee is the part everyone quotes, and it is close to the smallest part of the bill. Lose a dispute on a $100 sale and the sale itself is only the first line: the product is gone, the shipping is gone, the original processing fee is not returned, and staff time goes into a response the bank may still reject. One widely cited industry costing — Mastercard research with Javelin, updated June 2026 — puts the average at $128 per chargeback in internal and third-party costs alone, before the merchandise is counted. Canadian merchants pay Canadian per-dispute fees on top of that stack, and those fees, plus the rules for getting them back, differ enough by processor to change which disputes are worth fighting. General information only, not financial advice.

What Canadian processors charge per dispute

A chargeback fee is the processor's charge for handling a dispute a customer filed with their card-issuing bank. The disputed amount is held or debited while the case runs, and the fee is charged per case, win or lose, unless the processor's rules say otherwise. These are the published Canadian figures, checked October 26, 2026:

Two entries need a second look. Stripe's fee is really two fees: CA$15 when the dispute arrives, and another CA$15 if you contest it by submitting evidence. Contest and lose, and the dispute fees total CA$30; contest and win, and the countered CA$15 comes back while the received CA$15 does not — outside a small number of countries, that first fee is never refunded. Moneris is the opposite shape: one published $25 chargeback fee, the highest standard figure in the table, plus an $80 authorization chargeback handling fee that applies to authorization-related chargebacks specifically. A merchant comparing headline fees alone would miss both structures.

Contract-priced processors are absent from the table on purpose. Adyen, Checkout.com and Worldpay do not publish a public per-country dispute fee; theirs is set in the merchant agreement. Any specific Canadian figure attributed to them without a contract behind it would be invented, so the honest entry is: check your own agreement. The same caution applies to reseller-sold accounts generally, where the per-dispute fee lives in the contract paperwork rather than on a pricing page.

Win the dispute, keep the fee? It depends

Winning returns the sale. Whether it returns the fee is a separate rule, and it decides the economics of small disputes. On Helcim and Shopify Payments, a win refunds the full $15, so fighting a well-documented dispute costs time and nothing else. On Stripe, a win still leaves the CA$15 received fee behind, so a contested win on a $25 order can cost more than half the order's value in fees. On Square there is no fee either way; the cost of a Square dispute is the disputed amount itself, held from your balance or bank account while the bank decides — a process Square's documentation puts at up to 90 days, with an initial seven-day window to submit your information, and the bank's decision final.

Run that logic before contesting anything under about $30. Accepting a dispute costs the sale and the fee where one applies. Contesting it on Stripe costs a second CA$15 if you lose. Contesting it anywhere costs the staff time to assemble receipts, delivery proof and policy records. A dispute you are likely to lose, on a small ticket, is cheapest accepted quickly; a dispute with clean delivery evidence on a large ticket is the one worth the paperwork. Neither answer comes from the fee table alone — it comes from the fee table plus your evidence plus the ticket size.

The fee difference between processors on one case is $15 to $25. The difference between a disputed sale and a completed one is the whole stack.

The ratio problem above the fees

Every dispute also counts against a ratio, and past the networks' lines the costs stop being per-case. Since April 1, 2026, Visa's Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP) marks a merchant Excessive at a ratio of 1.5% — fraud reports plus disputes divided by settled card-not-present transactions — with at least 1,500 combined events in the month for merchants outside the CEMEA region, Canada included in the standard programme geography. Visa assesses fees on Excessive merchants. Mastercard's excessive-chargeback tiers start lower in count: the first tier covers 100 to 299 chargebacks a month at a 1.5% to 2.99% rate, with fines that escalate the longer a merchant stays in the programme, and a higher tier at 300 or more chargebacks and a 3% rate. Escalated individual cases carry their own exposure: scheme arbitration can add a filing fee of up to US$600, paid by the losing party.

Most small Canadian merchants will never reach 1,500 events or 100 chargebacks in a month, and the article says so plainly so the thresholds are not mistaken for everyday risks. The nearer risk is quieter: processors run their own reviews below the network lines, funds can be held while a dispute is decided, and a rising dispute rate is the signal those reviews read first. The ratio is a health metric even when no fine is anywhere near.

Decide which disputes to fight

A short procedure, in the order it should run when a dispute lands:

  1. Check the clock. Response windows are short and processor-specific — Square gives seven days to submit information, and the bank's decision after that is final. A missed deadline is an automatic loss at full cost.
  2. Price the fight. Add the fee outcome for your processor (table above) to the staff time an evidence packet will take, and compare it with the ticket. Small ticket, weak evidence, second Stripe fee at risk: accepting is often the cheaper decision.
  3. Match evidence to the reason. Delivery confirmation for not-received claims, the refund policy as shown at purchase for policy disputes, transaction records for unrecognized charges. Evidence that answers a different question does not move the bank.
  4. Fix the descriptor and the refund path. A statement descriptor customers recognize, and a refund route easier to find than the bank's dispute button, prevent the next case — the only move that reduces both the fees and the ratio.

Success looks like this: every dispute in a month was either contested with matched evidence inside its window or accepted deliberately at a priced cost — none lost by silence, and none fought at a fee higher than the sale it defended.
