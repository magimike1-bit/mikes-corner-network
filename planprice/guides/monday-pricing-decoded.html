Monday.com Pricing Decoded: The Seat Blocks and the AI Credit Line

Published 2026-10-18 · Last updated 2026-10-18

Monday.com's price list looks simple — four paid tiers and a per-seat rate for each — but the number a team actually pays is set by three rules that sit underneath the headline rates: a three-seat minimum on every paid plan, seat counts that grow in blocks of five, and an AI credit line that now bills alongside the seats. This guide decodes those rules for Canadian small and mid-sized teams, works the year-one math at realistic headcounts, and sets out where the effective per-person rate quietly drifts away from the advertised one. All prices are in US dollars, as monday publishes them, and were checked on 2026-10-18.

General information, not financial advice. Prices change — always confirm the current rates on the vendor's own pricing page before buying.

The five tiers: the published seat rates

Monday's Work Management product sells in five tiers. Free costs US$0 and covers up to 2 seats and 3 boards — an evaluation tier, not a team tool. Basic costs US$9 per seat per month billed annually (US$12 month to month) and adds unlimited items, unlimited free viewers, 5 GB of file storage and dashboards drawn from a single board. Standard costs US$12 per seat per month billed annually (US$14 monthly) and adds the Timeline and Gantt views, Calendar view, guest access and the first automation allowance. Pro costs US$19 per seat per month billed annually (US$24 monthly) and adds private boards, time tracking, chart view and the formula column. Enterprise is priced on application. Annual billing saves 18% against monthly, and monday confirms on its own pricing page that annual plans are paid in one upfront installment. A 14-day trial of the Pro tier is the standard evaluation route.

Two structural facts matter before any feature comparison. First, every paid plan starts at three seats — monday's own FAQ states plainly that plans start from 3 users. Second, monday sells more than Work Management: CRM, dev and service are separate products with their own per-seat pricing, so a team that adopts monday for both projects and pipeline is buying two seat subscriptions, not one platform fee. Neither fact appears in most comparison tables, and both change the year-one total.

The 3-seat minimum and the block of five

The three-seat minimum sets the floor under every paid account. A solo operator or a two-person team pays for three seats regardless: the real minimum spend is US$27 a month on Basic, US$36 on Standard and US$57 on Pro on annual billing — US$324, US$432 and US$684 a year before anything else is added. For a genuine three-person team that is a rounding detail. For a freelancer evaluating monday against a flat-rate tool, it is the whole decision.

Above the minimum, seats are added in blocks of five. A team of four pays for five seats; a team of six pays for ten; a team of eleven pays for fifteen. The consequence is that the advertised per-seat rate is only the true rate at headcounts of 3, 5, 10, 15 and so on. Between those points, the effective rate per active user climbs: a seven-person team on Standard pays for 10 seats at US$12, which is US$120 a month — an effective US$17.14 per active user, 43% above the headline rate it thought it was buying. Budget the licence count after rounding up to the next block, then divide by the people who will actually log in. That effective rate is the only honest number to compare against alternatives.

The AI credit line billed beside the seats

The newest layer of monday's pricing is the one most comparison tables still miss. Each paid plan now carries an AI credit bundle that bills alongside the seats: roughly 1,000 credits a month on Basic (about US$10), 2,000 on Standard (about US$20) and 3,000 on Pro (about US$30) at the annual-plan credit rate of US$0.01 per credit. Monday's own guidance is that a typical three-person team uses roughly 800–1,200 credits a month — 1,000 credits can cover roughly 50 resume screenings, five hours of meeting summaries and hundreds of automated workflow updates — so the bundle is sized to be consumed, and usage beyond the bundle is purchased at the per-credit rate.

The practical effect is that the seat price understates the invoice by a fixed monthly amount that scales with tier, not headcount. A three-seat Standard account is not US$432 a year; it is US$432 in seats plus roughly US$240 in AI credits — about US$672 a year. A three-seat Pro account is US$684 in seats plus about US$360 in credits. These are exactly the kind of per-use charges that surprise buyers after signature: the headline comparison said US$12 a seat, and the first invoice says otherwise. Any monday quote should be read as seats plus credits, and any comparison against a rival should use that combined figure.

Automation caps: the quiet upgrade trigger

The second meter is automation volume. Basic includes no meaningful automation allowance; Standard includes 250 automation actions and 250 integration actions per month; Pro jumps to 25,000 of each. The hundred-fold gap between Standard and Pro is deliberate pricing architecture: a team that builds a handful of automations will exhaust 250 actions quickly once those automations fire on every item change across several boards, and when the cap is reached the automations stop until the next cycle. Teams should count expected monthly automation runs before choosing a tier — a Standard plan that stalls mid-month is not cheaper than Pro, it is a Pro purchase deferred to the worst possible moment. The same logic applies to dashboards: one board on Basic, five on Standard, ten on Pro, up to fifty on Enterprise. Cross-board reporting is a tier feature, not a default.

Year-one math at real headcounts

At annual list prices, with the AI credit bundle included and seat counts rounded up to the next block of five: a 3-person team pays about US$444 a year on Basic, US$672 on Standard and US$1,044 on Pro. A 7-person team buys 10 seats: on Standard that is US$1,440 in seats plus about US$240 in AI credits — roughly US$1,680 a year, or about US$20 per active user per month once both the block rounding and the credit line are counted. A 12-person team buys 15 seats: Standard totals about US$2,400 a year (US$2,160 in seats plus credits) and Pro about US$3,780 (US$3,420 plus credits). Set against flat-rate or cheaper per-seat rivals, monday's case rests on the visual board model, guest access and the breadth of its product family — not on price. Teams whose headcount sits just above a block boundary should also weigh delaying seat additions or using free viewer and guest roles, which do not consume paid seats, before accepting the next block.

The decision framework

This is a buying-decision guide, not a setup procedure — the framework below is the process.

  1. Round up first. Compute the seat block your headcount actually lands in — 3, 5, 10, 15 — and price that, not your headcount.
  2. Add the AI credit line. Budget the tier's credit bundle (roughly $10/$20/$30 a month) on top of seats, and estimate overage if AI features will be used daily.
  3. Count automations before choosing Standard. If expected automation and integration actions exceed 250 a month, the real comparison is Pro against rivals, not Standard.
  4. Price every monday product separately. Work Management, CRM, dev and service each carry their own seat bill — a two-product adoption is two subscriptions.
  5. Use viewers and guests deliberately. Stakeholders who only read or comment may not need a paid seat at all — keep them out of the block math.

Frequently asked questions

How much does monday.com cost per user?

On annual billing, Basic is US$9, Standard is US$12 and Pro is US$19 per seat per month, with Enterprise quoted. Monthly billing raises those rates to US$12, US$14 and US$24. Paid plans start at three seats and grow in blocks of five, and each plan also carries an AI credit bundle billed alongside the seats, so the effective cost per active user is higher than the headline rate for most teams.

Is there a free monday.com plan?

Yes, but it is capped at 2 seats and 3 boards, which makes it an evaluation tier. The standard way to test the paid feature set is the 14-day Pro trial.

Why did the first monday invoice come in higher than the per-seat price suggested?

Three reasons account for almost every surprise: the seat count was rounded up to the next block of five, the plan's AI credit bundle billed alongside the seats, and annual billing was charged as a single upfront installment. All three are visible before purchase once the quote is read as seats plus credits at the block count.
