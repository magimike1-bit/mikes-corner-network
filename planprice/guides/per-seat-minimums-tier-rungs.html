Per-Seat Minimums and Tier Rungs: The Math the Pricing Page Hides

Published 2026-10-12

The pricing page says $9 a seat. You have two people. Your invoice says $27 a month — because the minimum is three seats, the feature you need sits a tier up, and nobody's per-seat math survives contact with the actual plan structure. Per-seat pricing has three hidden mechanics — minimums, blocks, and rungs — and this guide gives you the one formula that sees through all of them.

General information, not financial advice. Prices change — always confirm seat terms on the vendor's own pricing page before buying.

The pricing page shows $9; your invoice shows $54

monday.com's Basic plan lists at $9 a seat a month on annual billing. But paid plans carry a three-seat minimum, and seats are sold in multiples of five beyond that. A solo founder pays for three seats ($27 a month); a two-person team pays for three; a four-person team pays for six. Reddit users flag exactly this friction: solo operators and two-person shops pay for seats nobody uses.

The waste is measurable. A four-person team on Basic billed for six seats pays $648 a year at $9 a seat — $216 of it, a full third, for empty slots. A 16-person studio buys 20 seats. The per-seat price is accurate; the number of seats is not yours to choose.

Minimums: paying for seats that do not exist

Seat minimums are the bluntest mechanic: the vendor sets a floor on headcount. monday.com's three-seat minimum means the effective entry price is triple the advertised per-seat rate — $27–$36 a month instead of $9–$12. For monday's CRM product, a two-person team pays $288 a year for a seat nobody uses.

Minimums hit smallest teams hardest and vanish as you grow: at 30 seats, three phantom seats are a 10% overhead; at two real users, they're a 50% tax. When comparing tools at small headcount, the minimum — not the per-seat rate — is the number that decides.

Blocks: the step-function nobody graphs

Blocks are minimums that repeat: seats sold in fixed increments, so every hire that crosses a block boundary buys the whole block. monday.com sells seats in multiples of five after the initial three — adding your fourth team member jumps the bill from 3 seats to 6, and the sixteenth hire jumps it from 15 to 20. Your cost curve is a staircase, and headcount growth lands on the risers.

The contrast is the flat-fee model: Crisp's Essentials plan is $95 a month flat and includes 10 agent seats — a team of 5 pays exactly what a team of 8 pays. Help Scout's per-seat model ($25 a user on Standard, $45 on Plus) makes every hire a new line item. Neither is always cheaper; the question is which shape matches your hiring plan.

Rungs: the feature you need is one tier up

Rungs are the vertical version: the capability you actually bought the tool for sits one tier above the entry price. Workflow automation — the reason most teams buy a CRM — starts on HubSpot's Professional tier ($890 a month), not Starter ($20 a seat). Time tracking on monday.com starts on Pro ($19 a seat), not Standard ($12). Zendesk's omnichannel Suite starts at $55 an agent — nearly 3× the $19 ticketing-only plan.

The entry price is therefore a fiction for most buyers: budget the rung with the features, not the rung with the headline. One 2026 pricing study's core finding was exactly this — the cheapest plan that includes the features the product is chosen for is the only honest "starting price."

The effective-per-seat formula

One formula cuts through all three mechanics:

Effective per-seat cost = (seats billed × list price) ÷ seats actually used

Frequently asked questions

What is monday.com's minimum seat requirement?

Three seats on all paid plans, with seats sold in multiples of five beyond that. A one- or two-person team pays the three-seat price ($27–$36 a month on Basic/Standard annual billing).

Why is the feature I need always on a higher tier?

Because tier design monetizes intent: the capabilities that make a tool valuable (automation, SSO, advanced reporting) are the ones vendors gate, since buyers will pay to unlock them. Budget the tier with your must-have features, not the entry tier.

Is per-seat or flat-rate pricing better for a small team?

Flat-rate wins when headcount sits comfortably inside the included seats (Crisp's $95 for up to 10 agents); per-seat wins when the team is tiny and stable. The deciding inputs are the seat minimum, the block size, and your hiring plan — run the effective-per-seat formula for both.
