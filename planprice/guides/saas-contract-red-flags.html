SaaS Contract Red Flags: 9 Clauses to Catch Before You Sign

Published 2026-10-12

Most SaaS contracts are vendor paper — drafted to resolve every ambiguity in the vendor's favour. You don't need a legal team to catch the expensive parts; you need to know which clauses to read and what to push for. Here are nine flags ranked by cost, in plain language, plus a 30-minute scan to run before signing.

General information, not legal advice. Read your own agreement — and get counsel for anything you might actually dispute.

The big three: renewal, uplifts, true-ups

Flags 1–3 do the most damage because they compound. An uncapped renewal uplift is an open-ended cost — the single highest-value term to negotiate, since everything else in the contract is bounded. Auto-renewal windows of 60–90 days quietly recommit you for another year; diarise the notice date the day you sign. True-up asymmetry means exceeding your seat count generates an invoice while shrinking your team generates nothing — push for the overage rate locked at your original per-seat price (not list) and a true-down at renewal.

Why the renewal clause matters — a small business reviewed Salesforce on Trustpilot in September 2026: "They put me (without consent) on an auto renew, and then renewed my subscription. 4 days later I tried to cancel and they won't let me." Another reviewer the month after put it more bluntly: "Too difficult to use, expensive and difficult to cancel. Buyer be aware!"

The pricing flags: SSO tax, seat minimums, onboarding fees

Flags 4–6 hide in the pricing, not the legalese. The SSO tax: many vendors gate single sign-on — a baseline security control — behind a premium tier. If SSO matters to you, confirm which tier includes it before you price the deal; the "right" plan may be a tier higher than the one you budgeted. Seat minimums: monday.com requires at least three seats on paid plans, so price the minimum, not the per-seat number. Onboarding fees: HubSpot's Marketing Hub Professional carries a US$3,000 one-time onboarding fee (checked 2026-10-11). And check for per-device or per-integration meters — "one provider had a cost per device connected even if it was just reading data" — because the contract meters more than seats.

The 30-minute pre-sign scan

Read these sections, in this order:

  1. Term and renewal (5 min). Find the renewal date, notice period, notice method, and whether the renewal term steps down or rolls the full length. Extract the cancellation deadline onto your calendar immediately.
  2. Fees and price changes (5 min). Find the renewal pricing language — "then-current rates" with no cap is the flag. List every fee beyond the subscription: onboarding, implementation, overage, support tiers.
  3. Licenses and seats (5 min). Seat minimums, whether you can reduce seats mid-term, and what happens to unused seats. If the contract is silent on reduction, assume you can't.
  4. Termination and data (10 min). Termination rights (for convenience vs for cause), what happens to your data when the contract ends, export format, and whether transition services exist. Push for data back in an industry-standard, machine-readable format — CSV, JSON, or SQL — plus ~60 days of transition services.
  5. Changes and SLAs (5 min). Can the vendor modify terms by posting to a website? What uptime is promised, and what happens when it's missed — a vague "best efforts" with no remedy is a flag.

Frequently asked questions

What is the SSO tax?

Vendors gating single sign-on behind a premium tier, effectively charging extra for a baseline security control. Confirm which tier includes SSO before you price the deal — the tier you need may cost more than the tier you budgeted.

Can SaaS vendors raise prices at renewal?

Usually yes, unless the contract caps it. "At the then-current rates" with no cap is standard vendor paper — which is why negotiating a 3–5% or CPI cap is the highest-value term in the agreement.

Do I need a lawyer to review a SaaS contract?

For standard small-team subscriptions, the 30-minute scan above catches the expensive parts. For multi-year, high-value, or heavily negotiated agreements — or anything you might dispute — get counsel. This guide is information, not advice.
