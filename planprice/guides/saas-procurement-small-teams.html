A SaaS Procurement Process That Fits a 10-Person Team

Published 2026-10-12

Every SaaS procurement guide assumes you have a procurement department, a legal team, and an RFP process. A 10-person team has none of those — the owner is procurement, legal, and finance, usually on a Tuesday afternoon. This guide gives you the lean version: a four-step buying loop that takes about an hour per purchase and prevents the five most expensive buying mistakes small teams make.

Why small teams skip procurement (and what it costs)

Skipping procurement feels efficient — someone needs a tool, they buy the tool, work continues. The costs arrive later, and they're always bigger than the hour you saved. Small businesses typically spend US$10,000–50,000 a year on SaaS (roughly 10–20% of the IT budget), and without a process that money leaks through four familiar holes: shadow purchases on cards, auto-renewals with short notice windows, seat creep after onboarding, and duplicate tools bought by different teams. Tropic reports that 80% of its customers have had a contract auto-renew without their knowledge — that's a procurement failure, not a vendor crime.

The fix isn't bureaucracy. It's a loop small enough to actually run.

The 4-step loop

  1. Intake (15 min). Before any subscription is bought, answer the five intake questions below. If you can't answer them, you're not ready to buy — you're ready to research.
  2. Shortlist (20 min). Compare at most three vendors on three things: must-have features, true price (seats × rate × term, plus onboarding and overage), and exit terms (export format, notice window). Three vendors, three criteria, one page.
  3. Sign-right (15 min). Before signing, check the renewal clause (notice period and method), the price-increase cap (or its absence), and seat flexibility (can you drop seats mid-term?). These three checks catch the traps that cost the most later.
  4. Review (10 min, at every renewal). Calendar the cancellation deadline — not the renewal date — with a 45-day lead reminder. When it fires, re-justify the tool: still used? still the right tier? still the right price?

What success looks like: every subscription in the company has a one-page intake note, a named owner, and a renewal deadline on the calendar. New purchases take an hour; surprise renewals stop happening.

The 15-minute intake ritual: five questions

Answer these in writing before any purchase. They fit on an index card:

  1. What job does this do that nothing we own does? If an existing tool does 80% of it, the answer is usually "configure what we have," not "buy a new tool."
  2. Who uses it, and how many seats on day one? Name the humans. Watch for seat minimums — monday.com requires at least three seats on paid plans, so a solo user pays for three regardless.
  3. What's the true first-year cost? Seats × rate × 12, plus onboarding fees (HubSpot's Marketing Hub Professional carries a US$3,000 one-time onboarding fee, checked 2026-10-11), plus any overage or integration charges. The pricing page is the teaser; the order form is the price.
  4. How do we leave? Export format, notice window, data retention after termination. If the answer is "we'll figure it out later," you're signing a lock-in, not a subscription.
  5. Who owns the renewal decision? A name, not a team. If nobody owns it, it auto-renews — that's the default outcome, not an accident.

What each skipped step costs

Skipping intake's trial question: you commit to an annual plan for an unproven tool. At monday.com Basic's US$9/seat/mo billed annually (checked 2026-10-05), a 10-seat annual commitment is US$1,080 for a tool the team might abandon by March. Skipping the duplicate scan: two project tools at ~US$10/user/mo each doubles a category — Asana Starter at US$10.99/user/mo (checked 2026-10-05) alongside a second tracker is US$250+/month for one job. Skipping sign-right's renewal check: the auto-renewal that 80% of Tropic's customers have lived through, at whatever price the vendor sets. Each skipped step has a price tag; the loop exists because the hour it costs is always cheaper.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between SaaS procurement and purchasing?

Purchasing is the transaction — buying the software. Procurement is the discipline around it: needs assessment, vendor comparison, negotiation, and lifecycle management. Small teams need the discipline, not the department.

How much should a small business spend on SaaS?

Benchmarks put it at 10–20% of the IT budget, or roughly US$10,000–50,000 a year for a small business. The number matters less than the process — unmanaged spend at any level leaks through the same four holes.

Do I need procurement software?

Not at 10 people. A shared doc with the intake answers, a calendar with renewal deadlines, and the discipline to run the loop cover it. Procurement platforms earn their keep when you're managing dozens of vendors and renewals — that's a later problem.
