Shadow IT Cost Control: Finding the Subscriptions Nobody Approved

Published 2026-10-12

Shadow IT is the software your team bought without telling you: the note-taking app on someone's personal card, the second video tool marketing expensed, the AI assistant three people pay for individually. None of it went through any approval, all of it renews itself, and nobody owns the total. This guide shows you how to find it with three free checks, what to do once you find it, and what the consolidation is worth.

General information. This is cost-control guidance, not a security audit — unapproved tools handling sensitive data deserve a proper security review too.

What shadow IT costs a small team

The subscription fees are the smallest part. The real costs show up indirectly, in four leaks:

  1. Redundant spending. Multiple teams independently pay for apps that do nearly the same thing — a task app here, a near-identical one there — with no consolidation, no negotiated pricing, and no one checking whether one tool could replace all three.
  2. Security exposure. Software nobody knows about doesn't get patched, and company data sitting in unapproved apps is outside your control — where it's stored, who can access it, how long it's kept. One industry analysis put the average cost of cleaning up after shadow-IT-related breaches at over US$4 million per incident; small businesses feel that kind of hit even harder.
  3. Lost productivity. Shadow apps rarely talk to your core systems. Data gets entered twice, reports don't reconcile, and salaried hours go into manual work that shouldn't exist.
  4. Compliance exposure. An unmanaged app handling sensitive data can trigger violations whose fines dwarf whatever the app cost to license.

The three free detection checks

You don't need a SaaS management platform to find shadow IT. Run these three checks — they cover nearly everything:

  1. Review identity-provider and OAuth grants (20 min). The most revealing check. In Google Workspace, go to Security > Access and data control > Third-party apps. In Microsoft 365, open Entra ID > Enterprise applications. Look for scheduling bots with calendar access, email assistants with inbox access, and document tools with drive-wide permissions. Flag everything without a verified corporate contract — and revoke the ones that shouldn't be there.
  2. Audit card and expense records (20 min). Shadow tools leave a paper trail on expense reports. Scan corporate cards for US$10–40/month micro-subscriptions — charges from OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Otter.ai, Notion AI, or unfamiliar overseas billing platforms are the tell.
  3. Ask the team directly (10 min). Send one message: "Reply with every software tool you pay for or use for work that IT didn't set up for you. No consequences for two weeks — after that, unapproved tools get cut." An amnesty window gets you the list that no console ever will.

What success looks like: a single list of every app in use, each tagged approved or unapproved, with the unapproved ones carrying a keep-or-kill decision and a date.

The amnesty-and-consolidate play

Finding the tools is half the job; the savings come from what you do next. Declare the amnesty, inventory everything, then consolidate duplicates into one vetted platform. The argument that lands with the team isn't financial — it's operational: one platform means one integration, one access review, one offboarding path.

The math: a 10-person team where marketing pays for Notion Plus (US$10/user/mo, checked 2026-10-11) for five people while the rest of the company runs on something else is US$600 a year for a duplicate. A second video tool at Zoom Workplace Pro prices (US$13.33/user/mo, checked 2026-10-11) for even three users is another US$480 a year. Two consolidations like that fund the entire audit many times over — and each killed duplicate also kills its renewal, its offboarding gap, and its support burden.

For the tools worth keeping, onboard them properly: corporate contract, admin ownership, renewal date on the calendar. Shadow IT you decide to keep isn't shadow anymore — it's just IT.

Shadow AI: the new unapproved line item

The newest shadow spend doesn't look like software at all — it's individual AI subscriptions. Employees expense US$10–40/month AI tools that bypass procurement entirely: chatbots, writing assistants, meeting notetakers. Each one is small; ten of them is a second SaaS stack nobody approved.

Handle it the same way: the card-statement check catches the subscriptions, and the amnesty catches the free-tier usage. Then make a deliberate decision — one approved AI tool on a team plan is almost always cheaper and more governable than a dozen individual subscriptions, and it keeps company data inside one contract instead of scattered across personal accounts.

Frequently asked questions

What is shadow IT?

Hardware, software, or cloud services that employees use for work without going through IT approval — typically bought on personal or corporate cards and expensed. It's defined by the lack of oversight, not by the tool being bad.

How do I find unauthorized software in my company?

Three checks cover it: review OAuth/third-party app grants in your identity provider, scan card and expense records for micro-subscriptions, and ask the team directly during an amnesty window.

Should I ban all unapproved tools?

No — banning drives it further underground. Inventory everything, keep the tools worth keeping under proper contracts, consolidate the duplicates, and kill the rest. The goal is visibility and ownership, not a ban list.
