Webex Pricing Decoded for Canadian Small Businesses

Published 2026-10-23 · Last updated 2026-10-23

Search for Webex pricing and you will find $12, $14.50, $17, $25 and $27 per user per month — all of them defensible, none of them the whole story. The confusion is structural, not sloppy: Cisco sells two overlapping line-ups, a Meet range on its own pricing page and a Suite range that bundles meetings with a cloud phone system, and third-party trackers quote whichever line-up they found first. This guide decodes the current structure from Cisco's official Webex pricing page (checked October 23, 2026), separates the two line-ups, and works the number a Canadian small business actually pays — which, because Webex licenses hosts rather than attendees, is usually smaller than the headcount suggests. All prices are USD as published; Cisco publishes no Canadian-dollar price list for Webex, so budget exchange on top of every figure below. General information only, not financial advice.

The Meet plans, as Cisco publishes them

Free is $0 with no time limit on the account: unlimited meetings of up to 40 minutes each, up to 100 attendees per meeting, screen sharing with annotation, whiteboards, local recording, calendar integration, advanced noise cancellation and unlimited messaging. The 40-minute cap is the whole catch — it is generous for internal standups and disqualifying for client meetings that run long. Starter is $144 per user per year — $12 a month billed annually: everything in Free, plus the Webex AI Assistant, meetings up to 24 hours, up to 150 attendees, 5 GB of cloud meeting recording, live polling and Q&A by Slido, closed captions and advanced support. Business is $204 per user per year — $17 a month billed annually: everything in Starter, with the attendee cap raised to 200 and cloud recording doubled to 10 GB. Enterprise is quote-only: up to 1,000 attendees, local and unlimited cloud recording, and FedRAMP-authorized security — a compliance tier, not a small-business one. Add-ons sit on top, and one is worth seeing before assuming dial-in is cheap: the Call Me add-on, where the meeting phones you instead of you dialling in, is listed at $429 per license per year billed annually, covering 70 countries and regions.

The Suite line-up: where the other prices come from

The $14.50, $25 and $27 figures in tracker articles are not invented — they belong to the second line-up. GetApp's provider-supplied listing, for example, prices Webex Meet at $14.50 per user per month and Webex Suite Business at $25 per user per month, with a standalone Call Plan at $17. The Suite is a different product shape: meetings plus a cloud-based phone number for each license, phone menus and extensions — a phone system with meetings attached, sold to businesses replacing a PBX, not to a firm that only needs video calls. The practical rule: if a quoted Webex price does not say whether it is a Meet plan or a Suite bundle, the comparison is meaningless. A business that needs meetings only should price from the Meet table above; a business genuinely replacing its phone system should price the Suite — and compare it against phone-system rivals, not against Zoom.

The per-host rule: the line that decides the bill

Webex licenses the host, not the audience. Attendees join free on any plan — the caps in the table are how many guests a host's meeting holds, not how many licences you buy. For a small business this is the single most valuable line in the pricing structure: licences track the handful of people who schedule and run meetings, not the whole payroll. A twelve-person firm where four people host client calls needs four paid seats, not twelve. The contrast with Google Meet matters here: Meet is not sold standalone — it rides inside Google Workspace, where every user pays (Business Starter at CA$9.20 per user per month on annual commitment, per Google's Canadian page) whether or not they ever host, though in exchange the seat also carries email, storage and the rest of the suite. Neither model is wrong; they meter different things, and which one is cheaper depends entirely on your host-to-staff ratio.

At the free end the comparison flips: Webex Free's 100-attendee cap is roomy, but its 40-minute limit is the binding constraint for client work — a firm whose meetings all run under 40 minutes can honestly pay $0, and a firm whose discovery calls run an hour cannot.

What the reviews say

Review coverage of Webex skews enterprise, which is itself information: this is a platform whose centre of gravity is larger organizations. A representative G2 review (a student researcher at an enterprise-sized education employer, January 2026, 3 of 5 stars, titled “Reliable for Academic Meetings, but Can Feel Heavy and Takes Time to Learn”) praises exactly the small-business virtues — scheduling and joining from a single interface is straightforward, and core features such as setting up meetings, sharing screens and managing participants work reliably with minimal configuration — and names the recurring complaint: the application can feel heavy on lower-end devices, especially during longer meetings, and some features are not intuitive at first, taking new users a while to learn. For a Canadian firm with clients on older hardware, that heaviness is a practical trial item: run the first client meeting on the client's typical laptop, not on yours, before the annual payment.

Who should buy Webex, and who should not

Buy Webex if your hosts are few and your meetings are long, if you want the AI Assistant and cloud recording inside the entry paid tier rather than as add-ons, or if a Suite bundle genuinely replaces a phone system you are paying for separately. Skip it if your team already pays for Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 (Meet or Teams is already in the building), if most of your meetings are short internal check-ins the Free plan covers, or if your clients' hardware is old enough that the app's weight becomes your support problem. And whichever way the decision goes, take the annual price only after a month on monthly or free terms — the decoded structure above is stable, but fit is a thing you learn from your own meetings, not from a pricing page.
