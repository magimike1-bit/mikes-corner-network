Wrike Pricing Decoded: Team, Business, and the Seat-Band Trap

Published 2026-10-15 · Last updated 2026-10-15

Wrike's price list looks short — a free plan, two published per-user tiers, and two quote-only tiers at the top — but the number a team actually pays is decided by three rules that sit underneath the headline rates: which seat band the team falls into, whether the work genuinely needs the Business tier, and which add-ons and AI allowances sit outside the seat price. This guide decodes those rules for Canadian small and mid-sized teams, works the year-one math at realistic headcounts, and sets out the point at which Wrike's premium over cheaper tools is and is not defensible. All prices are in US dollars, as Wrike publishes them, and were checked on 2026-10-15.

General information, not financial advice. Prices change — always confirm the current rates on the vendor's own pricing page before buying.

The five tiers: what is published and what is quoted

Wrike currently sells five plans. Free costs US$0 and covers essential task management for unlimited users. Team costs US$10 per user per month billed annually and is sold for teams of 2–15 users; it adds shareable dashboards, interactive Gantt charts, custom fields, and an AI Essentials allowance. Business costs US$25 per user per month billed annually for teams of 5–200 users; it adds reusable templates, standard integrations, resource and capacity planning, and deeper workflow customisation. Above Business sit Pinnacle and Apex, both priced on request. Pinnacle is aimed at advanced reporting, budgeting and capacity work, while Apex is the enterprise tier, bundling the deepest integration and synchronisation options. There is no published monthly-billing equivalent to lean on for the upper tiers: Business and above are sold only as annual subscriptions, so a team that wants Wrike's distinctive features is committing for a year at a time.

That structure matters more than the two published numbers suggest. A four-person team cannot buy Business at all under the stated 5-user floor without paying for a seat it does not use, and a 16-person team has already outgrown Team's ceiling. The tiers are not a smooth ladder a team climbs one seat at a time; they are bands, and crossing a band boundary changes the per-seat price for everybody, not just for the person who triggered the move. Budgeting for Wrike therefore starts with the band the team will occupy in twelve months, not the headcount on the day of purchase.

Seat bands: the purchase unit is a group, not a person

Wrike sells seats in fixed groups rather than strictly one by one: groups of five up to 30 seats, groups of ten from 30 to 100 seats, and groups of 25 above 100 seats. A team of 27 and a team of 30 can therefore carry the same licence count, while the 31st hire can force the purchase of a whole additional group. This is the single most under-reported line in Wrike budgeting, because per-user calculators quietly assume every seat is both bought and used. Finance teams should model the licence count after rounding up to the next band, then divide by actual headcount to get the true effective rate. A Business team paying for 40 licences with 33 active users is not paying US$25 per person in any meaningful sense; it is paying roughly US$30 per active person, and that effective rate — not the list rate — is the number to compare against alternatives.

The band system also changes renewal behaviour. Shrinking a team does not automatically shrink the bill until the licence count drops below a band boundary, and growing by one or two people can be free if spare licences already exist inside the current band. Before any renewal, count active users, count licences owned, and identify the gap between the two. That gap is either wasted spend to reclaim at renewal or headroom that makes the next hires cost nothing at the margin — and the two situations call for opposite decisions.

Team vs Business: a US$15 gap that buys the distinctive features

The jump from Team at US$10 to Business at US$25 is a 150% increase per seat, and it is where most buying decisions are really made. Team is a competent planning tool: dashboards, Gantt charts, custom fields and basic workflow customisation cover a small team that mainly needs to see and sequence its work. The capabilities that make Wrike Wrike — formal request forms, approvals, proofing, resource and capacity planning, shared real-time reporting and the stronger AI allowance — sit on Business and above. A team buying Team for the Gantt chart is buying sensibly; a team buying Team and then bolting on separate tools for intake, approvals and resourcing has usually under-bought, and the combined bill often exceeds Business.

Run the comparison honestly at 10 users. Team costs 10 × US$10 × 12 = US$1,200 a year. Business costs 10 × US$25 × 12 = US$3,000 a year. The US$1,800 difference is the price of the workflow layer. If that layer replaces even one separate subscription, or removes a recurring manual reporting routine, it can pay for itself; if the team would not use approvals, proofing or capacity planning in a typical month, it is a premium paid for features that sit idle. Neither answer is automatic — but the question has to be asked feature by feature, not settled by the cheaper headline.

Add-ons and AI quotas: the costs outside the seat price

Two categories of spend sit outside the published seat rates. The first is named add-ons — options such as Wrike Lock, Wrike Integrate and Wrike Datahub carry custom pricing and, on the current structure, are associated with the top tier rather than included in Team or Business. A team that needs a specific integration or security add-on may therefore be buying a tier upgrade and an add-on at the same time, and should get both numbers in writing before comparing Wrike with anything else. The second is AI usage. Team carries an AI Essentials allowance and Business and above carry AI Elite actions, with monthly quotas that took effect in April 2026; a team that exceeds its quota can buy an additional AI actions pack or wait for the next cycle. Neither cost appears in a simple per-seat comparison, and both are exactly the kind of per-connection and per-use charge that surprises buyers after signature. List every add-on and every expected AI use before committing, and price the whole stack.

Year-one math at three headcounts

At annual list prices, a 10-person team pays US$1,200 a year on Team or US$3,000 a year on Business. A 25-person team pays US$3,000 on Team — if it still fits the work — or US$7,500 on Business. A 50-person team on Business pays US$15,000 a year before add-ons, before any AI pack purchases, and after rounding licence counts up to the applicable seat band. Set against a mid-tier competitor at US$12–19 per seat, Wrike Business is the premium option in this category; its case rests on depth — proofing, approvals, request handling and resource controls inside one system — not on price. Teams that do not need that depth have cheaper credible homes, several of which are covered elsewhere in these guides. Teams that do need it should still negotiate from the effective per-active-user rate, not the list rate, and should confirm in writing which add-ons are included at the quoted tier.

The decision framework

This is a buying-decision guide, not a setup procedure — the framework below is the process.

  1. Start from the band, not the headcount. Round licences up to the next seat group and compute the effective rate per active user.
  2. Decide Team vs Business feature by feature. Approvals, proofing, request forms and capacity planning are the triggers; if none is used monthly, Team is the honest buy.
  3. Price the add-ons and AI allowance separately. Get Lock, Integrate, Datahub and any AI pack pricing in writing before signing.
  4. Accept the annual commitment consciously. Business and above are annual-only; there is no monthly escape hatch to budget around.
  5. Audit licences before every renewal. Reclaim band headroom when the team shrinks; spend it deliberately when it grows.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Wrike cost per user?

Team is US$10 per user per month and Business is US$25 per user per month, both billed annually. Pinnacle and Apex are quote-only. Seats are sold in fixed groups, so the effective rate per active user can be higher than the list rate.

Is there a free Wrike plan?

Yes. The Free plan covers essential task management for unlimited users, which makes it a reasonable evaluation and light-use tier — but dashboards, Gantt charts and custom fields require Team or above.

Why does Wrike cost more than other project tools?

Its Business tier bundles workflow depth — approvals, proofing, request forms and resource planning — that cheaper tools either lack or sell separately. Whether that depth is worth US$25 a seat depends entirely on whether the team uses it every month.
