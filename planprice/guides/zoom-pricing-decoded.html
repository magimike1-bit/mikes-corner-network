Zoom Pricing Decoded: Workplace Plans, Phone and the Add-On Stack

Published 2026-10-18 · Last updated 2026-10-18

Zoom's seat price is the smallest part of a Zoom bill for most small teams. The plan rates themselves have moved — several comparison pages still quote a Pro price and a cloud storage allowance that Zoom's own pricing page no longer shows — and the real total is decided by the add-ons stacked on top: phone lines, webinar capacity, extra storage. This guide decodes the current official prices, works the year-one math for a ten-person team, and asks the question comparison tables skip: when a team already pays for a suite that includes meetings, what exactly is the second meetings bill buying? All prices are in US dollars, as Zoom publishes them, and were checked on Zoom's official pricing page on 2026-10-18.

General information, not financial advice. Prices change — always confirm the current rates on the vendor's own pricing page before buying.

The four Workplace tiers: dated official prices

Zoom Workplace sells in four tiers. Basic is free: meetings cap at 40 minutes with up to 100 participants, recording is local only, and the AI features are metered — meeting summaries for three hosted meetings a month and three AI note-taking uses. Pro costs US$16.99 per user per month billed monthly, or US$14.16 billed annually (about US$170 per user per year). It lifts meetings to 30 hours, keeps the 100-participant cap, and adds local plus 10 GB of cloud recording storage per user, unlimited meeting summaries and AI note-taking, and live chat support. Business costs US$21.99 monthly or US$18.33 annually (about US$220 per user per year): the participant cap rises to 300, whiteboards become unlimited, and the admin layer arrives — single sign-on, managed domains, an admin portal, company branding, and archiving and data-loss-prevention APIs. Enterprise is quote-only, with 1,000-participant meetings, unlimited cloud storage, a full-featured PBX phone system and 500-attendee webinars bundled in.

Two corrections to what much of the web still says. First, the Pro annual rate is US$14.16, not the US$13.33 figure that older comparisons continue to republish — at 25 seats the stale figure understates the annual bill by roughly US$250. Second, the cloud recording allowance on Pro and Business is now 10 GB per user, not 5 GB. Dated price checks are not pedantry here; Zoom revises this page often enough that any undated table should be treated as a rumour. Annual billing is charged as a single upfront payment, and the saving against monthly billing is worth roughly two months of service — meaningful, but a cash-flow decision, not a free discount.

What Pro actually buys over the free plan

The free plan's constraints are specific, and they define the upgrade triggers precisely. The 40-minute cap ends group meetings mid-conversation; local-only recording means recordings live on one person's laptop; and the AI allowance — three summarised meetings a month — is a demonstration, not a workflow. Pro removes all three constraints at once and adds the pieces a team feels daily: cloud recordings that can be shared by link, unlimited AI meeting summaries and note-taking, polling, co-host controls and recording transcripts. What Pro does not buy is capacity or administration: meetings still cap at 100 participants, there is no single sign-on, and there is no admin portal. A team whose largest regular meeting is a 120-person all-hands is not choosing between Pro and Business on features in general — the participant cap alone settles it.

One structural point protects small budgets: meeting participants never need a licence. Clients, candidates and contractors join free; only people who host and schedule meetings consume paid seats. A ten-person firm where four people host client calls needs four Pro licences, not ten — and licensing occasional hosts is the single largest avoidable line on most small-team Zoom bills. The same logic caps total capacity: every plan allows 100 participants by default, and the Large Meeting add-on raises a licence to as many as 1,000 without a tier change.

The add-on stack: Phone, Webinars and the bundle that beats stacking

Beyond the seat price, three add-ons account for nearly all small-team Zoom overspend. Zoom Phone lists at US$10.50 per user per month for US & Canada Metered calling and US$16.00 for US & Canada Unlimited. Zoom Webinars starts at US$66.67 per month for 300 attendees on the starter plan — and it requires a paid Workplace subscription underneath it, so it is always an addition, never an alternative. Extra cloud recording storage is sold in blocks (US$10 for an additional 30 GB). Each is reasonable alone; stacked uncritically on every seat, they can double the bill.

The bundle is the counter-move. Business Plus combines Zoom Workplace Business with Zoom Phone US & Canada Unlimited at US$24.50 per user per month billed annually (US$29 monthly) for teams of 10 to 250 users. Bought separately on annual billing, the same combination costs US$18.33 plus US$16.00 — US$34.33 per user per month. The bundle therefore saves US$9.83 per user per month, which is US$1,180 a year at ten users, for identical headline components. The mirror-image warning also applies: Business Plus bought for users who never make phone calls is a US$6.17-per-user premium over Business alone, paid for a dial tone nobody uses. Bundle where the phone is real; stack where it is not.

Year-one math for a 10-person team

On annual billing, ten Pro licences cost US$1,699 a year; ten Business licences cost US$2,200. Add Zoom Phone Unlimited to every Business seat bought separately and the total reaches US$4,120 a year — against US$2,940 for the same ten users on Business Plus. A monthly webinar habit adds roughly US$800 a year at the 300-attendee starter tier, before any attendee overage. None of these figures is hidden; all of them are absent from the per-seat number a buyer compares first. Two legitimate reductions belong in the same calculation: verified nonprofits receive 50% off Workplace Pro, Business, Webinars and Large Meetings, and a licence audit — counting who actually hosted a meeting in the last quarter — routinely frees seats that can be dropped at renewal.

The decision framework: pay for Zoom, or use the meetings tool already owned?

This is a buying-decision guide, not a setup procedure — the framework below is the process.

  1. Start with what is already paid for. A team on Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace already owns a meetings tool inside that subscription. Zoom then has to justify itself as a second bill — on meeting quality, client familiarity, webinar capacity or its included AI summaries — not as the default.
  2. Count hosts, not heads. Licence the people who schedule and host; participants join free. Re-count every renewal.
  3. Let the participant cap pick the tier. Over 100 regular attendees means Business or a Large Meeting add-on; SSO and managed domains settle the question the same way.
  4. Bundle phone only where phone is real. Business Plus beats stacking by US$9.83 a user a month — and wastes US$6.17 a user where nobody calls.
  5. Date every number. Zoom revises this pricing page regularly; a quote built on an undated comparison table is how the stale US$13.33 Pro rate keeps entering budgets.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Zoom cost per user in 2026?

From Zoom's official pricing page, checked 2026-10-18: Pro is US$14.16 per user per month billed annually or US$16.99 billed monthly; Business is US$18.33 annually or US$21.99 monthly; Basic is free and Enterprise is quote-only. Older tables showing Pro at US$13.33 are out of date.

Is the free Zoom plan enough for a small team?

For short internal calls, often yes. The binding limits are the 40-minute meeting cap, local-only recording and three AI-summarised meetings a month. The moment meetings regularly run long, or recordings need to be shared, Pro pays for itself in avoided friction.

Does everyone in a meeting need a paid Zoom licence?

No. Only hosts need licences; participants join free without a Zoom account. Small teams should licence their hosts and leave everyone else unlicensed.
