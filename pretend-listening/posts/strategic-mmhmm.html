The Art of the Strategic "Mmhmm"

Core technique · 5 min read

The "mmhmm" is the foundation of the entire pretending-to-listen repertoire. It is a single word that says "I am here, I am with you, and I absolutely followed that plot twist" — whether or not any of those things are true. Like any instrument, it rewards practice. Deployed carelessly, it fools no one. Deployed strategically, it can carry you through a full rehearsal while your mind is, let's say, elsewhere.

The three registers

Every mmhmm lives in one of three registers. Master all three and you can score an entire scene.

1. The Engaged Mmhmm (high pitch, quick). Bright, short, slightly upward. This is your "oh, interesting!" mmhmm. Deploy it when the monologue takes an unexpected turn — a betrayal, a reveal, a sudden prop change. It signals enthusiasm and buys you several minutes of goodwill. Use it sparingly: more than three engaged mmhmms per scene and you sound like a game show audience.

2. The Cruising Mmhmm (neutral pitch, relaxed). The workhorse. Flat, warm, unhurried. This is the mmhmm of the long middle of the scene, the part where your kid is explaining the backstory of a character you met six rehearsals ago and have never once retained. The cruising mmhmm says "keep going, I'm tracking it" and asks nothing of anyone. It is the cargo van of mmhmms: not glamorous, carries the whole load.

3. The Thoughtful Mmhmm (low pitch, slow). Deep, deliberate, almost a hum. Reserve this for the dramatic moments — the pause before the big line, the emotional beat, the part where the music would swell if this had a budget. The thoughtful mmhmm suggests you are processing. It implies depth. Paired with a slow nod (see our nod timing guide), it can make a parent who is thinking about grocery lists look like a parent moved by art.

Placement: where the mmhmm lands matters more than the mmhmm itself

A mmhmm dropped at the wrong moment is worse than silence. Three placement rules:

  • Never interrupt the run-up. When your kid is building to the big line, stay quiet. Your job is the floor, not the spotlight. The mmhmm lands after the line lands — on the exhale of the scene, when they glance at you for confirmation.
  • Mind the two-beat delay. A mmhmm that fires instantly every time sounds like an auto-reply. Let one or two beats pass, as if you're taking it in. This tiny pause is the difference between "convincing" and "clearly an NPC."
  • Match the scene's energy. Fast, funny scene: keep mmhmms light and quick. Slow, sad scene: go low and slow. Nothing exposes a faker faster than a cheerful "mmhmm!" during the monologue about the lost dog.

The danger zone: the rising "mmhmm?"

Occasionally you'll be tempted to add a question mark — "mmhmm?" — to suggest you're prompting them onward. This is advanced territory and frankly dangerous. A rising mmhmm invites a follow-up question, and a follow-up question requires actual listening. If your comprehension of the current scene is below 40%, keep the pitch flat and the mouth closed. Do not open conversational doors you cannot walk through.

Plausible deniability: the supporting cast

The mmhmm never works alone. It needs a supporting ensemble:

  • Eye contact, loosely held. Look at their face, not the phone in your lap. You can let your eyes glaze at medium distance; just don't let them slide to the side where the actual phone lives.
  • The settled posture. Sit like someone who plans to stay. Shifting around reads as boredom. A parent sunk into the couch with a cushion is a parent who appears committed to the performance.
  • The occasional eyebrow. One raised eyebrow during a plot twist does the work of five mmhmms. Use it once per scene, maximum. Two eyebrows is surprise; one is intrigue. Know the difference.

What never to do

Don't stack mmhmms — "mmhmm, mmhmm, mmhmm" is the sound of someone stalling, and your kid knows it. Don't mmhmm during your own yawn. And never, ever deploy the thoughtful low mmhmm at a joke; laughter misfires are how parents get caught.

The golden rule

The mmhmm is a seasoning, not the meal. It exists to fill the spaces between the real moments of attention you're still giving — because you are giving some. A well-placed mmhmm doesn't replace listening; it just papers over the gaps while your brain files the evening's logistics. Master the three registers, respect the placement rules, and you'll survive the rehearsal season with your reputation as an attentive parent fully intact. Mostly intact. Intact enough.
