Surviving the Complaint About the Sibling: A Full Hearing, No Ruling

Family tactics · 6 min read

Short answer first: most sibling complaints do not want a verdict. Expert guidance on tattling puts it at 90 percent of the time — the child wants the parent to listen, be outraged on their behalf, and do nothing. Read that again, because it rewrites the whole job. The long presentation heading your way, complete with background, exhibits, and a dramatic reconstruction of who touched whose stuff first, is not a case filed for judgement. It is a request for a hearing. Your role is the bench, the gallery, and the official expression of disbelief — and then, crucially, no sentence is passed.

Stage one: the opening presentation

The complaint arrives at speed, usually mid-stride, and the first ten seconds decide how long it runs. Dismissing it is the one opening move the guidance rules out: do not wave the complaint away, because a dismissed child does not drop the case — the case gets louder and comes back with supporting witnesses. Take the opposite posture. The recommended position for the parent is mediator, not hired gun: present, attentive, and visibly not yet owned by either side.

So let the opening run. Interruptions at this stage are read as rulings, and an early ruling in the other child's favour is the fastest known way to turn one complaint into two. Your contributions during the presentation are small and structural: stay in the room, keep your face in listening mode, and let the teller reach the end of the story they walked in carrying.

Stage two: the evidence, heard in full

Mid-complaint is where reflective listening earns its place. The goal, as the sibling-rivalry guidance frames it, is not to take sides or solve the problem — it is to make the child feel understood. Reflecting back what was just said, in plain words, does that job without awarding anyone a victory: “So she took the good scissors, hid them, and then said you were the one who lost them.” Nothing in that sentence rules on scissors law. Everything in it tells the teller the evidence was entered into the record.

One practical note travels with the technique: reflective listening works best when it has been practised outside the heat of the moment. A calm kitchen-table grumble is training; a full-volume doorway incident is the match. Parents who have rehearsed the phrases on small complaints find them available for large ones, which is also roughly how the approach's roots in Ross Greene's collaborative work are usually described — understanding first, solutions later, if solutions are needed at all.

Hearing versus ruling: the comparison that matters

The moveA hearing sounds likeA ruling sounds like
Opening“Tell me the whole thing.”“What did you do?”
Middle“She hid them and blamed you. That is maddening.”“Well, did you take her charger yesterday?”
EmotionOutrage on the teller's behalf, warmly deliveredA verdict on who started it, delivered to both parties
Close“How could you two work this out?”“Right. Both of you, no screens.”

The right-hand column is not evil. It is simply a different product, and it is rarely the product that was ordered. The documented motive says the order, nine times out of ten, is the left-hand column: a full hearing, emotional solidarity, and no action. Supply what was ordered and the customer usually leaves satisfied.

Stage three: the favouritism audit

Every hearing is audited. The teller is watching for signs that the other sibling gets a softer bench, and the charge, when it comes, is some version of “you always take her side.” Handle this one with care, because the listening guidance carries a specific warning: defending yourself against a favouritism charge validates the child's fear rather than calming it. Arguing “I do not, I am completely fair” lands as confirmation that fairness needed defending.

The steadier tool is Say What You See: narrate the observable facts of the complaint, without verdict or self-defence. “You came to tell me straight away. You are angry about the scissors. You think I will believe her first.” Facts, stated calmly, give the fear nowhere to grow. The teller hears their own case described accurately, which is the one form of fairness a child can actually inspect.

Closing lines that end it kindly

A hearing needs an ending, and the ending should match the motive. Three closers, in rising order of involvement:

The outrage close. “Hidden scissors and you got blamed? Outrageous. I would be furious too.” This supplies the documented main request — solidarity — and many complaints simply dissolve at this point, their business complete.

The hand-back close. Use the mediator's question from the guidance: “How could you work this out?” It returns the problem to its owners, kindly, and signals that the bench trusts them with it. Some pairs actually go and work it out, which feels like a clerical error the first time it happens.

The adjournment close. When the teller is too wound up for either of the above: “I have heard the whole thing, and I am not forgetting it. Let us let it sit while we have lunch.” Adjournment is not dismissal — the record stays open, the hearing was real, and lunch is allowed to be lunch.

The sequel complaints

There will be a sequel. Possibly before lunch. Repeat complaints are not evidence that the hearing failed; they are evidence that the hearing is the household's trusted court, and trusted courts get repeat business. Keep the procedure identical across siblings and across sequels — same full hearing, same outrage where outrage is owed, same refusal to rule — because the audit never stops, and consistency across cases is the only fairness a parent can demonstrate. The teller changes, the scissors change, the bench does not. That steadiness, repeated often enough, is what turns a complaint into a five-minute ritual instead of a household trial: heard in full, sided with emotionally, ruled for nobody, closed with kindness.
