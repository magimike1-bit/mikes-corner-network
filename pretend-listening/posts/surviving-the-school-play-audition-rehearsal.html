Surviving the School Play: Audition Season and the Hundredth Rehearsal

Family tactics · 6 min read

The kitchen has become a theatre again. Dinner is at the warming stage, a backpack is blocking the fridge, and from the doorway comes the opening number — the same opening number, at full volume, for what the programme notes in your head record as rehearsal number forty. The performer finishes, looks up, and asks the question that makes this different from every other monologue in the house: “Was that good?” Not “did you hear me.” Good. A verdict has been requested, the cook is also the audience, and the honest answer involves one steady verse, one brave high note, and a middle section that wandered off somewhere around the second cupboard.

School play season is hard on listeners for a reason most parenting advice never names: repetition. The standard guidance is built for single, important conversations. Rehearsal is the opposite shape — the same lines and songs, night after night, with an audition at the end of them and a child checking your face for news each time. The stamina problem is the whole problem.

What your face is doing while they sing

Start with the uncomfortable documented fact: children detect half-listening. Guidance on listening to children is blunt about putting distractions away, because a child who senses a divided audience tends to shut down rather than compete with the phone. That does not mean staging a gala performance of attention at the stove. It means the cheap signals have to be real ones. The small responses count here: a well-placed “Yes,” “I see,” or “Really” tells a speaker you are tuned in, and those three short words survive contact with a rehearsal better than any grand speech about how proud everyone is.

Your face has a second job during a song, which is to avoid filing a report the singer did not ask for yet. A wince at the high note is a review. So is a glance at the clock during the slow verse. Keep the face neutral-warm, save the commentary for the pause, and let the performer finish the run. Interrupting a rehearsal to fix it is a temptation the listening guidance warns against in conversation generally: reflect and validate first, and resist the urge to fix everything immediately. A rehearsal is not a problem to solve between verses. It is a child practising being heard.

Encouragement that is honest: praise a moment, not a destiny

The wobbly audition tape question — “how did I sound, honestly?” — defeats most parents because the available answers feel like a choice between a lie and a bruise. There is a third drawer. Honest encouragement comments on things that are checkable: effort, a specific moment, and change since the last rehearsal. None of those requires a verdict on talent, and none of them can be contradicted by the recording.

Comment typeExample lineWhy it holds up
A specific moment“The second verse was steadier than yesterday — you kept going right through the hard line.”It names something the singer can verify and repeat
Effort, plainly stated“You have run that song every night this week. That is the work showing.”True regardless of how any single run sounded
A question instead of a verdict“Which part felt best to you that time?”Hands the judging to the performer, where auditions will put it anyway
A prophecy about the outcome“You are definitely getting the lead.”Avoid this row. It is a promise no listener controls, and casting day collects on it

Notice what the good rows share: they are short. A parent who delivers a paragraph of praise sounds like a press release, and young performers can hear the difference between a person and a press release at forty paces. One true sentence, delivered at normal volume, carries further than a speech.

A quick framework before you answer “was that good?”

Three checks, run in the pause while the performer catches breath:

1. Can it be pointed at? If the comment names a moment, a line, or a change, it is safe ground. If it floats above the whole performance — “amazing,” “perfect” — it will be cross-examined, and vague praise always loses cross-examination.

2. Is it feedback, or is it fixing? A singer who asked how a song sounded is owed a response to the song. A list of corrections is a different product, delivered by directors, at rehearsals that are not the kitchen. Validate first; fix only when fixing is actually requested.

3. Would it still be true on a bad day? Effort and specific moments stay true even when a run wobbles. Outcome predictions do not. Choose the sentence that survives the wobbliest tape in the house.

Audition week: the retellings multiply

As the audition approaches, the rehearsal escapes the kitchen. Children open up in low-eye-contact moments — car rides and bedtime are the classic settings — and audition nerves follow the same route. Expect the full debrief in the passenger seat: who else is trying out, which song the rival chose, the exact expression on a teacher's face at a moment you did not witness. These retellings look like information and behave like rehearsals. The child is practising saying the scary thing out loud, to the safest audience available, in a car where nobody has to hold eye contact.

Treat the car debrief with the short responses and save the longer encouragement for solid ground at home. A “Really” at a red light is not a failure of parenting theatre. It is the correct instrument for a moving vehicle, and it leaves the singer still talking, which is the point of the exercise.

Small mercies when patience runs out

Two documented mercies belong in every rehearsal-season kitchen. The first is humour, framed in the parenting literature as a secret weapon for diffusing tension: a household that can laugh about rehearsal number forty — with the performer, about the situation, never at the singing — lowers the temperature for everyone, audience included. Request the encore of the funny verse. Applaud the dramatic bow. Let the season be a family joke with a cast of one very committed singer.

The second mercy is the reset. When patience genuinely runs out, a short parent timeout is the recommended move, and it beats the alternative, which is a sharp word delivered at full volume to a child holding a song sheet. Step out, breathe, return for the next run. Rotation works the same way at the household level: another adult takes the front row for a night, the performer gets a fresh audience, and the regular listener returns tomorrow with a face that can be believed again. Nobody's encouragement survives being mined to exhaustion. Protect the supply, and rehearsal forty-one gets the audience the first one did.
