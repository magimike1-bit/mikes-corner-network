Surviving the Show and Tell Rehearsal: Three Full Run Throughs Before Breakfast

Rehearsal tactics · 6 min read

What exactly has the parent in the kitchen chair been hired to do? Not to write the talk. Not to direct it. Show and tell, in the guidance this piece leans on, combines choosing an object, remembering a few ideas, and speaking while classmates watch, and the early years foundation behind it is plain: children develop communication through responsive speaking and listening, not through being pushed towards a flawless performance.

Flawless is not the assignment. The assignment, for the child, is a short familiar story told out loud. The assignment, for you, is to be the room it gets told in, as many times as the teller requires.

That sounds restful until the second full run through begins, identical to the first in every particular except that you now know precisely when the long pause is coming. The rehearsal has a shape, and the shape is worth learning, because a parent who can name the phase they are in complains less about the phase they are in. The route a child can own has four parts: name the object and hold it where people can see, say where it came from or why it matters, share one interesting detail, and finish with a question classmates might ask. Four parts. One object. Your whole evening.

Run through one: the object, held high

The first run through is a sizing exercise. Practice guidance suggests beginning with one part only, such as naming the object, and stopping after one calm attempt, then adding the next part on another day. Real living rooms rarely enjoy that schedule, because show and tell is tomorrow, and so the parts arrive stacked. Still, the principle survives the compression: the first attempt is for getting the object named and held where people can see it, and a parent who grades attempt one as if it were the final performance has misunderstood the calendar.

Your visible duties in this phase are modest. Sit where the object can be held up to you. Look at the object when it is held up, which is harder than it sounds by the third showing, because familiarity breeds a glazed nod that fools nobody over the age of five. Respond to the meaning first. The detail about where the object came from is the content. The wobble in the delivery is weather.

Run through two: the because sentences

By the second run through, reasons start arriving. One parent account of preparing a first show and tell describes asking why throughout the preparation, on the theory that an opinion sentence should carry the word because inside it: the snake is cool because it is powerful, and so on. The same account leaned on a rule of three borrowed from public speaking and writing, the idea that people pay more attention when three items are grouped together. Three reasons, three facts, three anything. The parent in the chair will hear all three, in order, possibly in the wrong order, and then again in the right one.

This is the run through where directing urges peak. A reason is missing. A fact has wandered. The object is now being held at an angle no classmate will ever see. Resist the promotion. The preparation trap list is short and it is aimed at you: writing a long script for the child, correcting every word during a practice turn, and choosing the object because it seems impressive. Notice that two of the three traps are audience failures. The script and the corrections are what happens when the listener quietly takes over the show.

Run through three: standing, no script

The later run throughs shed equipment. Speech practice guidance describes rehearsing aloud several times with a timer keeping the talk to 1 to 3 minutes, with gestures, eye contact, and deep breaths in the mix.

One parent account describes a child who, after reading a typed text aloud twice, practised standing against a wall addressing an imaginary audience with no clipboard and no cue cards. The aim in that account was explicit: not memorisation, but remembering the basic facts and sharing them comfortably, even if the words come out differently each time.

Hold on to that sentence, because it is the licence for everything your face needs to do next. Different words each time means the run through you are hearing is not a failed copy of the previous one. It is the exercise working. Preschoolers aged 4 to 6 manage very short show and tell style talks, and children aged 7 to 11 prepare speeches of 1 to 3 minutes, which means the whole production, at full length, is shorter than the average discussion about whether to have another run through. You can afford to watch it like it matters. It is, measurably, nearly over.

The watching column is not passivity dressed up for print. Feedback guidance from the Iowa Reading Research Center, written for reading but portable to any practice turn, puts specificity at the centre: children who struggle are quick to detect cheerful but insincere praise, and useful feedback names the exact behaviour that helped. “Great job” evaporates. “You held it up the whole time you were talking about it” stays in the room, because it is true, and because the child watched you notice it.

The one note rule: a decision check between run throughs

Sooner or later a run through ends and the teller looks at you, and the look asks a question with real consequences: was that all right, and what should change? The same Iowa guidance offers a ratio worth stealing outright. It is not necessary to correct every error. Choose one correction for every three positives identified, and focus that one correction on the most important point that will help progress. Delivered matter of factly, in a neutral tone, a correction is information. Delivered as a bundle, it is a review the teller never applied for.

Run the check before you speak.

Can the note be said in one sentence? If it needs a paragraph, it is a lesson, and lessons are for another day. Is it about the talk, or about your taste? A fact that went missing belongs to the talk. Your preference for a different object belongs to you, and the trap list has already claimed it.

Would the note still help if it were the only one? If three positives and this one note were the entire review, the rehearsal can end on it. If the honest answer is that the note mainly relieves your feelings as a witness, keep it. The teller has a class to face. You have a chair, and the chair is winning.

The invented classmate question, and the kind end

Every rehearsal route ends the same way, with a question classmates might ask. In the living room, this part has a second function nobody warns you about: the teller invents the question, answers it at length, and discovers, mid answer, a whole section that could be improved, which means the run throughs begin again under new management. Treat the invented question as part of the show, because structurally it is. A final run for family, with positive feedback, is a recognised closing step in speech practice, and the question round is simply that step wearing a disguise.

Answer the invented question as a classmate would: briefly, and with genuine curiosity, and then let the evening close on the strongest full run through rather than the most recent one.

One honest boundary belongs here. If difficulty with speaking persists across situations, causes distress, or affects everyday communication, the sourced advice is to seek individual guidance from the school, a doctor, or a qualified speech and language therapist. That is a different conversation from rehearsal fatigue, and it deserves better than a humour column.

For the ordinary night before, though, the work is done when the object has been named, the reasons have landed, and the audience of one has stayed an audience to the end. Put the object somewhere safe. Tomorrow it performs without you, which was the point of every run through you just survived.

— Grace Okafor, Family & Parenting Writer
