ABC analysis for retail shelves: sorting SKUs so the important ones get the attention

Ordering & stock levels · 7 min read

Most small stores manage every product the same way: the same glance, the same reorder habit, the same shelf treatment. But the products are not equal. A small share of a typical range produces most of the value moving through the store, while a long tail of lines barely moves at all. ABC analysis is the simple sorting exercise that makes that imbalance visible — and once it is visible, a retailer can stop spreading attention evenly and start spending it where it pays.

What ABC analysis actually tells a small store

ABC analysis applies the Pareto principle — the familiar 80/20 pattern — to a product list. Lightspeed's ABC guide describes the usual shape: A items are roughly 10–20% of the range contributing about 70–80% of the value, B items are roughly 20–30% contributing about 15–25%, and C items are the remaining 50–70% contributing only about 5–10%. Effective Inventory Management, writing about rank-based counting, puts the concentration even more sharply: in practice only about 10–13% of items often account for 80% of sales, and no more than about half of all products account for 95% of sales. The exact percentages vary by store. The pattern — a steep head and a long, thin tail — is what matters.

The point of sorting is not the labels. It is that each tier then gets a deliberately different policy: tight control where an error is expensive, and a light touch where an error barely registers. A stockout on an A line costs real sales every day it lasts. A stockout on a C line costs very little, and overstocking that same C line in the name of safety quietly parks cash on a shelf.

Run the classification in a spreadsheet

The standard basis for ABC is annual consumption value: the units sold in a year multiplied by the unit cost. Lightspeed defines it exactly that way, and Atomix Logistics' step-by-step walkthrough uses the same method: pull twelve months of unit sales by SKU, multiply by unit cost, sort descending, and add a running cumulative percentage of total value. Twelve months matters because it smooths seasonality instead of letting one strong month promote the wrong products.

  1. Export one row per SKU with units sold in the last twelve months and the unit cost. If the point-of-sale system cannot rank products, a spreadsheet does the whole job — no special software is required.
  2. Add a value column: units × unit cost for each row. This is the annual consumption value.
  3. Sort the sheet by that value, highest first.
  4. Add a cumulative percentage column: each row's running total of value divided by the sheet's total value, expressed as a percentage.
  5. Draw the tier lines on the cumulative column. A common convention is A up to about 80% of cumulative value, B from there to about 95%, and C for the remainder. Treat 80 and 95 as starting lines, not laws — a very small range may need the A line lower so the tier stays manageable.
  6. Sanity-check the edges before acting on the result (see the edge cases below). The sort is arithmetic; the judgment is the retailer's.

Choosing the ranking basis

Annual consumption value is the standard basis because the exercise decides where control effort and cash should concentrate. But it is not the only view, and the choice changes the answer. Atomix's walkthrough separates three views clearly:

Ranking basisWhat it showsBest used for
Annual consumption value (units × cost)Where working capital sitsCounting, control, and stock-level policy
RevenueWhat sellsA quick first pass when cost data is unreliable
Gross margin dollarsWhat actually funds the businessMerchandising and range decisions

Running two views and comparing them is where the interesting decisions live. A line that ranks A by revenue but C by margin is busy without being profitable — a candidate for a price review or a smaller facing, not for more control effort. One caution from practitioners: movement is not the same as profit. A long-running discussion among independent game-store retailers on the RPGnet forums makes exactly this point — volume alone ignores what it costs to carry the product — and lands on a practical rule of thumb: always protect the A movers, choose B lines deliberately, and consider making the weakest C lines special-order only.

One prerequisite sits under all of this: the ranking is only as good as the item data behind it. Clean, consistent item records — real costs, real units, no duplicate SKUs splitting one product's sales across two rows — are what make the sort trustworthy. An hour spent merging duplicates before the export saves a misleading result afterward.

What each tier gets: shelf and review policies

  • A items — protect availability. These earn the best shelf positions and full facings, a review every week, and the most frequent counts. Published cadences for A items range from weekly or every two weeks (Atomix) to six counts a year as a minimum rhythm (Effective Inventory Management) — a small store should pick the frequent end it can actually sustain. An A stockout is the most expensive empty space in the store.
  • B items — manage steadily. Standard shelf positions, a monthly review, and counts on a monthly cycle or at least twice a year. B items matter, but they do not need daily attention; steady beats intense.
  • C items — simplify. Smaller facings or a single home location, a quarterly review, and counts quarterly or twice a year. Order C lines in fewer, larger drops to cut ordering effort, and let the weakest of them become special-order or be delisted at range reviews. The savings in C are mostly time and shelf space, not unit cost.

The edge cases that break the math

A mechanical sort misclassifies a predictable set of products. Atomix's walkthrough flags the main ones: a product launched six weeks ago will look artificially small against twelve months of history, and a discontinued line can look artificially large on old sales. Handle them explicitly:

  • New lines: judge them on their selling rate since launch, not their trailing total, and promote the ones clearly selling at an A pace.
  • Seasonal lines: a product that earns its year in eight weeks should be classified on its in-season rate, then demoted out of season — not averaged into invisibility.
  • Discontinued lines: remove them from the ranking entirely; they belong in a clearance decision, not in a tier.
  • Loss leaders and basket builders: a cheap line that pulls customers toward A purchases can justify B treatment even on C numbers. Record the override and the reason, so next year's sort does not silently reverse it.

Keeping the tiers current

Tiers decay. A promotion, a competitor closing, or a shift in taste moves products between tiers within a quarter. Re-run the classification on a fixed rhythm — quarterly is realistic for most small stores, and the rerun takes minutes once the spreadsheet exists. Compare the new tiers against the old ones and look specifically at the movers: a B line climbing toward A needs its stock level and shelf space adjusted before it starts stocking out, and an A line sliding down is an early warning that its space could soon earn more elsewhere.

What success looks like: every SKU in the store carries a current A, B, or C label from a dated spreadsheet run; A lines have a named review day and rarely sit empty; C lines occupy visibly less space and attention than they did before the first sort; and the tier list is re-run on schedule rather than whenever someone remembers. The analysis takes an afternoon. The policies are what pay for it, week after week.

FAQ

Should the ranking use revenue or cost value? For stock control, annual consumption value — units multiplied by unit cost — is the standard basis, because it shows where working capital sits. Revenue is an acceptable first pass; margin dollars are the better lens for range decisions. Comparing two views is more informative than either alone.

How often should each tier be counted? Published cadences put A items at weekly to every two weeks, B items monthly, and C items quarterly or twice a year. The right cadence is the most frequent one the store can sustain without the counts slipping.

What if the store's split is nothing like 80/20? That is normal, especially in a small range. The percentages in the guides are a starting reference, not a law — use the cumulative curve the store's own data produces and draw the lines where they keep the A tier small enough to manage tightly.
