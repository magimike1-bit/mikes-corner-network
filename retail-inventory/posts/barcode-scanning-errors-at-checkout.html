Barcode scanning errors at checkout: causes and fixes, sorted by where the fault actually lives

Checkout operations · 8 min read

Every checkout scan failure looks the same from the customer's side of the counter: a beep that does not come, a cashier turning the package over, a queue quietly lengthening. From the store's side, though, "it won't scan" is three different problems wearing one coat. The scanner may be unable to read the symbol at all. The symbol may read perfectly and still be rejected because the point-of-sale item file does not know the code. Or the code and the file may both be fine while the scanner itself — its window, its settings, its enabled symbologies — drops the read. Each class has different causes, different fixes, and a different owner. Treating them as one problem is why some stores have been "fixing the barcodes" for a year without the failures going down.

Class 1: the scanner cannot decode the symbol

A scanner does not see a barcode the way a person does. GS1's guidance is blunt about the mechanism: typical scanners read with a beam of red light and detect the contrast between bars and spaces, and a contrast that looks perfectly satisfactory to the human eye may be insufficient for the scanner. The optimum combination is black bars on a white background — dark colours for the bars, light backgrounds for the spaces and the quiet zones around the symbol. Most no-read failures are this physics asserting itself through one of a short list of label faults:

  • Damage and wear. Scratches, smudges, tears, condensation, and freezer burn on the label break up the bars and spaces the scanner is trying to measure. Cold-storage and chilled goods are repeat offenders because the film of moisture arrives with the product.
  • Contrast killers. Pale bars on a tinted background, bars printed in a colour the red beam cannot separate from the background, and glossy finishes all starve the scanner of contrast. GS1 notes that high-gloss substrates change reflectance and that transparent over-wraps can reduce contrast — the check that matters is on the finished, wrapped package, not the bare label.
  • Quiet zone violations. The blank space before the first bar and after the last is part of the symbol: GS1 requires quiet zones on both sides for a successful scan, and GS1-based artwork guidance sets the minimum at 2.5 mm of clear space on each side, with no text, lines, folds, or background patterns intruding. A price sticker or a design element parked in that space can make a well-printed code unreadable.
  • Size out of range. EAN/UPC symbols have a magnification range — 80% at the smallest, 200% at the largest, per GS1 specifications. A label shrunk to fit a small package, or stretched by a printer scaling error, falls outside what the scanner expects. Shortening the bars to save space (truncation) is its own trap: GS1 warns that reducing a symbol's height has a negative impact on readability for the omnidirectional scanners used at checkouts, and should be the last resort.
  • Placement. A code printed across a seam, around a curve, or under a closure cannot present all its bars to the scanner at once. GS1-based guidance puts the symbol on a flat, scannable area — ideally the lower right of the back of the package — and specifically not across seams, curves, or closures.
  • The wrong symbol for the till. Not every barcode belongs at a checkout. EAN/UPC symbols are the ones designed for the retail point-of-sale and its high-volume scanning; ITF-14 and GS1-128 case codes are distribution symbols and are not applied at the POS. A cashier fighting a case code on an outer carton is not looking at a faulty barcode — the sellable unit's own code is usually on the inner pack.

Class 2: the symbol decodes, but the item file says no

The second class announces itself differently: the scanner beeps, the code is read, and the till responds with "not on file", "item not found", or an inventory system's "invalid scan". That message does not mean the barcode failed. As inventory-system troubleshooting guidance puts it, an invalid scan means the system received the scan but could not match it to any item, bin, or order — a data problem, not a reading problem. This is a large, measurable failure class, not an edge case: retailers in ECR Retail Loss's 2025 working-group discussion reported "not on file" incidents ranging from fewer than five per store per day to over eighteen, with some chains reaching 500,000 incidents a month across their networks. The usual causes in a small store:

  • The item was never set up. New lines, seasonal stock, and one-off buys get shelved before anyone adds them to the item file. ECR's retailers found produce — especially items with multiple barcode variations from different suppliers — the most common offender, with seasonal items close behind, sometimes failing because the barcode sits awkwardly on a foil wrapper.
  • The product carries more than one valid code. A product can legitimately have several barcodes — a UPC and an EAN, or the manufacturer's code and an internal one. If the file holds only one of them, the other scans as a stranger. Inventory systems handle this with an additional-barcodes field: the extra codes are recorded against the same item rather than forced in as duplicates.
  • Two products share one code. Barcodes must be unique per item, and repackaged, bundled, or private-label goods genuinely do arrive sharing a code. The fix is the same additional-barcodes route, plus a decision about which product the shared code primarily means — not two item records fighting over one number.
  • The digits under the bars disagree with the bars. The human-readable numbers printed beneath a symbol must match the GTIN the symbol encodes. When they do not — a supplier artwork error — staff keying the printed digits and staff scanning the bars get two different items, and the discrepancy looks like a stock error.

Class 3: the scanner or its configuration is the fault

The third class is the one stores check last and should check early. A scanner has per-symbology settings, and some ship with length limits: scanner configuration guidance notes that some devices default to a maximum of 13 digits for EAN/GTIN codes, so a 14-digit code scans as invalid until that symbology is enabled in the scanner's profile. Add the unglamorous physical causes — a scratched or dirty scanner window, a failing cable, a presentation scanner whose field no longer covers where cashiers actually present items — and a meaningful share of "bad barcode" complaints turn out to be one lane's hardware. The tell is pattern: if failures cluster on one till and the same items scan cleanly on another, the barcode is innocent.

The five-minute triage procedure

When an item fails at the till, work the classes in order. The point is to isolate which home the fault lives in before anyone starts fixing things:

  1. Try the item on a second scanner. Scans there? The fault is class 3 — the first scanner, its window, or its configuration. Clean the window, then check the symbology settings before blaming any label.
  2. Key the human-readable digits in by hand. If the till finds the item from the typed digits but not from the scan, the item file is fine and the symbol itself is at fault — class 1. Inspect the label against the class-1 list: damage, contrast, quiet zone, size, placement.
  3. If the typed digits also come back unknown, it is class 2. The item file does not hold this code. Check for a sibling code — search the item by name and see which barcode the file does hold — and record the new code as an additional barcode on the existing item instead of creating a duplicate item.
  4. Log it before the queue moves on. Date, till, item, the digits, and the class. One line is enough. Several of the retailers in ECR's discussion admitted they lacked robust tracking of not-on-file items, which made the problem impossible to quantify — and therefore impossible to argue about with a supplier. A small store's log can be a notebook at the till; what matters is that it exists.
  5. Clear the backlog weekly. Work the log: set up the missing items, add the additional barcodes, flag the repeat label offenders to the supplier. A scanning problem that is only ever worked around at the till is never actually fixed.

What success looks like: any failed scan gets sorted into label, scanner, or item file within a couple of minutes; the same item never fails twice for a class-2 reason, because its code went into the file the first time; and the till log shrinks week over week instead of becoming part of the furniture.

Fix it at receiving, not at the till

The cheapest scan check happens when the goods arrive, with the carton open and the supplier's paperwork still on the counter. Three habits do most of the work. First, scan one of every new line at receiving — if the code is not in the item file, it gets added while the delivery note is still in hand, not discovered by a customer. Second, run a label check on new suppliers and new packaging against measurable criteria: magnification inside the 80–200% range, quiet zones clear on both sides, dark bars on a light background, the symbol flat and away from seams, and the printed digits matching the encoded GTIN. A label that fails those checks is a conversation with the supplier that week — ECR's retailers likewise stressed working with suppliers on barcode accuracy, particularly for private-label goods, which are the codes nobody else will fix for you. Third, test through the packaging: a code that scans bare but fails through shrink-wrap or a gloss finish is a class-1 failure waiting for its first customer, and GS1's advice is to check the completed package for exactly that reason.

Why the workaround is the expensive part

A scan failure costs a minute at the till. The workaround costs inventory accuracy. When a cashier cannot scan an item, the path of least resistance is to key the price manually or ring it under a generic department code — and ECR's retailer discussion names the consequence directly: doing so distorts inventory records and reduces visibility of the real problem at the same time. The sale is recorded, but no unit leaves any item's stock count. The item that actually sold still shows as on hand, its reorder point never triggers, and the next stock count surfaces a shortage that gets written down as shrinkage — theft that never happened, created by a barcode. Stores that take scanning seriously are not being fussy about beeps; they are protecting the one input every other inventory number depends on. Keep the manual override for genuine emergencies, log every use of it against the item, and treat the log as the real defect list: it is the only place the store's scanning problems are written down.

FAQ

The same product scans at one till but not another. What is broken? Almost never the product. Failures that cluster on one lane point at that scanner — a dirty or damaged window, or a configuration difference such as a symbology length limit that rejects longer codes. Test the known-good scanner's settings against the failing one.

An item scans but shows the wrong price. Which class is that? A class-2 variant: the code decoded and matched an item, but the item file's price is stale or the code is shared with another product. Check whether two products are carrying the same barcode, and whether the price change reached the item file — the label read fine.

Can a store fix a supplier's bad labels itself? For goods the store sells under the supplier's code, the durable fix is the supplier's artwork; the store's own tools are detection and pressure — the receiving label check, the till log, and evidence in the form of counts. For the store's own labels and private-label goods, the GS1 criteria above (size range, quiet zones, contrast, flat placement, digits matching the code) are the standard to print to.

Is keying the number in really that harmful? Keying the item's own digits is fine — the sale still records against the item. The harm is in keying a bare price or using a generic department code, which records money but no unit sold. That is the workaround that quietly corrupts counts and reorder points.
