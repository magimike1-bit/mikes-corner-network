Choosing inventory software for a small shop: spreadsheet, app, or POS system

Software decisions · 7 min read

The usual advice says a spreadsheet is a beginner's tool a serious shop outgrows. That is not quite true, and the correction matters because it changes what you buy. A spreadsheet is a legitimate inventory system inside a narrow band: few products, one person editing, one place where stock is sold. Outside that band it does not get gradually worse — it fails at specific, predictable points. The buying question is therefore not "which product is best?" but "which of the three tiers is my shop actually in?" There are two quick tests that answer it, and then a cost comparison that is honest about what each tier charges beyond the sticker price.

The three tiers, honestly described

Spreadsheet. A stock list in a spreadsheet, updated by hand. Published comparisons put its comfort zone at under roughly 50 SKUs, a single location, and low volume, at a cost of $0. Inside that zone it is not a compromise; it is the right tool, because every alternative charges money to solve problems the shop does not have.

Dedicated inventory app. A cloud tool whose only job is stock: item records, counts, and adjustments, edited from more than one device. Cloud all-in-one tools in this tier run from $0 to $29 a month in the pricing roundups, with the free end of that range being where most small shops start.

POS system with inventory. The till and the stock file are the same system, so every sale decrements the count at the moment it happens. Retail POS-plus-inventory entry pricing starts at $69 a month and up in the same roundups — before the card-processing fees and hardware that ride along with a till, which the next sections count separately.

One caution applies to every number in this piece: plans change and prices shift, so confirm current pricing on the vendor's own page before deciding. The tier boundaries move more slowly than the prices do, which is why the tests come first.

Test one: SKUs, editors, channels

Three questions, answered with the shop's real numbers:

  • How many SKUs? Under roughly 50, a spreadsheet holds up. Well past that, the list itself becomes the workload — finding, checking, and correcting rows by eye.
  • How many people edit the stock record? This is where spreadsheets fail first, ahead of size. Shops describing the switch report the same pattern: with multiple editors come version conflicts and overwritten data — two copies of the truth, and no way to tell which one a count was checked against.
  • How many channels sell the stock? One counter is one list. Add an online storefront or a market stall and the spreadsheet has to be reconciled against each channel by hand, because it has no integrations to do it automatically.

Fail any one of the three and the spreadsheet tier is done, whatever the other answers say. The failure mode users describe as the tipping point is consistent: spreadsheet errors start costing more time to fix than a subscription would cost to prevent them.

Test two: scanning and channel sync

The second test is about movement rather than size. If receiving, counting, or selling involves scanning barcodes, the spreadsheet tier is out on its own: it offers no barcode scanning workflow, so scanned work has to be keyed in anyway, which removes the point of scanning. If an online channel must draw from the same stock numbers as the shop floor, the same applies — sync is an integration, and integrations are exactly what a spreadsheet does not have. A shop that scans and sells in one place only can still stop at the app tier; a shop whose till must be the stock record — because staff sell from the same numbers all day — is describing the POS tier.

One-year total cost, tier by tier

The advertised monthly price is not the cost. Total cost of a POS tier in particular also includes payment processing fees, hardware such as scanners, card readers, and printers, and add-on integrations. The table works one small shop's first year from the published entry prices (checked October 2025, US pricing as published by the roundups):

TierSubscription, year oneWhat else the year charges
Spreadsheet $0 Nothing in software. The cost is editor time, and it arrives disguised as count corrections once test one starts failing.
Inventory app $0–$348 (at $0–$29/mo) A scanner only if the shop scans outside the till. No processing fees attach to this tier — it does not take payments.
POS with inventory From $828 (at $69/mo) Card processing fees on every sale, plus till hardware — scanners, readers, printers — and any paid add-on integrations. These are real, recurring, and sized by the shop's card volume, so they are listed rather than guessed at here.

Read the table with the two classic mistakes in mind, because both are overspending: staying on a spreadsheet long after the error-fixing time exceeds a subscription, and buying more software than the business needs. The free end of the app tier has an exit point, and it is not a date — it is the day test one or test two starts failing.

What migration actually involves

Moving tiers is mostly a data job, and it is worth pricing in effort before choosing a tier on price alone. The item list has to leave the old home clean: one row per SKU, duplicates merged, dead items dropped rather than carried into a fresh system. Counts should be verified at the move, not copied on faith — importing a wrong on-hand number gives the new system a confident, wrong starting point. Run the new tier alongside the old record for one full counting cycle before retiring the old one, so a discrepancy has a reference to be checked against. None of this needs the expensive tier; it needs a weekend and a complete stock count, and it is the same work whichever direction the move goes.

The decision table: your answers, your tier

Your answersYour tier

Write down which test answer would change the decision, and check it twice a year. Shops rarely regret the tier they chose; they regret noticing a year late that they had moved into a different one.
