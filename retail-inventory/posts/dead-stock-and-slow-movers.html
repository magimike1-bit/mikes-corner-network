Dead stock and slow movers: finding stock that stopped selling and clearing it in stages

Ordering & stock levels · 7 min read

Every store carries a quiet pile of money that stopped moving: the lines that sold briskly once, slowed, and then stopped altogether, still occupying shelf space and still showing up in the stock value as if they were an asset like any other. Dead stock rarely announces itself. It accumulates one reasonable order at a time, and by the time it is obvious, the season for selling it at full price is long gone. The fix is not a one-off clearance panic. It is a short monthly routine that finds slow stock while it still has recoverable value — and a staged plan for moving it out.

What dead stock actually costs

The first cost is the obvious one: capital locked in product that is not selling is capital that cannot buy the lines that are. A POS vendor's guide for gift retailers makes the same point from the shop floor: money sitting in slow-moving stock could otherwise be reinvested in the store's best-selling lines. The second cost is space. Shelf and stockroom room given to a dead line is room denied to a live one, and in a small store space is the scarcest asset there is. The third cost is subtler: dead stock distorts decisions. Stock figures that include hundreds of dollars of unsellable goods make the business look better stocked and better funded than it is, and reorder habits built on those figures keep feeding the problem. Finally, value decays. Fashion dates, packaging fades, technology moves on, and dated goods expire — a line that could have cleared at a modest discount this quarter may only be donatable next year.

Define your thresholds before you pull the report

"Dead" needs a definition before it needs a report, or every review turns into an argument about individual products. A widely used progression in inventory guidance sorts stock by time since last sale:

BandDays since last saleWhat it meansDefault response
Active0–30Selling at an expected rateNormal reordering
Slow-moving30–60Velocity is declining; worth watchingCheck the cause; stop topping up
Excess60–90Stock on hand is above what the current rate justifies; pricing action is dueFirst markdown or bundle; no reorders
Dead90+No sales activity and little prospect of a normal saleWork the exit ladder below

These bands are a starting framework, not a universal law, and the store's own rhythm should adjust them. A gift shop with deliberate seasonal peaks and a hardware store with steady year-round lines will read the same 90 days very differently. What matters is that the thresholds are written down, dated, and applied the same way every month — a definition that changes to spare a favorite product is not a definition. Quantity matters alongside age: two units unsold for 90 days is a nuisance; forty units unsold for 90 days is a cash problem, which is why the report below sorts by value on hand, not just by age.

The monthly dead-stock routine

  1. Pull the report. Filter the product list by days since last sale and sort by quantity on hand and value on hand — that combination, high stock that has not sold recently, is the dead-stock list. Any basic point-of-sale or spreadsheet inventory can produce it; no special tooling is required.
  2. Total the pile. Add up the cost value of everything in the excess and dead bands and write the number down with the date. The routine's progress is measured by whether that number shrinks month over month — and a store that has never totaled it is usually surprised by it.
  3. Run the seasonal exception check. Before anything is marked down, ask of each candidate: is this line out of season rather than out of demand? Stock that reliably sells in a specific season — the classic pattern being seasonal goods whose demand returns on a predictable calendar — should be stored and held for its season if storage is cheap, not cleared at a loss weeks before its market returns. The check is one question per line, and it is what separates a dead-stock routine from a seasonal-stock accident.
  4. Check for fixable causes. Some "dead" stock is a merchandising failure, not a demand failure: it was never displayed, it is priced against a competitor's newer model, or its shelf position guarantees it is never seen. Give those lines one corrected chance — proper display, correct location — for one review cycle before they join the exit ladder.
  5. Assign every remaining line a ladder step and a date. A line discussed but not assigned tends to be discussed again next month, unchanged. The decision made in the meeting should be visible on the shelf within the week.

The exit ladder: clearing stock in stages

The options for clearing dead stock are well established — markdowns, bundles, supplier returns, donation — but a flat list invites stores to either jump straight to the deepest discount or stall at the shallowest one. Sequenced as a ladder, each step has a trigger and a purpose:

StepActionTrigger
1Move and re-present: better position, grouped with full-price lines it complementsFirst entry into the excess band (60–90 days)
2First markdown, or bundle with a bestseller at a combined priceStill unsold at the next monthly review
3Deeper markdown or a limited-time promotion to the store's own customer listEnters the dead band (90+ days) or fails step 2
4Ask the supplier about return, exchange, or credit against a live lineAny point — but ask before the deepest markdown, while the goods are still current
5Clearance at any price that recovers something, then donate or write off what remainsTwo review cycles at step 3 without movement

Two principles govern the ladder. First, earlier and shallower beats later and deeper: a modest discount while a line still looks current recovers more than a drastic one after it looks tired, so the ladder front-loads action instead of regret. Second, a markdown on long-unsold stock is not a new loss — the money was spent when the stock was bought; the discount converts shelf stock back into cash that can work again. Independent retailers have historically had fewer exits than large chains — essentially their own floor, a clearance section, and their customer list, where big retailers also use liquidation channels and off-price partners, as retail commentator Savvy Shopkeeper has noted — which makes working those home exits deliberately, in order, more important, not less. On the final step: how a write-off or donation is treated for tax depends on the store's situation, so confirm the treatment with an accountant rather than assuming it.

Stopping the next pile-up

Clearing dead stock without changing the buying that created it just schedules the next clearance. Three habits close the loop:

  • Reorder from sales, not from habit or from a tempting price. A supplier deal on a line already sitting in the slow band is not a deal — check the pile before approving the purchase order, and let the monthly report veto reorders of anything in the excess or dead bands.
  • Record why each dead line died. One line of notes per cleared item — bought too deep, season misjudged, trend passed, quality complaints — turns the clearance list into buying guidance. Patterns repeat: the same cause three times is a policy waiting to be written.
  • Buy new lines shallow. A small first order that sells out is a cheap lesson and an easy reorder; a deep first order that fails is next quarter's dead stock. Depth should be earned by demonstrated sales, not granted on optimism.

What success looks like: the store can state, on any given day, the cost value of its excess and dead stock and the date of the last review; every line in those bands carries an assigned exit step with a date; genuinely seasonal stock is identified and stored deliberately rather than cleared by mistake; and the dead-stock total trends down across quarters instead of being rediscovered, larger, once a year.

FAQ

How long without a sale makes stock "dead"? A common working definition is 90 or more days with no sales activity and little prospect of a normal sale, with slow-moving (30–60 days) and excess (60–90 days) as the warning bands before it. Adjust the bands to the store's selling rhythm — and apply them consistently once set.

Is discounting dead stock just accepting a loss? The purchase money was already spent; an unsold line returns nothing at all. A staged markdown recovers part of the cost as cash that can fund stock that actually sells — which is why acting while the goods still look current recovers more than waiting.

What besides markdowns can a small store do? Bundle slow lines with bestsellers, promote them to the store's own customer list, ask the supplier about returns or credit, and — for what genuinely will not sell — donate or write it off and reclaim the space. The ladder above sequences those options instead of treating them as interchangeable.
