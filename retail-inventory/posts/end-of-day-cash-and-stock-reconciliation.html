End-of-day reconciliation: close the cash, the card batch, and the stock exceptions together

Daily operations · 8 min read

A drawer that balances is not a closed day. It is one number agreeing with another number, and it says nothing about the return sitting behind the counter that never went back into stock, the damaged unit the system still offers for sale, or the card batch that is $42 higher than the point of sale total because one sale was keyed as cash. Most closing guides treat the close as a cash exercise with a lockup attached, and the stock record quietly inherits every mistake the day made. This close runs three reconciliations in one fixed order, cash, card batch, and stock exceptions, and finishes all three before the register session is closed, because in at least one major system that session cannot be reopened once it is.

The stop line, set before anyone counts

Sterling's rule for any daily money routine: write the exit conditions while nobody is tired, defensive, or looking at a specific variance. Published guidance offers a starting point, not a policy: POS Solutions suggests variances under a few dollars are logged and monitored, larger ones trigger recounts and manager review, and material differences are escalated the same day, not weeks later. A shop still has to pick its own number, in dollars, per drawer, and write down what happens at each level. The framework later in this piece gives the three levels. The stop line sits underneath them:

  • Three closes over threshold in any rolling ten trading days for the same drawer ends solo closing for that drawer. A second person counts or verifies until ten consecutive clean closes rebuild the record.
  • Any variance the closer cannot explain with a document, a payout slip, a refund record, a batch report, stops the close from being signed as explained. It is logged as unexplained, with an owner and a morning deadline. Signing an unexplained variance as routine is the point where a control becomes a ritual.
  • Any stock exception involving a held item, a transfer, or a damaged unit still shown as sellable is cleared or logged before lockup, every night, no carry over by memory. If it is still open at the third consecutive close, the item is counted in full and the record corrected from the count, not from estimates.

None of this accuses anyone of anything. Variances are evidence about a process first: training gaps, a payout habit that skips the record, a price file that is wrong. Theft is a separate question with its own procedure, and it is not answered by a closing sheet.

Three reconciliations, in this order

End-of-day reconciliation, in the standard definition, compares what the point of sale says happened, in sales, refunds, payouts, and payments, against what actually happened in the till, the card batches, and the bank deposits. Split that definition by where each kind of money and stock actually lives, and the order of work sets itself.

The cash arithmetic is one line. Expected cash equals the opening float, plus cash sales, minus cash refunds, minus cash payouts or withdrawals. Square's drawer sessions show the same components: starting cash, cash sales, cash refunds, cash paid in and out, and the expected cash amount. Worked drawer, labelled illustrative: float $150.00, cash sales $486.25, cash refunds $22.00, one recorded payout of $18.00 for cleaning supplies. Expected cash is $150.00 plus $486.25, minus $22.00, minus $18.00, which equals $596.25. The blind count comes to $591.25. The drawer is $5.00 short, and because every component is documented, the $5.00 is a question about change giving or a miscount, not a mystery about the whole day. Record the difference every day, even when it is small: the closing guidance is explicit that consistent small variances are often the first visible sign of a training issue or a chronic process error.

The card batch reconciliation is a match, not a count. Close the batch on each terminal, take its total for card sales and refunds, and match it against the point of sale card total for the same trading period. The two should agree tonight. The bank deposit should not be forced to agree tonight: card money lives in terminals, processor batches, and bank deposits, and the net deposit is matched to the bank later, taking fees into account. Shops that chase the bank figure at close invent a variance out of settlement timing and then stop trusting real ones.

The close, step by step

  1. End trading cleanly. No open, parked, or draft transactions belong to today when the reports run. Close time means the final transaction has settled in the records, not the time printed on the door, because a last minute return can change cash, card totals, tax, inventory, and store credit in one action.
  2. Run the reports while the drawer stays shut. The end of day summary with totals by payment type, plus the refunds, voids, and discount or price change reports. A correct grand total can hide a card sale entered as cash, so tenders are reviewed separately, never as one lump.
  3. Work the stock exceptions before touching the session close. Walk the counter, the returns spot, and the back door. Returns go back to sellable stock or to the damaged location, with the stock record matching the decision. Damaged units come out of sellable stock tonight. Items held for a customer get a record or stop being treated as held. A transfer box left on the counter gets received properly or logged as in transit, not shelved from memory. Refunds, voids, and price overrides from the reports get a line each: item, amount or quantity, and what was observed.
  4. Count the drawer blind. The expected figure exists, printed or saved, but the person counting does not see it. Count by denomination, record the counted total first, separate the float that stays for tomorrow, and only then compare. If the figures disagree, recount once from the beginning. Nobody adds cash, removes an overage, or edits a worksheet to force a zero.
  5. Close the card batches and match them. Batch total against point of sale card total, per terminal. A mismatch is written as a tested statement, in the form the closing checklist guidance recommends: card batch exceeds point of sale card total by a stated amount, with the register and time attached. Not: the register is wrong.
  6. Log every variance and exception that survives. Each entry carries the register, the time, the amount, the receipt or transaction reference, what the closer observed, and the name of the person who will review it. Facts on the log, guesses labelled as guesses or left off.
  7. Close the register session. Only now. In Shopify POS, closing a session means entering the counted cash, setting the float that stays, and reviewing the completion screen where counted and expected should match, and after a session is closed it cannot be reopened or edited. In Square, ending a drawer merely ends the session, while closing it records the actual counted amount, so the counted close is the one to use. Either way, the session is the lock on the day's record, which is exactly why the stock work sits before it.
  8. Prepare the deposit and hand over. The deposit amount comes from the completed cash worksheet, not from a fresh count with different assumptions. Record the amount, the bag or envelope identifier, the date, who prepared it, and who verified it, and seal it before the physical lockup tasks begin.

What success looks like: the whole close takes the 10 to 15 minutes the guidance budgets for a well set up small shop, and the opener can answer three questions from the sheet alone: what varied, what is still open, and whose name is on each open item. A close that needs the closer in the room to explain it has not finished.

Variance triage: log it, recount it, or escalate it tonight

LevelTestAction
LogAt or under the shop threshold, first occurrence, components all documentedRecord the amount, the drawer, and the probable cause if one is visible. No recount beyond the standard second count. Watch the pattern column.
Recount and reviewOver threshold, or the second occurrence inside ten trading days at any sizeRecount by denomination, verify the float, check every payout and refund against its record, and review the session's cash activity before the deposit is sealed.
Escalate tonightMaterial amount by the shop's written definition, any undocumented component, or a card batch mismatch with no transaction referenceThe owner or manager is told the same night with the log entry attached. Memory fades and the trail cools; a week-old variance is usually a permanent one.

Two habits keep the triage honest. First, each register closes separately, with its own report and its own count, and cash is never mixed between tills before counting, so a variance belongs to a drawer and a session instead of to the day in general. Second, roles are named: the cashier counts and records the drawer, a supervisor or the owner reviews the reconciliation and signs off. Where one person closes alone, the sealed deposit and the log get an independent review at opening, which is weaker than a second person at night and still far stronger than no review at all. Session discipline helps more than suspicion: a register session left open too long makes even the system's own expected cash and discrepancy reports inaccurate, so sessions close at every store close and every staff change, without exceptions for quiet days.

What the opener receives

The handoff is a single sheet, paper or digital, with five things on it: the signed sales and tender close; the blind count, the expected amount, and the recorded variance; the sealed deposit record and where it is held; the exception log covering cash, card, and stock, with a named follow-up owner on every open line; and notes on returns, damages, holds, transfers, or equipment problems that the opening team will meet before the closer is awake to ask. That is the whole product of the close. A store is not closed when the lights go off. It is closed when the money, the card record, and the stock record have each been brought to a known state, and the person opening tomorrow can prove it from the sheet without making a phone call.
