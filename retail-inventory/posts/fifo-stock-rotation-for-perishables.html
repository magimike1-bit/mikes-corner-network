FIFO stock rotation for perishable retail stock: sell by the date, not by the delivery

Stockroom operations · 8 min read

FIFO — first in, first out — is usually taught as a delivery rule: the stock that arrived first gets sold first, so new deliveries go behind what is already on the shelf. For undated goods that rule is the whole story. For perishable stock it is only half of it, and the missing half is where the write-offs hide. Deliveries do not arrive in expiry order. A supplier can send a short-dated case this week and a long-dated case next week, a promotion can pull old stock forward and push it back again, and two facings of the same product can quietly hold different dates. A store that rotates strictly by arrival order will, sooner or later, carefully sell its way through fresh stock while a short-dated case expires behind it. Rotation for dated goods runs on the date printed on the pack. Arrival order is only the tiebreaker.

The two rules, in the right order

USDA Foods storage guidance states both rules and their order. The general rule is a first-in-first-out system, worked by marking cases or containers with the date of receipt. The override follows immediately: products marked with the earliest best-if-used-by date should be used first, even if those items were received after a similar item already in inventory. That second sentence is the one most retail rotation advice leaves out, and it changes the daily habit: the question at the shelf is not "which of these arrived first?" but "which of these expires first?" Warehouse and food-service practice has a name for the date-first version — FEFO, first-expired-first-out — and for any product carrying a date, it is the rule that governs. FIFO by receipt date still earns its place: it is the right order for undated and long-life stock, and the tiebreaker when two batches carry the same date.

Know which date you are reading

Rotation is only as good as the date it reads, and retail packs carry several different ones. The USDA guidance separates them plainly. A best-if-used-by (or best-if-used-before) date indicates when the product will be of best flavour or quality. A use-by date is the last date the manufacturer recommends using the product while at peak quality. A sell-by date is aimed at the store rather than the customer: it is the date by which the manufacturer recommends the product be sold, given for inventory management purposes. The same guidance notes that product dates on retail foods are not federally regulated and function as quality indicators rather than safety deadlines — which cuts both ways. It means a best-before date is a quality line the store manages with markdowns, not a cliff edge; and it means no printed date excuses selling stock the store knows is spoiled, damaged, or temperature-abused. Rotate on the earliest date on the pack, and treat a true use-by date on high-risk perishables as the hard limit it is meant to be.

The rotation procedure

Rotation is set at receiving and maintained at the shelf; it cannot be retrofitted during a busy Saturday. The procedure below assumes one person can work it in a small store:

  1. Read the dates before the case leaves receiving. Food safety guidance on stock rotation puts date-checking at delivery first for a reason: this is the one moment the new stock and the delivery paperwork are in the same pair of hands. Note the earliest date in the delivery, and flag any case whose date is earlier than the stock already on the shelf — that case does not go to the back by default.
  2. Shelve by date, not by delivery. New stock goes behind existing stock only when its date is later. When the incoming date is earlier, the incoming stock goes in front, or onto a "sell first" spot — the physical order on the shelf must match the date order, because staff and customers both take from the front.
  3. Mark what has no usable date. For cases where the pack date is a production code rather than a readable expiry, follow the USDA pattern: mark the case with its date of receipt and rotate those goods on receipt order, keeping the receipt record so age is never a guess.
  4. Face up by pulling forward, never by topping up. When a facing is refilled, the existing units come forward and the new units go behind them. Dropping fresh stock into the front of a half-empty facing is how rotation dies in practice — it takes ten seconds longer to do it properly, and it is the whole system.
  5. Sweep the dated categories weekly. Pick a quiet hour and work the perishable categories shelf by shelf: check dates on what is delivered, used, or displayed, pull anything out of date off the shelf, and move short-dated stock to the front or into a marked markdown spot. Food Alert's rotation checklist makes the same three habits — dates checked, short life at the front, out-of-date stock removed — the core of the routine.
  6. Markdown before the date, not after. Stock nearing its date can be reduced as a special to move it while it is still sellable; that is a normal, sanctioned rotation tool. Stock past its date is not a pricing problem — it comes off sale, full stop.

What success looks like: any unit picked at random from a dated facing has an expiry date no earlier than the units behind it; the weekly sweep finds short-dated stock to markdown rather than expired stock to write off; and expiry write-offs shrink to the occasional forecasting miss instead of a standing weekly line.

Date marking for stock the shop opens or prepares

Some perishable stock wears the shop's dates, not a manufacturer's: food prepared on site, and commercially prepared food the shop opens and holds — deli salads, sliced meats, prepared ready-to-eat lines. Here the FDA Food Code rules that university extension guidance summarises apply. Date marking exists to control pathogen growth — notably Listeria monocytogenes, which can grow under refrigeration — and ready-to-eat time/temperature control for safety (TCS) food that is prepared on site, or opened after commercial processing, must be labelled and dated if it will be held in the refrigerator for more than 24 hours. The limit is seven days: such food should not be kept for more than 7 days, and a shorter manufacturer's use-by date takes precedence where one exists. Mark each container with the date it must be sold, eaten, or thrown out — writing the preparation or opening date, or the discard date itself, are both acceptable systems, as long as every staff member is trained in the one the shop uses. One rule catches people out: when foods are combined, the combined dish takes the earliest date of its components. Extension's worked example makes it concrete — chicken prepared on October 21 combined with shrimp prepared on October 20 gives the dish a discard date counted from October 20 as day 1: October 26. The freshest ingredient does not reset the clock.

Where rotation breaks on a real sales floor

Most rotation failures are not ignorance; they are friction. Customers pull stock out of order, take the front unit from one facing and the back unit from another, and abandon chilled goods on warm shelves — the weekly sweep exists to absorb exactly this. Deliveries arriving during trading hours get shelved by whoever is free, in whatever order is fastest, unless the receiving date-check is a standing step rather than a favour. Multi-buy promotions invert the problem: the promoted line sells through its front stock, staff refill from the newest case because it is nearest the door, and the older case migrates to the back of the stockroom. None of these are solved by a poster about FIFO. They are solved by the two habits that survive contact with a busy shop: dates read at receiving, and facing refills that always move old stock forward before new stock goes in. Everything else is the weekly sweep catching what those two missed.

FAQ

Is FIFO or FEFO correct for retail food? Both, layered. For dated goods the earliest expiry sells first (FEFO), even when that batch arrived later — that is the USDA position. FIFO by receipt date governs undated stock and breaks ties between batches with the same date.

A delivery arrived with a shorter date than the stock on the shelf. What should happen? The new, shorter-dated stock goes in front or into a sell-first position, the supplier gets told (short-dated deliveries are a service failure worth raising while the paperwork is fresh), and the case is never buried at the back on arrival order alone.

Does rotation apply to non-food perishables? Yes, in proportion. Cosmetics, batteries, film, and medicines all degrade or expire on their own clocks. The same earliest-date-first order and the same weekly sweep work; only the urgency and the markdown timing change.

Can out-of-date stock be discounted and sold? No. Near-date stock can be marked down while it is still in date; stock past its use-by date comes off the shelf. Rotation exists precisely so that markdowns happen early enough that the question rarely comes up.
