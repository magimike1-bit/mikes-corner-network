GMROI for a small shop: rank categories by how hard their stock dollars work, and cut the ones that stop earning

Ordering & stock levels · 7 min read

0.92. That is the GMROI of the T-shirts category in Shopify's published worked example, inside a store whose overall GMROI is 2.70. The store looks healthy. One category inside it earns 92 cents of gross profit for every dollar it keeps tied up in stock, which is less than the dollar itself. Gross margin return on inventory investment, GMROI, is the ratio that finds that category: gross margin dollars divided by average inventory at cost. This guide runs it the way a small shop can actually use it, by category, with the stop line stated before the arithmetic, because the stop line is the part the formula guides leave out.

The stop line first: who should skip this metric, and when a category has to go

Skip GMROI, for now, if the shop cannot value its inventory at cost with a straight face. The denominator is what the shop paid for the stock, and a ratio built on guessed costs produces confident nonsense: it will rank categories, and the ranking will be wrong in proportion to the guessing. Shops in that position get more from fixing item costs first.

The metric also says nothing on its own about a category the shop is obliged to carry, the loss leader that pulls traffic for everything else. Read those categories with their eyes open and their GMROI noted, not obeyed.

For every other category, the kill criterion is this. A category that posts a GMROI below 1.0 for two consecutive measurement periods, after the fixes in the last section have been applied in order, loses its open order budget. It is cut to special orders only, or dropped, at the second reading.

Below 1.0 is not a soft warning. Published guidance is consistent that under 1.0 the stock returns less gross margin than the cost of the inventory tied up to sell it, which means the category is consuming capital before rent, wages, or any other overhead enters the picture. Two periods, not one, because a single seasonal quarter can drag a sound category under the line on timing alone.

What GMROI counts, and the two ways to average the stock

GMROI = gross margin dollars ÷ average inventory at cost. Gross margin is revenue minus cost of goods sold over the period. A result of 2.0 means each dollar held in stock at cost produced two dollars of gross margin in that period.

The result is a multiple, not a percentage, and it is not net profit: operating expenses do not belong in it, and the formula deliberately leaves out holding costs such as storage, insurance, and the cost of the capital itself. One analysis firm notes the consequence plainly: a product posting 4.0 can still be net negative once fulfilment and carrying costs are counted. GMROI ranks stock. It does not close the books.

Two method rules decide whether the number can be trusted. First, value the inventory at cost, never at retail. Dividing by the retail value is described in the guides as the most common GMROI mistake, and it deflates the ratio so that healthy categories look weak. Second, build a category figure from sums, never by averaging the GMROI of its individual products: add the category's gross margin dollars, add its average inventory at cost, then divide. Averaging ratios lets a tiny product with a freak result pull the whole category around.

The average itself comes in two forms. The quick method takes the beginning and ending inventory at cost and divides by two, which is fine for a steady category. The fuller method averages the month-end values across the period: for a full year, the twelve month-end figures plus the opening balance, divided by 13.

For a seasonal category that builds stock before a peak and draws it down after, the monthly average tells the truth where the two-point average flatters or punishes the result depending on which two days happen to be picked. Whichever method a shop chooses, the period must match on both sides: a full year of gross margin against a full year of average inventory, never against one month's snapshot.

The benchmarks disagree: one working floor for a small shop

Ask the published guides what a good GMROI is and the answers spread out fast.

SourceWhat it calls good
MerchantSheets guide2.0 to 3.0 solid; above 3.0 strong in most categories; roughly 3.2 a standout
Iris FinanceAbove 3.2 healthy, per widely cited industry benchmarks; below 1.0 destroys capital
MerchantSheets category ranges, early 2026Health and beauty about 2.7 to 5.2; apparel about 2.2 to 3.0; footwear about 1.8 to 2.0; jewellery about 1.1 to 1.8
OnlyCalculatorsFlorists and specialty gift shops routinely above 10; electronics retailers target 3 to 5; apparel typically 1.5 to 3.0

The spread is not sloppiness. It is the metric working as designed: a florist turns perishable stock at high margin and posts numbers a jeweller, carrying slow and expensive pieces, will never reach, and both can be running sound businesses. So a small shop should not import a target. It should take the one point every source agrees on, 1.0 as the floor below which stock loses money, and then set its working target from its own category history, this year against last year, category against category inside the same four walls. Shopify's guidance lands in the same place: compare against similar retail categories and against the shop's own historical results, not against a universal retail number.

Two categories, worked end to end

Illustrative figures, one year, for two categories in the same small shop. The arithmetic is the point; the dollars are a scale model.

KitchenwareGiftware
Revenue$96,000$54,000
Cost of goods sold$57,600$27,000
Gross margin dollars$38,400$27,000
Average inventory at cost$16,000$22,500
GMROI2.401.20

Read the margin percentages alone and giftware wins: it keeps 50 cents of every sales dollar against kitchenware's 40 cents. Read GMROI and the result reverses. Giftware parks $22,500 of stock at cost to produce its $27,000 of margin, while kitchenware produces $38,400 from $16,000. The margin report says buy more giftware. The GMROI report says giftware is where the shop's cash goes to sit down. Both reports are accurate. Only one of them is about the money tied up.

The denominator is where most small shops bleed, quietly. A 2025 supply chain benchmark cited by Shopify found 55% of small and mid-sized businesses holding at least 20% excess stock, and 46% reporting that 5% or more of their inventory is dead stock. Every dollar of that excess sits in the denominator of every category it belongs to, dragging the ratio down whether the selling is good or bad.

Read the two halves: margin problem or turn problem

GMROI splits into two factors that multiply together: margin on cost (gross margin dollars divided by cost of goods sold) times inventory turns (cost of goods sold divided by average inventory at cost). Splitting a weak result names the disease before anyone prescribes.

KitchenwareGiftware
Margin on cost0.671.00
Inventory turns3.61.2
GMROI (the product)2.401.20

Giftware's margin on cost is the better of the two. Its problem is turns: the stock moves 1.2 times a year, so each inventory dollar gets used once and then rests. That is a stock depth problem, and price cuts are the wrong first tool for it, since cutting price attacks the one half of the ratio that is healthy. Kitchenware, if its GMROI ever sagged, would be the opposite case: turns are fine, so the lever would be price or landed cost. The decision rule is short. Low margin on cost with healthy turns means a pricing or supplier problem. Healthy margin on cost with low turns means an ordering problem: too much stock bought too far ahead. Both low means the category itself is the problem, and it goes on the kill criterion clock.

The fixes, in order, and the date the category dies

Work a weak category in this order, cheapest lever first, and re-measure after each step rather than pulling all of them at once.

  1. Cut the order quantity. The denominator is the fastest lever a buyer controls. Smaller, more frequent orders against the same sales lift turns without touching price. This is also where the excess stock figures above get fixed, one purchase order at a time.
  2. Renegotiate the landed cost. A lower cost per unit raises margin on cost and shrinks the denominator at the same time, the only fix that pulls both halves in the right direction.
  3. Reprice, carefully. A higher price lifts margin and can slow sales enough to age the stock, which hurts the turns half. Test on part of the category, as Shopify's guide suggests for pricing changes, before applying it across the line.
  4. Clear the tail. Bundle slow lines with fast movers, run the clearance, or donate for the write-off. One caution from the guides: a fire sale that doubles velocity while halving margin leaves GMROI exactly where it started, so clearance is a cash recovery move, not a ratio fix, and should be judged as one.
  5. Prune. What is left after the first four steps is the rationalization list, the products that no longer earn their place in the assortment.

Run the calculation at least quarterly, and monthly for fast-moving or short-shelf-life categories where the picture changes between quarters. Then hold the line set at the top of this guide: below 1.0 at two consecutive readings, with the fixes worked in order, and the category loses its order budget on that date. Not when it feels tired. On the date. A kill criterion written before the money is spent is a decision; the same thought afterwards is an argument.
