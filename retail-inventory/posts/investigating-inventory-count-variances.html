Investigating inventory count variances: a triage order that finds the cause before anyone adjusts the record

Counting & accuracy · 7 min read

The count is short, so someone must have taken it. That is the first conclusion most shops reach — and the least likely one. A count variance is a signal with several plausible parents: a short delivery signed for unchecked, stock sitting one bin over from where the system thinks it lives, a transfer nobody posted, a case counted as units. Theft is on the list, but published guidance is blunt about its place on it: unauthorised removal should be concluded only after the evidence has been reviewed, not assumed at the first short count. What follows is a triage — the cheapest checks first, the record adjusted last.

The stop line first: do not adjust the record yet

The instinct to correct the system figure to match the count, on the spot, destroys the evidence the investigation needs. Two moves come before any adjustment. First, control movements in the affected area: pause or tightly track receipts, transfers, returns and sales of that item while the check runs, and pin down the cutoff — the exact moment the count describes, and every posting that landed around it. A receipt posted an hour after the count closes a "variance" that never existed. Second, freeze the original figures: system quantity, counted quantity, counter's name, date. An adjustment made later should add to that record, never replace it.

Kill criterion, stated plainly: if the shop's habit is to overwrite the system number at every count, stop — that habit is the first defect to fix, ahead of any single variance. Counts that are routinely overwritten teach the system nothing, and the same discrepancies return wearing the same face.

Size it before you solve it

Three numbers decide how much investigation a variance has earned.

  • Unit variance = physical quantity − system quantity. Negative is short, positive is over — and overages are investigated too, because an overage today is usually another item's shortage or a receiving error that has not surfaced yet.
  • Variance percent = unit variance ÷ system quantity × 100. The same five units are 9.6% of a 52-unit line and under 1% of a 520-unit line; the first is a broken process, the second may be a rounding habit.
  • Value variance = unit variance × unit cost. Percent says how wrong the count is; dollars say how much the error matters.

Illustrative: a line counted at 47 against a system figure of 52 is −5 units, −9.6%, and −$60 at a $12 unit cost. For accuracy reporting over a whole count, use absolute variances — total absolute unit variance against total system quantity counted — so shortages and overages cannot cancel each other into a falsely healthy score.

One threshold rule: investigate, or adjust and log

Published tolerance guidance for cycle counts runs at 2%–5%: tight for expensive or low-piece-count items, looser for cheap bulk lines. Most operators pair it with a dollar threshold, because a 1% variance on a high-value line is real money. Combined into one rule a small shop can post on the stockroom wall:

ResultAction
Inside 2% and under the shop's dollar threshold Adjust the record, log it with a reason code, move on
Outside 5%, or over the dollar threshold at any percent Investigate before adjusting — work the cause list below
Between 2% and 5% Investigate if the item is high-value, fast-moving, or has varied before; otherwise adjust and log
Any variance on a line that also varied last count Investigate regardless of size — repetition is the signal

The dollar threshold is the shop's own number — set it where an unexplained loss starts to hurt, and write it down. A threshold that lives in someone's head moves with their mood.

Work the causes in cost order

Each step below is cheaper than the one after it, and each is a documented procedure, not a hunch.

  1. Recount, blind. A second person counts the item without being told the expected figure, and counts its neighbours on the same shelf. Interrupted counts, double counts and counts taken from the wrong facing are the cheapest explanation there is.
  2. Check the other locations. Stock physically present but in an unrecorded, adjacent, staging or exception location reads as a shortage at its home location. Look in the receiving area, the returns shelf, and the bin next door before concluding anything is missing.
  3. Check unposted movements. Transfers, returns, disposals and sales that happened physically but were posted late, twice, or not at all. Review the item's recent transactions: recent sale, return or transfer first.
  4. Check receiving. Compare what arrived against the purchase order and the invoice, delivery by delivery: short shipments, over-receipts and receipts posted against the wrong order all enter here. Group the gaps by supplier — a supplier short on three deliveries in ten, while others run one in twenty, is a supplier conversation, not a staff conversation.
  5. Check status and identity. Damaged, expired or quarantined stock counted as available; returns put back without being booked; units of measure confused — a case received as units, a pack sold as singles; similar labels, duplicate item codes or wrong pack sizes sending transactions to the wrong SKU. Count the look-alike variants nearby as part of the same check.
  6. Only then, loss. If every check above comes back clean, the remaining explanations are damage that was never recorded and unauthorised removal. At that point the shop has a time window and a transaction trail to look at — evidence, rather than a suspicion aimed at whoever counted.

What success looks like: every investigated variance ends in one of three states — found (the stock or the posting error located), explained (a cause identified and a process fix named), or written off (adjusted with the loss accepted and recorded). A variance that ends as "adjusted, unknown" two counts running is the procedure failing, not the staff.

Close it with a reason code

Every adjustment, investigated or not, lands in a variance log: date, SKU, system figure, counted figure, unit and value variance, the cause found, and the adjustment made. Short reason codes keep it fast — RCPT for receiving, LOC for location, MOVE for unposted movement, UOM for unit-of-measure, DMG for damage or status, CNT for counting error, UNK for unexplained. One discipline sits underneath the log: never enter a guessed quantity to make a variance disappear. A record corrected with data nobody verified is worse than a variance left open — clean data is what every later count, reorder and report depends on, and an invented figure quietly poisons all three. Where the system keeps an adjustment history — one major platform retains 180 days of changes per product, with who made each change and why — use it; the log and the history should tell the same story.

The pattern review: what the log is for

A single variance is an incident. The log, reviewed monthly or quarterly, turns incidents into patterns: variances clustering on one supplier point at receiving; on one category, at labelling or unit-of-measure; on one movement type, at a posting step nobody owns. Fix the pattern and the counts get quieter on their own. The review has one question per cluster — what is the cheapest process change that stops this code recurring? — and one measure of success: the same reason code, on the same lines, should be rarer next quarter than this one. If it is not, the fix was a hope, and the log has done its second job: proving it.
