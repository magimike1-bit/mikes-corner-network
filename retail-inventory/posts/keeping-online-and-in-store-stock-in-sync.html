Keeping online and in-store stock in sync: one available number, verified against the platform states

Stockroom systems · 7 min read

A customer buys the last blue jacket on the website. An hour earlier, the same jacket sold at the shop counter. The refund and the apology email are already written in everything but fact — so which number failed? Not the shelf count: the jacket really was there, once. The number that failed is the one the website sold from, and keeping that number honest is a specification problem before it is a software purchase. This piece checks the specification first — what "available" actually means, in the platform's own definitions — then compares the three setups a small shop can run, then the guardrails and the test that prove the setup works.

The number customers buy from is Available, not On hand

Shopify's Help Center — the definitions below are its wording, checked against the official inventory-states page — splits every product at every location into five states:

StateWhat it means
On hand All units at the location — the total of Committed, Unavailable and Available
Available Units that can be sold: not committed, not set aside as Unavailable, and not counting Incoming
Committed Units set aside that cannot be sold — in an unfulfilled order, reserved in a draft order, or in a transfer marked ready to ship
Unavailable Units stocked at the location but held back — damaged, quality control, or safety stock
Incoming Units expected on inbound shipments; separate from on-hand until received

The verification this table makes possible takes five minutes and catches the most expensive sync error there is: a channel selling from the wrong state. If the website's sellable figure can include committed units, the shop will sell jackets it has already promised to someone else. If it includes incoming, it will sell stock that has not arrived — and may not. If damaged units sit in Available instead of Unavailable, they are for sale. The identity to hold onto is the one in the first row: on hand is a sum, and only one part of it — Available — is safe to sell from. Other platforms name the states differently, but any setup worth trusting can answer the same five questions: what is physically here, what is promised, what is held, what can be sold, what is merely expected.

Three architectures, three oversell windows

Every small shop selling in person and online runs one of three architectures, whether it chose it or drifted into it.

Separate countsBatch syncOne shared pool
How it works Each channel keeps its own stock number Counts exported and imported between systems on a schedule — daily or weekly in the manual version Every channel reads and writes one central inventory, updating automatically at each sale
The oversell window Permanent: each channel believes it holds the full quantity, so ten units can be promised several times over The interval between syncs: every channel sells from outdated numbers until the next run Moments: the near-simultaneous purchase, narrowed further by the guardrails below
Signature failure The last unit sold twice, discovered at picking time A busy Saturday outrunning a Sunday-night import A fast seller bought in the same minute on two channels
Fit One channel, or channels selling genuinely separate stock Low order volume, slow sellers, a deliberate stepping stone Shared stock, more than one active channel, any real order volume

Update speed is the measurable difference between the second and third columns. Market leaders are reported updating digital inventory every five minutes or less, with live calls for high-velocity items; an hourly batch leaves a risk window twelve times that size, and a daily spreadsheet import leaves a window no apology process can outrun. Before buying anything, a shop can audit which architecture it actually has — genuinely unified, or only loosely connected through periodic batch syncs. That difference, not the brand of software, determines the overselling risk being carried.

The guardrails that do the actual work

A shared pool narrows the window; guardrails close what is left.

  • Reserve at checkout. The moment a shopper buys, those units are allocated and the available quantity drops for every channel — the standard behaviour in unified setups, and the single mechanism that turns "the same minute" from a double sale into a sold-out message for the second buyer.
  • A display buffer on fast movers. Show the item as out of stock online while a small number remain — one published example uses two units — so timing gaps, a damaged unit, or a counting error cannot be sold. The buffer is a deliberate lie of two units, told to protect every other promise the shop makes.
  • Per-channel caps. Set a maximum available quantity per channel instead of exposing 100% of stock everywhere. If one channel surges — a marketplace promotion, a viral post — the cap acts as a circuit breaker while the other channels keep their share.
  • Returns stay out of Available until checked. Returned goods are kept separate until someone has inspected them and they are ready to sell again. A return scanned straight back into available stock sells a possibly damaged unit to the next customer.
  • Bundles sell their components. A kit's stock must be linked to the items inside it, so selling the kit reduces the components and selling the last component closes the kit. An unlinked bundle is a second, invisible stock count — the separate- counts problem rebuilt inside one system.

Two yes answers on the failure side of that list — manual updates somewhere, separate numbers somewhere, unlinked bundles, returns re-entering unchecked — are a published rule of thumb that a central inventory with automatic sync will likely pay for itself in saved refunds and saved customers.

Set it up and test it like you mean it

  1. Clean the item records first. Sync propagates whatever it is given, faster. Duplicate items, near-identical listings for the same product, and quantities nobody trusts should be fixed before channels are connected — connecting first spreads the mess to every storefront at sync speed.
  2. Pick the source of truth. One system holds the inventory; the till and the website read from it. Write down which system it is. "Both, mostly" is the separate-counts architecture with extra steps.
  3. Verify the states on five real products. For each, check that Available equals on hand minus committed minus unavailable, and that incoming stock is not inflating the sellable figure. Five products, five minutes, using the definitions above.
  4. Connect the channels and switch on reservation. Confirm a test online order moves units from Available to Committed immediately, and that the in-store view reflects it.
  5. Set buffers and caps on the fast movers only. Buffer of two on the lines that sell daily; caps on any channel prone to surges. Slow lines do not need the machinery.
  6. Run the simultaneous-purchase test. With two units of a test item available, buy one online and one at the counter at the same moment, then attempt a third purchase on each channel. Both third attempts should fail as sold out.
  7. Audit weekly. Sync systems drift: a channel quietly disconnected, a return workflow bypassed. A short weekly check — a few shelf counts against the system, the oversell and cancellation count for the week — catches drift while it is still cheap.

What success looks like: the third purchase in step 6 cannot be made, available figures on both channels agree with a physical count of the test items, and a returned item sits outside Available until it has been checked. Manual counting does not disappear in a synced shop — it changes jobs, from building the record to auditing a record worth trusting.

Bottom line: unify, batch, or stay manual

Stay manual if there is genuinely one channel, or the online side sells stock the shop floor never touches — there is no shared number to keep honest. Batch, deliberately, if a second channel is new, order volume is low, and the sellers are slow: name the interval, know the window it leaves, and set the display buffer to cover it. Unify — one pool, reservation at checkout, states verified — the moment two channels sell the same physical stock at any real volume, because from that point every oversell is an architecture decision the shop is making daily, not an accident. Treat overselling as a customer-service problem and the refunds become a recurring cost line. Treat it as the architecture problem it is, and it mostly stops happening. Fancier options — fulfilling online orders from store shelves, for instance — belong strictly after this: they add one more claimant to the same stock, and only a sync that has passed its own test can carry it.
