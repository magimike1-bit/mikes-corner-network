Minimum display quantities and backstock triggers: three shelf numbers, not one

Shelf stock · 8 min read

12 units fit the facing, 3 sell on a good day, and 5 are sitting in the backroom. How many belong on the shelf tonight? Most small shops answer that question with one number, usually whatever the low stock alert in the point of sale is set to, and that single number is asked to do three different jobs at once. It decides how the shelf should look, when someone should walk to the backroom, and when the shop should spend money on more stock. Those are three decisions, they count three different pools of stock, and mixing them is how a shelf ends up full of the wrong units while the right ones sit in a box, or how a routine top up turns into a purchase order nobody needed.

Three numbers, three different jobs

The chain retailers have a name for the first number. A minimum display quantity, also called a minimum presentation quantity, is the set of sizes and quantities a store is expected to carry at all times, set so the size run stays available and the presentation holds together. Allocation software builds it as a profile per store type. A single shop does not need the profile. It needs the same idea at the scale of one facing, plus two numbers the chain definition never mentions, because in a chain the other two live in different departments.

NumberPool it countsAction it fires
Display minimumUnits on that facing onlyNone by itself. It is the floor the shelf is not supposed to fall below, for availability and for how the shelf reads.
Pull triggerUnits on that facing, checked at a stated roundA walk to the backroom. Stock moves forward from backstock to the facing, up to shelf capacity.
Reorder pointTotal stock: shelf plus backstock, all locationsA purchase decision. New money goes out, sized by lead time and sales rate.

The reorder point is the number most point of sale guides teach, and it is a total stock number by construction. Square's small business guide puts it plainly: a par level is how much you want on hand, a reorder point tells you when to order more, and the reorder point is average daily unit sales multiplied by supplier lead time in days, plus safety stock. Four units a day, a seven day lead time, and ten units of safety stock give a reorder point of 38 units. Nothing in that formula knows or cares how many of the 38 are on the shelf and how many are in the back. That is correct for buying. It is useless as a shelf rule, and a shelf rule is useless as a buying rule.

Enterprise replenishment software keeps a related pair, a minimum and a maximum, and the logic is worth borrowing even though the software is not. In Quant's manual, minimum and maximum can be set as days of reserve or as fixed volumes: stock expected to fall below the minimum generates an order up to the maximum. The maximum can come straight from the planogram, where it means the number of products that fit the fixture based on its dimensions, so an order can never exceed the display space that exists.

Translated to a small shop: the shelf maximum is not a policy choice, it is a measurement. The display minimum is the policy choice, and it lives between a presentation floor and that measured capacity.

The runbook: set the display minimum facing by facing

  1. Measure the capacity. Fill the facing the way it is meant to look, neither crammed nor staged for a photo, and count the units. That count is the shelf maximum. A facing that holds 12 holds 12; a display minimum above 12 is a fiction that will generate pointless backroom trips.
  2. Set the presentation floor. This is the smallest quantity that still reads as stocked for that product. Enterprise systems encode the same instinct: Blue Yonder's slow mover replenishment respects a minimum presentation rule of at least 2 units to face a shelf even when the demand math says 1, so the store does not look empty. For a stackable staple the floor may be 3 or 4. For a single boxed item it may be 1.
  3. Work out the cover the shelf needs between pull rounds. Decide how often this category gets a pull round, daily, twice a week, weekly, and multiply the interval in days by average daily sales for the item. Twice weekly rounds and 1.5 sales a day need about 5 to 6 units of cover, because the shelf has to survive three to four days between rounds.
  4. Take the larger of floor and cover, capped at capacity. That is the display minimum. Worked example, labelled illustrative: a notebook facing holds 12, sells about 1.5 a day, and gets pull rounds on Tuesday and Friday. Cover is 6, the presentation floor is 3, so the display minimum is 6. If cover had come out at 14, the answer would not be 14; it would be a capacity of 12 and a shorter interval between rounds.
  5. Set the pull trigger at the display minimum, and check it only at the round. At each round, a facing at or below its display minimum gets pulled up to capacity, or to whatever the backstock can spare. A facing above its minimum is left alone. Checking at rounds, not continuously, is what keeps the job to minutes: nobody is restocking one unit at a time all day, and nobody is pulling stock forward that the shelf does not yet need.
  6. Write both numbers on the shelf label or the category sheet. A minimum that lives in one person's head changes every time a different person works the round. The label carries capacity and minimum, for example 12 and 6, and the pull decision becomes reading, not judgement.

What success looks like: pick ten facings at random. For each one, a staff member can state the capacity and the display minimum from the label, say whether the last round pulled stock for it, and point to where the backstock for it lives. If any of the three answers is a guess, the numbers exist on paper only.

When the shelf trigger fires but the backstock is empty

A pull round that finds an empty backstock shelf is not a failed pull. It is the moment the third number takes over, and the two decisions must not be blurred. The worked example in Puter's replenishment guide makes the distinction concrete: a storefront holds 6 units and should hold at least 12, a shortfall of 6, while the warehouse holds 46 against its own minimum of 20, leaving 26 above minimum. Moving 6 refills the shelf with no purchase, and the business total stays exactly where it was, 64 units. The move is recorded as a move, not a receipt, because a receipt would claim new stock arrived. When the warehouse sits exactly at its minimum and every other location is at minimum too, there is nothing above minimum to move, and pulling anyway only shifts the shortage to a different shelf. That is the buying question, and it is answered on total stock: shelf plus backstock against the reorder point, with lead time in the arithmetic.

One practical consequence follows. A low stock alert from the point of sale, such as the daily stock alert Square sends listing items that are low or out of stock, fires on total stock. It belongs to the reorder decision. Treating it as a pull trigger sends staff to a backroom that may be empty, and treating a shelf gap as a buying signal orders stock the shop already owns. Keep the alert, by all means, but file it under buying.

When the right display minimum is one, or zero

The display minimum is a default, not a law, and four common cases argue for setting it at 1, or keeping the item off the open shelf entirely:

CaseDisplay minimumWhy
High value or easily pocketed1, or none on the open shelfOne unit proves availability; the rest belong in a case or the backroom, where a loss costs less.
Bulky, one is a full facing1Capacity and minimum are the same number; a second unit has nowhere honest to go.
Slow fringe sizes and variants1Toolio's allocation guidance prefers open stock in single units for fringe sizes because fixed quantities pile up as leftovers; one on the shelf keeps the size offered without stocking the tail.
Dated or perishableCover only, never capacityEnterprise min and max logic caps the maximum by shelf life for the same reason: stock placed to look full can expire in place.

The money case for low minimums on slow lines is easy to understate because it arrives in small amounts. Illustrative arithmetic: 40 slow facings, each habitually filled with 4 units more than its sales justify, at an average cost of $6 a unit, parks $960 of cash on shelves. That cash earns nothing there, it cannot restock a proven seller, and the units absorb handling and shelf wear while they wait. Blue Yonder makes the same point from the other end for slow movers generally: holding stock centrally and moving it only on a specific need releases cash and avoids ordering just in case. The small shop version of a central pool is the backroom, and the display minimum is the valve between the two.

The stop line

Set it when the numbers are first written down. If a facing has not sold through its display minimum in 30 days, the minimum is cut to the presentation floor at the next round, and the freed units go back to backstock where they stop pretending to be a display. If it still has not sold through the floor quantity in the following 30 days, the facing itself is the problem: the item leaves the shelf plan, the space goes to a line that turns, and any reorder point for the item is suspended until the backstock is gone. A display minimum that never gets tested by a sale is not protecting revenue. It is rent paid in stock for space that could be selling.
