Open-to-buy for a small shop: a one-page monthly buying budget that stops overbuying before the order is placed

Ordering & stock levels · 7 min read

−$145,000. That is the open-to-buy result in one published worked example: planned sales of $125,000, planned markdowns of $10,000 and a planned end-of-month inventory of $250,000, set against $500,000 of beginning inventory and $30,000 already on order. The shop in that example was not short of stock. It was overbought by six figures, and the correct next order was no order at all. Open-to-buy (OTB) exists to produce that number before the buying trip, not after the stockroom fills up: it is a ceiling on new purchases for a period, calculated from planned sales, what is already on hand, and what is already on order. This guide runs it at small-shop scale, one page, one month, one category at a time.

The runbook: five inputs to gather before any order

The steps are the spine here. The formula is short; the discipline is gathering its five inputs honestly, in this order, before a vendor meeting or a reorder tempts anyone to skip them.

  1. Planned sales for the month, by category. Last year's same month, adjusted for the trend the till has actually shown since. A whole-store sales plan produces a whole-store budget, which is where the trouble starts: calculated by category, the budget says something; as one lump number, it says almost nothing.
  2. Planned markdowns. The reductions already expected this month, the promotion on the calendar, the seasonal line that will need help. Markdowns consume inventory value without a sale, so the plan has to carry them.
  3. Planned end-of-month inventory. The stock level, in dollars, the category should sit at when the month closes, enough to open next month trading, no more.
  4. Beginning-of-month inventory. What the category is actually holding today, counted or taken from a system count the shop trusts. A guessed opening figure poisons every line below it.
  5. On-order inventory. Every purchase order placed and not yet received, at full value. Money already committed is not available to commit again, the most common way small shops double-spend a budget is forgetting an order placed three weeks ago.

Then the arithmetic:

OTB = planned sales + planned markdowns + planned end-of-month inventory − beginning inventory − on-order inventory.

A positive result is the most that may be ordered this month for that category. It is a ceiling, not a target: nothing in the formula says the whole amount should be spent.

Retail or cost? Pick one basis and never mix it

Here the published guidance contradicts itself, and the contradiction matters. One current guide states plainly that OTB should always be calculated at cost, not retail value. Another, equally prominent, instructs the opposite: work it at retail. A third formulation, in LS Retail's official documentation, runs on a cost basis and adds planned negative adjustments to the mix. The formulas agree on structure. They disagree on what the dollars mean.

At costAt retail
Sales input Planned sales expressed as cost of goods sold Planned sales at selling price
Inventory inputs Beginning, ending and on-order all valued at what the shop paid Beginning, ending and on-order all valued at ticket price
The result reads as Purchase-order dollars, what can actually be committed to suppliers Ticket-value dollars, converted before an order can be placed
Who publishes it Korona POS guide; Uphance (cost dollars or units); LS Retail documentation RetailDoc guide and worked example

Either basis works. Mixing them does not. Planned sales at retail against inventory at cost inflates the budget by roughly the markup, and the shop discovers the error as a stockroom problem a month later. For a small shop placing its own orders, cost is the more direct choice, the answer comes out in the same dollars the supplier invoices, but the rule that protects the budget is consistency: every input, every month, on the basis written at the top of the page.

One small shop, worked end to end

Illustrative figures, at cost, for one category, knitwear in a small clothing shop, planning October:

InputPlanned
Planned sales$18,000
Planned markdowns$900
Planned end-of-month inventory$22,000
Beginning inventory− $26,000
Already on order− $4,000
Open-to-buy$10,900

$18,000 + $900 + $22,000 − $26,000 − $4,000 = $10,900 available for new knitwear orders in October. Not $10,900 to spend on the first buying day, $10,900 that may be committed across the month.

The number is a living one, and the mid-month recalculation is where it earns its keep. Suppose that by October 15 the shop has sold $10,200 of knitwear against a half-month expectation of $9,000, taken $700 of markdowns, received the $4,000 order, and placed $5,000 of the budget. The honest way to update is to re-run the same page for the remainder: remaining planned sales become the month's plan minus sales already made, the beginning figure for the second half is the stock now on hand, and orders already placed come off the top. Shops that update weekly or every two weeks as actual sales arrive make faster, steadier buying decisions for the rest of the period than shops defending a number written before the month began. What never changes mid-month is the basis: the recalculation runs at cost because the opening page did.

Spending the budget: an order, not a pile

A ceiling still needs a spending order. SCORE's published rule of thumb for allocating an OTB budget splits it roughly 50% to ongoing lines the shop knows it carries, 20% to size fill-ins and reorders, 20% to off-price opportunities, and 10% to testing new lines, with testing capped at about that tenth. The proportions flex by shop; the principle does not. Proven sellers get depth, new lines get a shallow test, and part of the budget stays unspent on purpose, held back so a line that takes off can be reordered while it is still selling. Spending the full amount up front converts a flexible budget into a fixed bet, placed on the month's first day, with the least information the month will ever offer.

When the number is zero or negative

Zero or less means stop ordering, that much every source agrees on. What happens next depends on a distinction the formula guides skip. A negative OTB has two different causes, and they take different fixes.

Overstocked means the stock on hand is the problem: orders on the way match the plan, yet the budget stays negative months out. The corrections work the existing pile, returns to suppliers, balancing stock to another class or location, and promotional effort, up to permanent markdowns, to move what is already owned. The post-mortem matters too: a negative sales trend, deliveries larger than what was ordered, markdowns taken too late, or stock accepted at the wrong time are the usual routes in.

Overbought means the commitments are the problem: too much is already coming in. The correction works the order book, not the shelf, review everything on order by brand, size, style and colour, starting with the biggest problem areas and the earliest ship dates, and modify or defer before goods land. Duplicated styles and price points, and lines that have consistently performed weakly, are cut first. A negative result, on the cost-basis formulation, leaves exactly three honest options: cancel orders, defer planned receipts, or accept that ending inventory will overshoot its target, chosen deliberately, not discovered later.

The stop line

Set it before the first budget is spent. While a category's OTB sits at zero or below, no new orders go out for that category, fill-in reorders of proven sellers come from the held-back portion of a positive budget, not from borrowing against a negative one. If a category closes two consecutive months negative, the problem is no longer arithmetic: run the overstocked, overbought branch above, in writing, before a third month's buying begins. The budget's whole value is that it says no early, in dollars, while saying no is still cheap.
