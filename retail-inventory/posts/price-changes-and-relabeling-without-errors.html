Price changes and relabeling without errors: a same-day procedure for small stores

Pricing & stock accuracy · 8 min read

A price change looks like one job and is actually three. Toolio's markdown guidance puts the point plainly: a markdown changes the actual price in the system and on the floor. That means new tags or sale stickers are printed, the inventory database is updated so the barcode reflects the lower price, and clear signage tells the customer what changed. Do only one or two of those three parts and the store is left selling the same item at two different prices, depending on whether the customer reads the shelf or listens to the till.

The cost of that gap is not just embarrassment. The price accuracy audit guidance from Taqtics states the rule a store is held to: charge the lowest of the advertised, posted, or labeled price. A tag that drops before the system does, a promotion that starts on the shelf before it starts at the till, or old signage left standing beside a new price all turn a routine markdown into a refund, a dispute, or a quiet loss on every unit sold until somebody notices. The fix is not more care in the abstract. The fix is a fixed order of operations, run the same way every time, so the system, the tags, and the signage land together on the same day.

A price change is three changes, not one

Start by naming the three places a price lives. The first is the inventory system: the record behind the barcode, which is what the till actually charges. The second is the shelf: the tag or sticker on or under the product, which is what the customer reads while deciding. The third is the signage: the promotional signs and shelf talkers that advertise the change, which are what a customer quotes back when the till disagrees. Taqtics frames the accuracy standard in exactly these terms — shelf tags must exactly match the POS price, labels must sit directly under the correct product, and promotional signage must be current and only on the correct items.

That framing explains why the order in the procedure below matters. If the tags change first, customers are promised a price the till cannot yet give them. If the system changes first and the tags wait until tomorrow, customers are charged less than the shelf says, and the store gives away margin it never announced. Neither error requires carelessness; both happen naturally when a price change is treated as a printing job instead of a coordinated change across three places. The procedure treats one person — or one clearly named owner for each step — as responsible for closing all three parts before the change is considered done.

The same-day procedure

  1. Write the change list before touching anything. One list, one page: each item, its old price, its new price, and the date the change takes effect. Work from the list, not from memory, and keep the list until the final check is done so there is something to check against.
  2. Change the system first. Update the inventory database so the barcode reflects the new price before any tag reaches the floor. If the system supports scheduled promotional pricing, set the activation and deactivation as scheduled, rather than relying on someone remembering to switch the price back at the end of the promotion — Taqtics lists that scheduled activation and deactivation as an explicit audit point.
  3. Print every tag from the updated system. Print the full set for the change list in one batch. Tags printed from the updated record carry the new price by construction; tags written or edited by hand introduce a transcription step where a digit can change without anyone noticing.
  4. Replace tags item by item, old tag off, new tag on. Move along the change list, removing the old tag before placing the new one directly under the correct product. Never leave two prices standing for one item, and never place a new tag beside the old one intending to tidy up later — later is when a customer finds both.
  5. Sweep the signage in the same run. Remove signage for any promotion that has ended, and place new signage only on the correct items. A sign that was accurate last week is an inaccurate price claim this week if the promotion behind it has finished.
  6. Run a scan-test sample before calling it done. Taqtics recommends random scan tests to confirm items ring at the shelf price. Take a sample from the change list — a handful of items spread across the changed sections — carry them to the till, and scan each one. Every scanned price must match its new shelf tag exactly.
  7. Fix mismatches from the system outward. If a scan disagrees with its tag, treat the system record as the thing to inspect first, then reprint that tag from the corrected record and re-scan that item. Record the mismatch on the change list; a pattern of mismatches on one section points to a step that was skipped, not to a single bad tag.
  8. File the list and note the date. Keep the completed change list, with the scan test noted on it, with the store's other inventory records. The next price change starts from a cleaner base, and a later customer question about what a price was on a given day has a dated answer.

What success looks like: every item on the change list scans at the till for exactly the price on its new shelf tag, each tag sits directly under the correct product, no ended promotion still has signage standing, and no item anywhere in the changed sections carries two different prices at once.

Manual retagging vs automatic markdown rules

A store running this procedure by hand should also know the automated alternative, because the choice changes where errors can occur. ResaleWorld's markdown documentation describes automatic markdowns set by time period or by tag colour, with markdown tags that show the reduction date and the new price. Because the price falls automatically on the set date, a clerk does not re-key the reduction at the sale — and removing that re-keying is what gives the automated route its accuracy and faster transactions, in ResaleWorld's own description.

Either route still ends at the same standard: the shelf and the till must agree. Automation removes one class of error — re-keying — and it does not remove the need for the signage sweep or the scan test, because a correctly calculated price under the wrong sign, or beside the wrong product, is still a wrong price to the customer reading it.

Timing the change: depth is a separate decision

This procedure covers executing a change accurately, whatever depth has been decided. The depth decision itself has its own discipline. Toolio's example cadence is 25% after 8 weeks and 50% after 12 weeks, and Toolio pairs markdown timing with weekly or monthly sell-through reviews rather than with a single end-of-season judgement. The same source states the two-sided risk plainly: cut too deep too soon and profit is lost on stock that would have sold for more; cut too late and the stock is left unsold. Deciding the depth in a sell-through review, and then executing it through the same-day run above, keeps the two decisions separate — the review decides how much, the procedure guarantees that the amount decided is the amount a customer is actually charged, on the tag, on the sign, and at the till.

FAQ

Which changes first, the system or the tags? The system. Updating the inventory database first means every tag printed afterwards is printed from the new price, and the scan test at the end compares the floor against a system that is already correct.

How many items should the scan test cover? The audit guidance calls for random scan tests confirming items ring at the shelf price; it does not set a fixed count. A practical sample spreads across every section touched by the change, so a skipped section is caught the same day rather than by a customer.

What applies when the sign, the tag, and the till all differ? Taqtics states the rule: charge the lowest of the advertised, posted, or labeled price. Honouring that rule at the till is the short-term fix; finding and removing the two wrong prices is the fix that stops the next dispute.

Do automatic markdowns remove the need for this procedure? They remove one part of it. Automatic markdowns by time period or tag colour, with tags showing the reduction date and new price, remove clerk re-keying at the sale. The signage sweep, the labels-under-the-correct-product check, and the scan test still apply, because those errors live on the floor, not in the calculation.

System first, tags second, signs swept, sample scanned. Run in that order, a price change is finished in one visit to each item — and the price a customer reads is the price the till charges.
