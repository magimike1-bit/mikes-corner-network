Receiving and checking in deliveries: a check-in workflow that keeps counts honest

Stockroom operations · 7 min read

Most inventory errors do not start at the register or on the shelf. They start at the back door, in the ten rushed minutes when a delivery is signed for, half checked, and put away on trust. Weeks later the error surfaces as a mystery: the system shows six, the shelf holds four, and nobody can say whether two were stolen, mis-sold, or simply never arrived. A receiving routine cannot prevent every problem, but it can make sure the goods the store pays for are the goods the store actually got — and that the record says so.

Why receiving is where inventory accuracy is won or lost

Receiving-error breakdowns in the inventory literature sort the failures into a short list: quantitative errors (the quantity received differs from the purchase order or packing slip), qualitative errors (goods arrive damaged, expired, or below standard), documentation errors (paperwork carries wrong codes or incomplete details), processing errors (goods are put in the wrong location and are effectively lost until someone stumbles on them), and supplier errors (the wrong items or quantities were sent in the first place). Every one of those, left uncaught at the door, becomes a wrong stock figure that every later decision — reordering, counting, range reviews — inherits. Clean data at the point of entry is the cheapest accuracy a store can buy.

Before the truck arrives

  • Have the purchase order ready. The check-in compares three things: what was ordered (the PO), what the supplier says was sent (the packing list), and what is physically in the cartons. Without the PO at hand, the receiver is checking the delivery against the supplier's own paperwork only — which can only ever agree with itself.
  • Keep a clear receiving zone. One defined surface or floor area where deliveries land and stay until checked. Goods that drift straight to the shelves skip the check entirely.
  • Set up a quarantine spot. A labeled box or shelf section for anything disputed. Material on hold should be physically marked so it cannot be confused with released stock, with the reason recorded and one named person owning the decision — that quarantine discipline, described in receiving-workflow guidance for look-alike stock, is what stops a disputed item being sold, shelved, or booked in by someone who never heard about the problem.
  • Know the unit of measure. A recurring error source is the case-versus-each mismatch: the supplier sells by the case, the store stocks singles. Confirm the conversion on the PO before the delivery, not over an open carton.

The check-in procedure

  1. Count cartons against the delivery note before signing. Note any carton that is missing or visibly damaged on the delivery receipt before the driver leaves. This step is not pedantry: receiving guidance is blunt that damage not noted at that moment makes a later freight claim very hard to sustain. Cartons missing or damaged in transit are the carrier's responsibility; wrong or poor-quality goods are the vendor's — the note on the receipt is what keeps those two claims separate.
  2. Open and check contents against the PO, line by line. For each line verify the item, the variant (style, color, size — the details the PO specifies), and the quantity. Variants deserve their own check rather than a glance: the right product in the wrong size is still a wrong delivery, and it is exactly the kind of error that later looks like two separate stock problems — a surplus of one size and a shortage of another.
  3. Decide carton counts vs piece counts — in advance, in writing. Counting every piece of every delivery is the accurate option and, for a one-person store, often the impossible one. The established compromise is: carton counts for trusted suppliers, piece counts for new suppliers, high-value goods, or any supplier with a history of shorting. Whichever rule the store chooses, it should be written down so the standard does not depend on who happens to receive that day.
  4. Check condition and dates as part of the same pass. Crushed corners, water damage, opened inner packs, and — for anything dated — expiry or best-before dates get checked while the carton is open, not discovered at the shelf. Short-dated stock accepted silently becomes the store's markdown problem.
  5. Price and label before goods leave the receiving zone. Merchandise should be priced before it leaves the receiving area; an unpriced item on the shelf cannot be scanned or sold cleanly, and it tends to be the item that ends up sold under a wrong code — tomorrow's discrepancy, created today. Keep identifying details in the item's attributes (color, size, season) rather than typed into the description, so reports and reorders group variants correctly.
  6. Book in what actually arrived, the same day. The receipt entered into the system should reflect what physically arrived, not what was promised. Record the actual quantity received rather than assuming the PO quantity, and enter it the day the goods land — a delivery booked three days late makes the stock figures wrong for three days, including for anyone who counts or reorders in between.

When something is wrong: the discrepancy routine

Discrepancies are normal; the routine is what separates a store that recovers the value from one that absorbs it. The standard categories, and the action each one gets:

DiscrepancyImmediate actionNever do this
Short shipment (fewer units than the PO or packing list)Note the exact shortfall on the delivery paperwork, book in only what arrived, and notify the supplier promptly with the PO number and the countBook the full PO quantity and "fix it later"
Over shipment (more than ordered)Quarantine the excess and decide deliberately: accept and pay, or returnShelve the excess — unpaid stock on the shelf corrupts both the count and the accounts
Damaged on arrivalNote it on the delivery receipt before the driver leaves; photograph the damage; quarantine the goodsSign a clean receipt and raise the damage afterward
Wrong item or wrong variantQuarantine the whole line, record what arrived versus what was ordered, and contact the supplier before booking anythingBook it under the ordered item's code to keep the numbers tidy
Unlabeled or mislabeled goodsHold in quarantine until the item's identity is confirmed against the POGuess the identity and label it — a guessed label is a processing error with a barcode

Two follow-through steps matter as much as the table. First, before paying, compare the supplier's invoice against both the PO and the actual receipt: prices and terms as agreed, quantities as received. LiveAbout's receiving guide makes the same three-way comparison the core of checking in freight, and it is the last cheap moment to catch a billing error. Second, close the loop: tell the supplier, in operational terms, what would make the next delivery easier to verify — clearer carton labels, variants separated rather than mixed, a packing list per carton. A discrepancy that only gets corrected at the door will keep arriving at the door.

One escalation option for a supplier whose deliveries keep failing the check: blind receiving for a period — the receiver counts and records what is in the cartons without seeing the expected quantities, and the counts are compared with the PO afterward. It is slower, so it is a targeted tool, not a default — but it removes the temptation to count toward the number on the page.

Booking it in the same day

The final step is administrative, and it is the one most often postponed: the receiving record — date, supplier, PO number, quantities actually received, discrepancies and their status — entered the same day, by the person who did the check where possible. That record is what a later count discrepancy gets investigated against. Without it, "the system says six, the shelf says four" has no paper trail and the investigation ends in a shrug and an adjustment. With it, the store can answer the only question that matters: did the two missing units ever arrive at all?

What success looks like: every delivery is checked against its PO before it reaches the shelves; the stock system shows what physically arrived, entered the same day; disputed goods sit in one labeled quarantine spot instead of drifting into stock; and when a count later disagrees with the system, the receiving record settles whether the error walked in through the back door. The routine adds minutes to a delivery and removes hours of detective work from every count that follows it.

FAQ

Should every delivery be piece-counted? Not necessarily. The workable rule used in receiving practice is carton counts for trusted suppliers, and piece counts for new suppliers, high-value goods, or suppliers with a history of shortages. The rule should be written down so it does not vary by who is on shift.

What happens if damage is only noticed after the driver leaves? It is much harder to claim: damage should be noted on the delivery receipt before the driver leaves, or the freight claim is likely lost. Photograph anything found later anyway and notify the supplier the same day — but treat it as the exception the routine exists to prevent.

Why do counts keep disagreeing even with regular counting? Often because the error entered at receiving: a short delivery booked at full quantity, a variant booked under the wrong code, or goods shelved before they were booked at all. Counting finds these errors; receiving discipline stops them being created.
