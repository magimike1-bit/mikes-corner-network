Seasonal stock planning and markdowns: a buying calendar and markdown ladder for small retailers

Ordering & stock levels · 8 min read

Seasonal stock punishes two opposite mistakes. Buy too little and the best selling weeks of the year run on empty shelves. Buy too much, or buy it too late, and the season ends with a stockroom of goods whose value falls every week the weather, the holiday, or the school term moves on. Most small retailers manage the first mistake carefully and the second one by instinct — which usually means a panicked clearance long after a calm, early markdown would have recovered more money. The alternative is unglamorous: decide the whole season, including its markdowns, before the first order is placed. Markdown strategy is a planning discipline, not a rescue tool: it determines when to discount, by how much, and on which items, so the season ends in a controlled sell-through instead of a fire sale.

Start from the end of the season, not the start

A season has one date that does not move: the point after which demand for the goods falls off a cliff. Summer goods do not sell at full price once the weather turns; holiday goods lose most of their value the day after the holiday; back-to-school lines stop moving once term has started. Every other date in the plan is derived from that end date by working backwards. The last acceptable delivery date is the end date minus the weeks needed to sell a final delivery at full price. The order date is that delivery date minus the supplier's real lead time — the realistic delivery time from the delivery history, not the optimistic quote. The first markdown date, the checkpoint dates, and the final clearance window all sit between delivery and the end date, and they are written down at buying time, not invented mid-season when the stock is already a problem. Planning markdowns at the buying stage — setting targets and triggers when the order is placed — is the habit that separates a controlled season from a reactive one; one major retail guide credits planned markdown strategies with materially higher full-price sell-through than reactive discounting, and the mechanism is easy to see: a trigger decided in advance fires on schedule, while a decision left to mid-season judgement fires late, under pressure, and usually too deep.

The buying calendar, worked backwards

For each seasonal category, the calendar needs only six dates, and a spreadsheet holds them as easily as any software:

  1. Season end date. The last week the category sells at anything like full demand. Be honest and be specific — "end of August" is a date; "late summer" is not.
  2. Final clearance window. The last one to two weeks, reserved for clearing whatever remains at the deepest planned discount. Stock that reaches this window unsold is a planning failure to learn from, not a surprise.
  3. Last delivery date. Work back from the season end by the number of weeks a late delivery realistically needs to sell through. A delivery that arrives with two selling weeks left and six weeks of stock in it was never going to clear at full price.
  4. Order-by dates. Subtract the supplier's actual lead time from each planned delivery date, then subtract a small buffer for the late delivery that eventually happens to everyone. If the order-by date for the main seasonal buy has already passed, the honest options are a smaller buy or no buy — not the same buy placed late.
  5. Checkpoint dates. Weekly or fortnightly dates, fixed in advance, on which sell-through is compared with the plan (next section). Mid-season and roughly two-thirds through are the two that matter most.
  6. First markdown window. For most seasonal stock the first markdown trigger lands about six to eight weeks before the season ends — earlier than most owners expect, which is precisely why it needs to be on the calendar rather than left to feel.

Depth of buy belongs on the same page as the calendar. The quantity to order is the quantity the checkpoints can manage: if the season's target sell-through cannot be reached at the planned selling rate, the buy is too deep no matter how good the supplier's price looks. A deal on seasonal stock that outlives its season is not a deal; it is next quarter's dead stock, bought early.

Sell-through checkpoints: the weekly control

Sell-through rate is the season's speedometer. Shopify's definition is the simple one: sell-through rate = (units sold ÷ units received) × 100, over the period being measured. Its worked example is a coffee shop that ordered 1,000 cupcakes for September and sold 800 of them — an 80 percent sell-through — then drilled into flavours: the lines at 95 and 90 percent were reordered in full, the line at 50 percent was dropped, and the one at 62.5 percent was reordered at half quantity. The same arithmetic, run per seasonal line at each checkpoint, turns a vague feeling about the season into three clear actions: protect, watch, or mark down.

The companion measure is weeks of supply: units on hand divided by average weekly sales. Compare it with the weeks left in the season. If a line holds twelve weeks of supply with six selling weeks left, it cannot clear at full price — the only remaining decision is whether the markdown happens now, while demand still exists, or later at a painful depth. That comparison is the strongest single markdown signal, and it needs nothing more than a stock count and last week's sales.

A checkpoint table keeps the decision consistent from week to week:

Checkpoint thresholds give the table its teeth. One widely published benchmark, from a retail pricing guide's FAQ, puts it in season terms: if sell-through at week 8 of a 16-week season is below 40 percent of the season target, a mild markdown of 10–15 percent should be initiated; below 30 percent, a more material 20–25 percent markdown should be considered. The exact percentages matter less than having percentages at all — a threshold written down in October gets applied in December, while a threshold left as "we'll see how it goes" gets debated until the season is over.

The markdown ladder

The ladder sequences the discounts so each step is smaller than the panic discount it replaces. The governing principle, consistent across markdown guidance, is that the earlier a markdown is taken, the shallower it needs to be: a modest cut made while shoppers still want the goods moves units at a better realised price than a deep cut made after demand has gone. Delaying markdowns does not avoid them; it converts them into deeper, margin-destroying discounts later, taken on stock that has also been absorbing carrying cost the whole time — holding inventory is commonly estimated at 20–30 percent of its value per year in storage, insurance, and tied-up capital.

StepTimingDepthTrigger
1 — First markdownAbout 6–8 weeks before season end20–30% on the lines behind paceSell-through behind target at a checkpoint, or weeks of supply greater than weeks left
2 — Second markdownAbout 3–4 weeks before season end35–40%Still behind pace after step 1 has had 10–14 days to work
3 — Final clearanceFinal 1–2 weeks50% or deeperWhatever remains; the goal is now cash recovery and shelf space, not margin

Three rules keep the ladder honest. First, depth at step 1 matters: a cut under about 10 percent is usually too small to change a shopper's decision, so it gives away margin without moving units — the worst of both worlds. The 20–30 percent first step is deep enough to register as real value while leaving room for later steps. Reserve 40 percent and deeper for genuinely late, genuinely stuck stock. Second, give each step 10–14 days before judging it: cutting again too fast wastes margin that did not need to be given, while waiting longer lets the season run out. Third, the ladder applies line by line, never storewide. A blanket clearance also discounts the lines still selling at full price, handing a reduction to customers who were already reaching for their wallets. Mark down the slow movers on the cadence; leave the performers untouched at full price for as long as they keep performing.

After the season, close the loop while the evidence is fresh: record, per line, the quantity bought, the quantity sold at full price, the ladder step at which the remainder cleared, and the quantity left over. That one page is next year's buying brief — it is what turns this season's overbuy into next season's smaller, better-timed order instead of a repeat.

What to leave at full price

Not everything seasonal is perishable in value. Lines that carry over cleanly — a classic design in a non-dated category, packaged goods with a long shelf life, the staple that will sell next season in identical form — can be stored and held if storage is cheap and the stock will not date, fade, or expire. The decision to hold should be made line by line at the two-thirds checkpoint, with the leftover quantity and storage cost written down, not made by default in January when the unsold stock is simply never dealt with. If holding is chosen, the stock leaves the sell-through reports and enters next season's plan as opening inventory — counted, not forgotten.

What success looks like: the store can show, for each seasonal category, a one-page calendar with the season end, order-by, delivery, checkpoint, and markdown dates set before the first order; every line behind pace at a checkpoint receives its ladder step within the week; no line reaches the final fortnight still at full price with more stock than it can sell; and the post-season record feeds a smaller or better-timed buy the following year.

FAQ

When should seasonal markdowns start? Earlier than instinct suggests. For most seasonal stock the first markdown trigger lands about six to eight weeks before the season ends, applied only to lines whose sell-through is behind target or whose weeks of supply exceed the weeks left. Starting then allows a shallow first cut; waiting forces a deep one.

How deep should the first markdown be? Deep enough to change a decision, shallow enough to leave a staircase: 20–30 percent is the commonly cited sweet spot for seasonal apparel and home goods. Cuts under 10 percent usually move nothing, and opening at 40 percent or more spends margin that a staged ladder would have kept.

How is sell-through rate calculated? Units sold divided by units received, multiplied by 100, over the period being measured — selling 800 of 1,000 units received is an 80 percent sell-through. Run it per line or category, not just storewide, so slow lines cannot hide behind strong ones.

Is it better to mark down early or discount deeply later? Early and shallow, in most cases. An early modest cut sells to a full pool of shoppers while demand exists; a late deep cut sells the same units for less, after weeks of carrying cost, to the few shoppers still looking. The most expensive markdown is the one taken too late.
