Shelf replenishment from the stockroom: a daily routine that fills gaps without overfilling

Shelf & stockroom routines · 7 min read

Most small stores do not lose sales because they failed to order stock. They lose them because the stock is in the building — in the stockroom, in the wrong aisle, in a box nobody opened — while the shelf the customer is looking at is empty. Availability research keeps finding the same thing: stock that is in the store but not on the shelf is frequently the largest single category of availability gap, and the most fixable, because the stock has already been paid for and delivered. The fix is not a bigger stockroom or smarter software. It is a short daily routine that treats replenishment as a loop — scan, triage, pick once, fill to depth, face, confirm — instead of a series of dashes to the back whenever someone happens to notice a hole.

An empty shelf has three causes, not one

Before the routine, the triage — because an empty facing is a symptom with three different causes, and each one needs a different next action. Filling is only the right response to one of them.

What the scan findsCauseNext action
Shelf empty, stockroom holds the itemBack-room stockReplenish in this round: add it to the pick list, fill to depth, face, and confirm.
Shelf empty, system shows stock, stockroom has nonePhantom inventoryDo not just reorder. Search the likely wrong homes (adjacent aisles, returns area, overstock shelf), then count and correct the record. A phantom record is self-perpetuating — the system believes stock exists, so no reorder triggers, and the shelf can stay empty until someone physically counts.
Shelf empty, no stock anywhere in the storeTrue stockoutThe fix is upstream, not on the shop floor: check the item against the reorder list and the next delivery. Face a neighbouring product into the gap only if the store's layout rules allow it, and note the gap as a stockout, not a fill.

The distinction matters because the record is often lying politely. Inventory records drift through receiving errors, incorrect adjustments, theft, damage, misplaced products, and checkout errors — and research by ECR found that correcting inventory inaccuracies produced roughly 4–8% sales increases across participating retailers in test-and-control experiments, partly because accurate records stop stock being replenished too late or too early. The gap scan is where a small store catches those lies daily, at the shelf edge, instead of at a quarterly count.

The daily replenishment routine

Run the loop once a day at a fixed time — before opening, or in the mid-afternoon lull — plus a short top-up pass before the evening peak if the store trades late. In most stores a replenishment workflow runs in six steps: identify, prioritize, locate, pick, replenish, and confirm. The routine below keeps that shape, sized for a shop where one person may run the whole loop.

  1. Scan for gaps, aisle by aisle. Walk every aisle with a list in hand (paper is fine) and note every empty or nearly empty facing, the item, and roughly how much space it has. Record what needs replacing as it is found — the scan is the list. Include dated stock checks on the same walk: pull anything past its sell-by date off the shelf, and note short-dated items for reduced-price ticketing.
  2. Triage each gap before touching stock. Work the list against the three causes in the table above. System shows stock? It is a fill or a phantom hunt. System shows zero? It is a stockout — flag it for reordering and move on. Never let the pick trip start until every gap on the list has a cause.
  3. Prioritise the fill list. High sellers and high-traffic positions first. A practical priority order weighs shelf availability, sales velocity, store traffic, time of day, and anything on promotion: a promoted, fast-selling line deserves the first trip; a slow mover with a few units left can wait for the next round. Chain SOPs start the shift the same way — from the out-of-stock or low-stock list, high sellers first.
  4. Pick once, for the whole list. Load a trolley or cage from the stockroom in one trip, working the list in stockroom order, checking case labels against the items needed. Searching is the biggest time sink in replenishment — knowing an item is somewhere in the building is very different from knowing where — so a grouped list that finishes several facings per trip beats five separate dashes, and a stockroom where each product has a known home beats both.
  5. Rotate as the shelf is filled. For dated stock, check the incoming date before it goes out: new stock must carry a later date than what is on the shelf. Move the existing shelf stock forward, place the new stock behind it, so the oldest sells first. Never place new stock in front of older stock with an earlier date — that breaks rotation and turns directly into waste.
  6. Fill to depth, then stop. Fill each facing to its planned depth — the depth the shelf is laid out to hold — and no further. The rules in the next section govern this step; it is the one most often skipped in favour of emptying the trolley.
  7. Face and front every filled shelf. Pull product to the front edge of the shelf, labels facing out, packaging straightened, gaps closed up. While facing, check the shelf tag: the tag's item number and price must match the product just placed, and any mismatch gets flagged before the round moves on — a correct product under a wrong tag is still a checkout problem waiting to happen.
  8. Confirm and update. Record what actually moved: if the store tracks stockroom and shelf as separate locations, update the count for the quantity moved so on-hand figures stay accurate; if it does not, the completed list itself is the record. Either way, finish the loop by flagging every item where the stockroom is now empty or nearly empty, so a reorder is placed before the shelf gap becomes a stockout.

What success looks like: at the end of the round, every gap on the scan list has a disposition — filled, phantom corrected, or stockout flagged — nothing is still sitting on the trolley, and tomorrow's scan starts from a shelf that matches today's list. If the same item appears on the gap list three days running, the routine is working and the item's ordering or shelf allocation is the problem to fix next.

Fill to depth: how much to bring out

“Fill the shelf” is incomplete advice, because a shelf has a planned capacity and exceeding it does quiet damage: overfilled facings cannot be rotated, the front row hides the stock behind it, and slow movers get buried under fresh cases of fast ones. Depth here means the number of units the facing is laid out to hold from front lip to back panel. The decision rules:

Shelf stateAction
Facing below half depth, stockroom holds stockFill to full planned depth.
Facing at or near full depthFace and front only — pull forward, straighten, bring nothing out.
Case quantity exceeds the space to full depthBring out only what fills the facing; the rest stays in the stockroom, in its labelled home, noted on the list as available.
Fast seller that empties dailyFill to depth in the main round and schedule it into the top-up pass; if it still empties, the answer is more facing space or a larger allocation, not stacking beyond depth.
Dated stock, new case has a shorter date than the shelfDo not put the shorter-dated stock behind the shelf stock. Hold it, flag the date problem, and sell the earliest dates first — rotation outranks filling.

When to leave stock in the back

Deliberately not filling is part of the routine, not a failure of it. Leave stock in the stockroom when the facing is already at depth; when the item is a slow mover whose shelf stock will cover days of sales; when bringing it out would break date rotation; and when the stock is a reserve for a dated promotion that has not started. The stockroom is not a failure state — it is the buffer that makes the next fill instant. What the stockroom must never be is unknown: every case left behind goes back to a labelled location, so the next scan's locate step takes seconds. A shelf-intelligence programme at one retailer found 43% of out-of-stocks were workable — stock existed somewhere it could be acted on — and lifted on-shelf availability by 5 percentage points, largely by making exactly this find-and-fill loop systematic.

FAQ

How often should the full routine run? Once daily as a full loop, with a short gap-scan top-up before peak trading if the store has one. Shelves must be restocked regularly to replace what customers buy; the fixed time matters more than the frequency — a routine that runs at the same time daily gets done, while “when someone notices” does not.

The system says six in stock, the shelf and stockroom are both empty. Now what? That is phantom inventory. Search the misplacement spots first — wrong aisle, returns area, top stock — then count and correct the record the same day. Until the record is corrected, no automatic reorder will fire and the gap will keep reappearing on the scan list.

Is facing really part of an inventory routine? Yes. Pulling stock forward to the front of the shelf is what makes the next gap scan accurate — a faced shelf shows its true gaps, while an unfaced one hides empty space behind a front row and sends the picker for stock the shelf does not need.
