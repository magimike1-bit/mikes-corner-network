Supplier lead times and safety stock: measuring real lead time and sizing the buffer

Ordering & stock levels · 8 min read

The companion guide on this site covers reorder points — the stock level that triggers the next order. This guide covers the two inputs that make a reorder point trustworthy or worthless: the supplier's lead time, and the safety stock that sits underneath the reorder point as a buffer. Both fail in the same quiet way. The lead time in the spreadsheet is the number the supplier quoted, not the number the delivery history shows; and the safety stock is a round figure chosen once, by feel, and never revisited. The result is a store that stocks out on lines it thought were covered and over-stocks lines it padded out of anxiety. Safety stock exists for a specific, narrow job: it is extra inventory held to absorb variation in demand, in lead time, or in both. It is not a universal percentage that every item deserves, and it cannot eliminate stockouts — it can only make them rarer, at a price. Getting the price right starts with measuring lead time honestly.

Lead time is a measurement, not a quote

Lead time is the amount of time, in days, between placing an order and the stock being delivered — shelf-ready, not merely dispatched. Every formula in this area consumes that number, and every formula inherits its errors. A supplier who quotes three days and usually takes five has not set a three-day lead time; the store has, by writing the quote into its records. The fix is to treat lead time the way sales are treated: as history to be recorded, not as a promise to be believed. Inventory guidance is consistent on the point — use the supplier's actual average performance, not the figure printed on a rate card — but the average alone is only half the measurement. The spread matters as much. A supplier averaging seven days who delivers between six and eight is a different risk from a supplier averaging seven days who delivers between three and fifteen, even though the averages are identical. Longer lead times and greater variability in demand or lead time generally require more safety stock; the variability is not a detail, it is half the calculation.

Keep a lead-time log

The log needs no software. A notebook by the receiving area or a tab in the stock spreadsheet, filled in at the moment of delivery, captures what memory will not:

  1. Record four fields per delivery: supplier, order date, promised date, and the date the goods were actually checked in and shelf-ready. The shelf-ready date is the honest end of lead time — a delivery that sat in the stockroom for two days before check-in consumed two days of selling time, whatever the carrier's tracking said.
  2. Compute the actual lead time for each delivery (check-in date minus order date) and keep a running average per supplier.
  3. Record the maximum as well as the average. The slowest recent delivery is not an embarrassment to be forgotten; it is an input the safety-stock formulas below consume directly.
  4. Note the cause of outliers. One line — "held at depot", "short-shipped, remainder followed a week later", "supplier holiday closure" — separates a pattern worth planning around from a one-off worth ignoring.
  5. Review the log quarterly. Suppliers drift. A log that shows the average creeping from five days to eight over two quarters is an early warning that every reorder point fed by that supplier is quietly going stale.

After a quarter, most small retailers have enough deliveries per main supplier to read the pattern: average lead time, worst recent lead time, and a rough sense of how jumpy the supplier is. That is all the input the simple formula needs, and most of what the statistical one needs.

Two ways to size safety stock

The max-min method is the version most small and medium-sized operations use, because it runs on numbers a lead-time log and a sales history already contain:

Safety stock = (maximum daily sales × maximum lead time) − (average daily sales × average lead time)

The logic is transparent: it holds enough buffer to survive the worst recent combination — the busiest sales day pattern stretched across the slowest delivery — instead of the average one. A worked example from inventory software guidance uses a phone case selling an average of 20 units a day with a 10-day average lead time, peaking at 30 units a day with lead time stretching to 12 days: safety stock = (30 × 12) − (20 × 10) = 360 − 200 = 160 units, giving a reorder point of (20 × 10) + 160 = 360 units once the full calculated buffer is applied. A second published example, a bakery's flour, shows the same arithmetic at kitchen scale: maximum 10 bags a day and maximum lead time 5 days, against an average of 4 bags a day and 3 days, gives (10 × 5) − (4 × 3) = 38 bags of safety stock and a reorder point of (4 × 3) + 38 = 50 bags. Average daily sales should come from a recent, representative window — 30 to 90 days is the range inventory guidance suggests for most products — not from the best week the store ever had.

The statistical (Z-score) method ties the buffer to a chosen service level — the probability of not stocking out during a replenishment cycle. In its common form:

Safety stock = Z × standard deviation of daily demand × √(lead time in days)

where Z is the service-level factor: 1.65 corresponds to a 95 percent service level and 2.33 to 99 percent. A published worked example: with daily demand varying by a standard deviation of 10 units, a 4-day lead time, and a 95 percent service target, safety stock = 1.65 × 10 × √4 = 1.65 × 10 × 2 = 33 units. Two properties of this method matter for a small retailer. First, the service level is an explicit choice with a price tag: moving from 95 to 99 percent does not add a little buffer, it adds a lot. Second, variability grows with the square root of lead time, not in proportion to it — doubling lead time raises the required buffer by a factor of about 1.41, not two. The method needs a demand history long enough to compute a meaningful standard deviation, which is its main cost.

Choosing between them, item by item

The choice is per item, not per store. A practical allocation follows the store's existing tiers. For the small set of bestsellers and must-never-stock-out lines — the items customers ask for by name — the Z-score method at a deliberately chosen service level is worth its data cost, because those are the stockouts that send customers elsewhere. For the broad middle of the range, the max-min method gives a defensible buffer from data the store already has. For slow, cheap, easily substituted lines, either method will usually produce a buffer larger than the item deserves; a minimal buffer, or none with an accepted occasional stockout, is a legitimate decision, because safety stock is protection bought with cash and shelf space, and some items are not worth protecting. What is not legitimate is the uniform rule — the same percentage padded onto every line regardless of its sales rate, its supplier, or its importance. Variability differs by item and by supplier; the buffer should too.

One more check before any buffer is increased: confirm the stock figure the calculation starts from is real. A safety-stock calculation built on a count that includes stock that is damaged, misplaced, or still sitting in a returns pile is arithmetic on a fiction. Count first, then calculate.

Keeping the buffer honest

Safety stock decays in two directions. It silently grows when every scare adds "a bit more" and nothing ever subtracts; and it silently shrinks in usefulness when lead times lengthen and nobody recalculates. The maintenance routine is short:

  • Recalculate on a schedule. Review reorder-point and safety-stock inputs quarterly at the tightest, and every three to six months at the loosest — published guidance lands in that range, and the inputs (sales rates, lead times) genuinely drift on that timescale.
  • Recalculate on an event. A supplier change, a carrier change, a visibly late delivery pattern, or a promotion that resets an item's sales rate all invalidate the old buffer immediately, whatever the calendar says.
  • Shrink the buffer by fixing the cause. The cheapest safety stock is the stock no longer needed because lead time got shorter or steadier. Established ways to improve lead-time performance include ordering smaller amounts more frequently, sourcing from local suppliers where the range allows it, keeping reliable suppliers and dropping chronically unreliable ones, and reducing paperwork delays on the store's own side of the order. A reorder point falls when daily usage drops, lead time shortens, or the buffer is reduced — a faster, steadier supplier literally lowers the inventory the store must hold.
  • Watch the buffer being used. If an item repeatedly eats into its safety stock, the inputs are wrong — demand rose, lead time lengthened, or both — and the answer is a recalculation, not a bigger guess. If the buffer is never touched for a year, it is probably too big, and the excess is cash sitting on a shelf.

What success looks like: every main supplier has a lead-time log with a current average and maximum; every important item has a safety-stock figure whose method and inputs can be stated in one sentence; buffers differ by item tier instead of following one blanket percentage; and at each quarterly review at least the drifted inputs — the suppliers whose lead times moved and the items whose sales rates changed — have been recalculated rather than carried forward by habit.

FAQ

Should safety stock use the supplier's quoted lead time? No. Use the actual performance from the delivery history — the average, and for the max-min method the slowest recent delivery. A quote is a promise; the log is data, and the buffer exists precisely for the days the promise is not kept.

Which formula should a very small shop start with? The max-min method. It needs only average and maximum daily sales and average and maximum lead times, all obtainable from a sales history and a simple lead-time log. Graduate the handful of critical items to the Z-score method once a solid season of daily sales data exists.

Does more safety stock always mean fewer stockouts? Fewer, not zero. Safety stock absorbs variation in demand and delivery; it does not repeal it. Each step up in service level costs disproportionately more stock than the last, which is why the level should be chosen per item tier rather than maximised everywhere.

How often should safety stock be reviewed? Quarterly is a sound default for key items, and no less often than every six months for the rest — plus an immediate recalculation whenever a supplier, carrier, or an item's sales pattern changes materially.
