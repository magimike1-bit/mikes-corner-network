Food delivery after work: the honest hourly math after fuel and wear

Honest math · 10 min read

Delivery apps advertise the gross number: the amount the app pays before your car takes its share. The honest number is what remains after fuel, wear, depreciation and the unpaid minutes at both ends of the shift — and drivers who track all four routinely discover that they keep roughly 60 to 70 percent of gross earnings, with the true hourly rate sometimes landing below minimum wage on slow shifts. None of that makes evening delivery a bad hustle. It makes it a hustle that has to be run on arithmetic instead of advertising. This guide lays out the full cost stack, a worked evening, and the rules drivers use to keep the shift on the right side of the line.

A worked evening, both ways

Take a representative three-hour dinner shift — gross figures in the range Canadian platform comparisons publish for food apps (roughly $15–$22 per active hour for the large food platforms, with base pay per delivery sometimes only a few dollars before tips). Say the app pays $57 for the shift including tips, over 62 kilometres of total driving. The app-screen math says $19 an hour and a good night. Now run the honest version. Fuel at, say, $9. Wear and depreciation at even half the CRA benchmark rate — 35 cents a kilometre to be kind to an older, already-depreciated car — is about $22. Total time door to door is three and a half hours once the drive to the zone and the last trip home are counted. Net before tax: roughly $26 for 3.5 hours, or about $7.50 an hour — a bad shift wearing a good shift's clothes. Change two variables and the same evening flips: a denser zone cutting the driving to 35 kilometres, and peak-period orders lifting gross to $72, and the honest rate roughly doubles. The lesson is not that delivery pays badly. It is that which hours, where, and in what car decide the wage more than effort does — a driver working the dinner peak in a compact, fuel-efficient car in a dense zone and a driver roaming a sprawl suburb at 9 p.m. in a thirsty SUV are doing different jobs at different pay, under the same app logo.

Rules that keep the shift profitable

  1. Set a minimum acceptable order before you log on. A per-order floor — dollars divided by total kilometres including the return — decided at home, not at a red light with a countdown running. Declining bad orders is the whole skill.
  2. Work the demand, not the clock. Dinner peak and weekend peaks are when per-hour figures in the published ranges are actually reachable. A two-hour peak shift usually beats a four-hour off-peak drift.
  3. Log every kilometre from your driveway. A mileage log is both your cost control and your tax record; in Canada, the log is what supports vehicle-expense claims, so the notebook in the console is worth money twice.
  4. Move the tax percentage the same night. Every payout, before anything else is spent. Self-employed income also means both halves of CPP contributions in Canada — another line the gross figure never mentions.
  5. Run a weekly honest-rate check. Total payouts minus fuel and a per-kilometre wear charge, divided by door-to-door hours. Below your floor two weeks running, change the zone, the hours or the hustle — do not simply add more hours to a losing rate; volume multiplies whatever the rate already is, including a negative one.
  6. Check your insurance before the first shift, not after the first accident. Personal auto policies commonly exclude delivery work; ask your insurer what carrying food for pay does to your coverage, in writing. One uninsured accident erases a year of evenings.

What success looks like after a month: a logbook showing kilometres, payouts and door-to-door hours; a tax set-aside account that has never been raided; and an honest hourly figure you can compare, clear-eyed, against every other evening option — including rest, which is also worth something and which this site's burnout guardrails exist to protect.

The second month is the truthful one

Judge this hustle at the end of the second month, not the first weekend. The first weeks flatter every gig: novelty carries the slow nights, the car has not yet presented a bill, and the tax set-aside has not yet been tested by a tempting purchase. By the end of month two the pattern is honest — you know your zone's real order density, your car has expressed its running costs at least once, and your logbook shows whether the peak-hour discipline survived contact with ordinary tired evenings after your day job. If the honest rate still clears your floor at that point, delivery has earned its place in your week. If it has not, the logbook is not a failure record; it is the evidence that lets you leave a losing hustle early and cheaply, which is a skill worth more than any single platform.

FAQ

Is multi-apping the answer? Running two food apps and taking whichever pings first can cut unpaid waiting, and platform comparisons describe top earners combining services. It also doubles the chances of accepting overlapping orders you cannot serve well. Try it only after single-app discipline — floors, zones, logging — is automatic.

Does the car choice really matter that much? It is the largest single variable you control. The per-kilometre cost gap between an efficient compact and a mid-size SUV, across a few hundred delivery kilometres a month, routinely exceeds the difference between platforms.

What about bike delivery? In dense downtown cores it deletes the fuel and most of the wear line at once, which is why its honest rate can beat car delivery despite lower gross. Range, weather and order size are its limits.

Our take: The app pays gross; your car collects its share in private. Price every shift with fuel, wear, unpaid time and tax included, work only the peak hours in a tight zone, and let the weekly honest rate — not the app screen — decide whether you keep going.
