Freelance Proofreading and Editing in the Evenings: Finding Clients Who Pay

$10.20, $20, and $35. Those are three real, published prices for proofreading roughly the same thousand words — a platform starting piece rate (s4), a Canadian per-word service rate (s3), and the top of a professional association's hourly band converted by pace (s1), all checked 2026-10-29. Proofreading in the evenings pays or fails on which of those systems your clients use, so the rates come before the client hunt.

What proofreading is — and what it is not

Scope is the product. Proofreading is the final pass: spelling, punctuation, grammar, consistency and formatting, with minimum changes to make a document correct and readable — one Canadian service describes its basic tier in exactly those terms, and prices the next tier up, which revises sentences for flow, at a higher per-word rate (s3). The moment a client asks for restructuring, rewriting or content advice, the job has become editing, and it should be quoted as editing or declined. Evening hours are too scarce to sell one service and deliver the other.

One boundary is absolute: for academic work, editing a student's own writing for grammar and style is a legitimate service — the Canadian service above returns work with tracked changes and can supply a certificate documenting that changes were grammatical and stylistic (s3). Writing or substantially rewriting the work for the student is not proofreading under any label. Decline it, every time.

The evening math: pace decides the hourly result

Per-word pricing converts to an hourly result through one variable: how fast you can proofread well. The association rate card pairs its $30–35 hourly band with a pace of 9–13 manuscript pages an hour (s1). Use that structure on the direct per-word rate. A 4,000-word document at 2 cents a word pays $80 (s3). If it takes two focused evening hours, the gross result is $40 an hour before costs and tax. If the text is difficult and it takes four hours, the same job pays $20 an hour — below the direct rate's promise and near the platform band (s4). Same price, same document, opposite verdicts.

That is why the first paid jobs are measurement jobs. Time every job, record words per hour by document type, and quote rush or difficult text accordingly. The kill criterion follows from the log: after ten paid jobs, if your realised rate — fees divided by all hours, including quoting and admin — sits below the platform band's experienced range of $20–28 an hour (s4), the direct route is not beating the easy route, and either the niche, the price or the pace has to change before more evenings go in.

The client route: directory, niches, sample edits

  1. Be findable where clients already look. Editors Canada's Online Directory of Editors lets clients search by genre, editorial skills, subjects, working languages and location (s5). A directory profile that names a narrow offer — business documents, non-fiction manuscripts, website copy — matches how that search is actually used.
  2. Watch the job board in the same system. Editors Canada also operates a national job board: posting is free for the client, posts generally appear after a two-day review, and postings are visible only to members (s5). For an evening freelancer, that board is a source of defined, scoped jobs rather than open-ended marketplace bidding.
  3. Pick two document niches you can pace reliably. Business documents carry the highest per-word rate in the Canadian table above (s3); academic essays carry the integrity boundary described earlier. Choose niches where you can quote from your own timed log, not from hope.
  4. Offer a short sample edit before the first paid job. The Canadian service in the table lets new clients request a sample before paying (s3). A one-page sample, returned with tracked changes, does the same work for a freelancer: it proves pace and judgement, and it surfaces a mismatch in scope before money is involved.
  5. Quote in writing: service tier, word count, rate, turnaround, revision limit. Turnaround must be written in evening terms — for calibration, the Canadian service's standard return is one day per 8,000 words (s3), a full-time pace. Quote the pace your evenings can actually repeat.

What success looks like: the client can see exactly what was changed and why, the document returns on the evening you promised, and your log shows the job paid at the pace you quoted.

Scope lines and taxes that protect the evenings

Three lines, stated in every quote. One revision pass is included; new text added after delivery is a new job. Rush turnaround — anything that requires giving up a planned evening off — is priced as rush or declined. And the service tier is named: proofreading means the final pass, not a rewrite, per the distinction in the rate table (s3).

On taxes, this is self-employment income: reported on your personal T1 return using Form T2125, with income and expenses tracked and GST/HST handled when required (s6). Per-job logs do double duty here — they are both the pricing evidence from the math section and the record the tax filing needs.

The decision, in one line

Start where the clients are — the directory and the board (s5) — sell one clearly scoped tier at a direct per-word rate (s3), and let ten timed jobs tell you whether your pace makes the evenings pay. If the log says it does not, the honest options are a better niche or a higher rate, never more volume at a losing pace.
