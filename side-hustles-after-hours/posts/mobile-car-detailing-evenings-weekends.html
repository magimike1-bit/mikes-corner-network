Mobile car detailing on evenings and weekends: the kit, the winter cliff, and the per-job math

Hustle ideas · 9 min read

$1,200 to $3,500. That is the launch-budget band one Canadian startup guide puts on a part-time mobile detailing setup — equipment, setup, insurance, supplies and a reserve for a slow month included. A leaner test setup runs $500 to $1,000, and a professional mobile unit runs $5,000 to $15,000 or more (Hustle Explained). The spread is the first lesson of this hustle: detailing can be an evening business or a small fleet business, and the kit catalogue will happily sell the second to someone who only has time for the first. This guide runs the launch as a runbook — jobs first, kit second — then prices a single detail down to its contribution, counts how many details an evening week can honestly hold, and plans for the Canadian winter that changes what the business sells.

Start with the jobs, not the van: a five-step evening launch runbook

  1. Set up the free storefront first. A Google Business Profile is free, and a Canadian detailing guide's customer sequence starts there: profile first, then a handful of friends' vehicles detailed free or at cost in exchange for a review, before-and-after photos, and permission to use the photos. Every paying customer after that gets a review request the day the job finishes, not the following week (FixyFlow).
  2. Register the business shape that fits the scale. In Ontario, a new sole proprietorship name registration is $60, and a sole proprietorship is a workable starting form in most provinces; GST/HST registration becomes mandatory once taxable sales pass $30,000 (FixyFlow; CRA).
  3. Work from the customer's hose and outlet. Most new mobile detailers can start using customer water and power for residential driveway jobs. A portable water tank matters for condo lots and commercial accounts, and a generator matters where there is no outlet — and buying either on day one is named in the Canadian guide as one of the most common money-wasted decisions in the trade (FixyFlow). Buy the tank the week a booked job genuinely needs it.
  4. Send the tap-and-outlet text the day before. Outdoor taps can be seized, locked, or shut off for winter. A reminder message asking whether the tap is accessible solves more water problems than equipment does (FixyFlow). For an evening operator with one slot a night, a dead tap is not an inconvenience — it is the whole evening's revenue, gone.
  5. Price three packages, middle first. Build three packages and price the middle one as the anchor most customers are meant to buy; never lead with the cheapest. Pricing too low is identified as the single most common reason new mobile detailers burn out, because it locks in customers who leave the moment a competitor undercuts (FixyFlow).

What success looks like: after the first month there are paid jobs on the calendar, reviews attached to them, a kit that still fits in a vehicle already owned, and no tank or generator sitting unused in the garage. Equipment bought ahead of booked work is the failure mode this sequence exists to prevent.

A total-cost-of-ownership view matters here: towels wear out, chemicals empty, and the vehicle doing the travelling consumes fuel and wear on every job, so the kit price is the smallest part of the first year's true cost.

The per-job math: what a $180 detail leaves behind

Many basic detailing jobs begin around $120 to $200 (Hustle Explained). Take the guide's worked example at the top of that band. A $180 detail, minus $35 in fuel, supplies and card fees, leaves a $145 contribution margin — gross margin (what a job leaves after the costs tied directly to that job) before fixed costs, tax and the operator's own pay. Three such jobs cover $435 in fixed costs in that example; they do not yet pay the operator a dollar of wages.

Run the break-even in units, because units are what an evening calendar holds. If monthly fixed costs — insurance, phone, booking tools, laundry, restock — totalled, for illustration, $290, the month needs two $180 details at a $145 contribution just to stand still, and every detail after that is where pay begins. The illustration uses the sourced $145 contribution; the fixed-cost figure is the reader's own to insert. Two disciplines protect that margin: a minimum booking price and a defined service radius, with jobs grouped by neighbourhood, so a low-priced job never consumes an entire afternoon in travel; and a deposit policy, so a late cancellation does not convert a booked evening into an unpaid one (Hustle Explained).

Capacity is the real cap: how many details an evening week holds

The constraint is not demand. It is sellable hours. After travel, setup, messages, towel washing and cleanup, a worker with 5 hours a week completes about 1 to 2 standard details; 10 hours supports about 2 to 3; 20 hours supports about 4 to 6 (Hustle Explained). A two-hour driveway service can consume 2.5 to 4.5 hours from departure to return, which means a single weekday evening holds one job, done properly, and a weekend day holds two at most.

Price from that cap, not from hope. At the sourced capacity, a 10-hour week is a 2-to-3-job week. An operator who quotes prices that only work at six jobs a week has built a full-time business plan on part-time hours, and the gap will be paid in burnout or in corners cut on paying customers' cars. The honest sequence is the reverse: fix the hours, count the jobs those hours hold, then set the package prices so that count clears fixed costs with pay left over — or do not launch.

Insurance is three separate questions

A Canadian detailing guide names three covers as non-negotiable, and they answer three different risks. General liability covers damage caused to a customer's vehicle or property and a customer slipping on a wet surface. A commercial vehicle endorsement matters because a personal auto policy explicitly excludes business use — a crash on the way to a job can be refused under a personal-only policy. Tools-and-equipment coverage replaces gear stolen from the vehicle or damaged on a job site (FixyFlow).

For an evening operator the vehicle endorsement is the trap, because the family vehicle is the business vehicle. The question to put to an insurer, in writing, before the first paid job is whether paid detailing trips need a business-use endorsement or commercial coverage. An answer obtained after an accident is worth considerably less.

Winter is a different business: the November-to-March interior plan

Interior detailing is described as the most reliable mobile-detailing revenue stream in Canada because it works year-round, even when exterior work is frozen out from November to March (FixyFlow). Outdoor taps are shut off at the basement for winter, which independently pushes the season indoors: the winter product is an interior detail performed in a customer's garage or on a driveway with the vehicle as the workspace, sold to the customers winter creates — parents with toddlers and minivans, dog owners, people preparing a car for a spring sale or a lease return.

The planning consequence is simple and rarely stated: an evening detailing business launched in April has roughly seven exterior months to recover its launch band before the product changes. Price and save accordingly. A launch that only breaks even if November behaves like July will not break even.

No income promises here. This is general information, not financial, tax or insurance advice. Detailing prices, startup ranges and terms cited were checked 31 October 2026 and change; confirm insurance treatment and local water and wastewater rules for your own circumstances before taking bookings. In Canada, detailing income is self-employment income: the CRA expects gig and platform-economy workers to report all of it, file Form T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities), keep proper records, and register for GST/HST once taxable supplies exceed $30,000 over four calendar quarters (CRA).

The kill criterion

Set it before the first job. Track, for eight consecutive weeks: jobs completed, door-to-door hours per job, and contribution after fuel, supplies and card fees. Stop — or reprice once, and retest for four weeks — if the logged contribution per door-to-door hour sits below what the same evenings would earn at any ordinary part-time job, or if the capacity cap means fixed costs cannot be covered at the package prices customers will actually pay. A detailing route that only profits when travel time is valued at zero is not a side hustle. It is a discount being given to strangers, funded by evenings.
