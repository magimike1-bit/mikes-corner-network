Print-on-demand after hours: the honest costs before the first sale

Hustle ideas · 9 min read

Print-on-demand sounds like the perfect evening hustle: no inventory, no packing boxes at midnight, no trips to the post office before work. A design goes up, a stranger orders, a print provider makes and ships the product, and the difference lands in the seller's account. That part is real. The part the launch guides rush past is the stack of small charges standing between the buyer's click and that difference — and how few evening hours there are to earn them back. This guide prices the model the way an after-work seller actually experiences it: one product, every fee taken out in the open, checked on 17 October 2026, with a hard rule about which upgrades to refuse until orders — not optimism — pay for them.

What print-on-demand actually charges, in order

Start with the fulfilment platform. Printify's free plan costs $0 a month, allows up to five connected stores and unlimited product designs, and gives full catalog access; free users pay only the base product cost plus shipping when an order arrives (Printify). Its premium plan costs $39 a month, or $299 a year — about $24.99 a month — and returns up to 20% off all products and up to ten stores (Printify). Hold that premium number; it gets its own section below, because it is the easiest money an evening seller can waste.

Next, the marketplace where buyers find the product. On Etsy, the listing fee is $0.20 USD per listing, charged when the listing is created or renewed and again every four months. The transaction fee is 6.5% of the total sale price, including any shipping charged to the buyer. Payment processing varies by country — 3% plus $0.25 in the US, and 3.5% plus C$0.25 for Canada in the breakdown checked for this article — and a regulatory operating fee of 0.05% to 1.97% can apply depending on location (beancount.io, summarising Etsy's published fee policy). None of these numbers is large. Together, on a low-priced product, they are the business.

Then comes the charge most beginners never budget: getting found. Custom Ink's cost breakdown puts a typical designed tee at $24.99 to $29.99, with base and fulfilment costs of $8 to $11 and marketplace fees of roughly $3, leaving $8 to $12 of gross profit before ads — and then notes that paid advertising of $10 to $15 per order can erase that margin completely (Custom Ink). A product that looks profitable in a spreadsheet can be a small, reliable loss-maker the moment paid traffic enters the picture.

The worked example: one tee, every fee taken out

One published breakdown works the full stack on a single shirt, and the numbers are worth copying into a notebook before listing anything (TeeInBlue):

  • Sale price: $28.00
  • Base product cost: $12.00
  • Shipping: $4.99
  • Etsy transaction fee: $1.82 — 6.5% of the sale
  • Payment processing: $1.09 — 3% plus $0.25
  • Listing fee: $0.20
  • Advertising: $2.00 per unit sold
  • Left over: $5.90 — a 21% net margin

Read that table twice. The first time, notice that nothing went wrong: no refund, no misprint, no discounted sale. The second time, notice how much of the $28 the seller never touches — and that the example already assumes a modest $2 advertising cost, not the $10 to $15 per order Custom Ink warns about. The same source makes a second point evening sellers should take seriously: figures published by Printful put posters and wall art among the strongest-margin categories, while basic apparel carries the hardest price competition — basic apparel is, in its words, the worst place to start, despite being where nearly every beginner starts (TeeInBlue). A design that works on a poster does not have to fight the entire internet's t-shirt pricing to earn its evening back.

Free plan vs premium: the 17-order line

Printify's own break-even example does the premium math plainly. A product with a $12 base cost drops to about $9.60 with the 20% premium discount — a saving of $2.40 per order. Against a $39 monthly subscription, that saving needs roughly 17 orders a month to break even; on annual billing, at about $24.99 a month, the line drops to around 11 orders a month (Printify).

Translate that into evenings. Seventeen orders a month is not a testing phase; it is a shop that already sells most days. An after-work seller listing a first handful of designs has, by definition, zero orders a month. Paying $39 a month at zero orders is not an investment in margin — there is no margin to discount yet. The decision rule is simple enough to write on a sticky note: stay on the free plan until completed orders, counted over a full month, pass the break-even line for the billing cycle being considered. Eleven sustained orders a month makes annual premium a conversation. Seventeen makes monthly premium defensible. Below that, the subscription is a second job's wage paid to a platform.

The evening budget nobody lists: samples, IP checks, and time

The honest startup budget is small, but it is not zero. One budget guide puts a realistic small starting spend at $15 to $30 for a domain plus sample orders, and calls a $39-a-month storefront platform the wrong small-budget pick (HustleMint). The sample order deserves emphasis. A design file can look perfect on a screen and print muddy, off-centre, or a size smaller than the mockup suggests. Ordering one unit of the exact product, from the exact provider, before strangers pay for it converts an unknown into a known cost — and it is far cheaper than refunding a disappointed buyer and absorbing a one-star review in a shop's first month.

The legal check is the other unbudgeted step. Using trademarked phrases, copyrighted characters, celebrity likenesses or team logos can create legal trouble even when the artwork is redrawn, and the same guide recommends searching the US trademark database before printing a design (HustleMint). Ten minutes of searching per design is evening time, so count it — but it is cheaper than building a catalog on phrases a seller has no right to sell.

Finally, count the design time itself. A design that takes three evenings to produce and sells twice has not failed because of fees; it has failed the only test an evening hustle has to pass, which is whether the hours came back. There is no public dataset for average print-on-demand seller earnings, so any guide quoting an average income figure should be treated as inventing it (HustleMint). The honest scoreboard is personal: hours in, net dollars out, per design.

A four-evening launch procedure

This procedure fits the work into four ordinary evenings and ends with a clear success check:

  1. Evening one — pick one niche and one product type. Choose a buyer who can be described in one sentence and a product with margin room; remember the worked example, where wall art out-margined basic apparel.
  2. Evening two — run the IP check and build two or three designs. Search each phrase and image concept against the trademark database before designing around it, and keep designs original.
  3. Evening three — do the full-fee math before listing. Write the sale price, base cost, shipping, 6.5% transaction fee, payment processing for the seller's country, and the $0.20 listing fee in a row. If the leftover number needs advertising the budget cannot carry, reprice or drop the product now, while dropping it is free.
  4. Evening four — order the sample and list on the free plan. Connect one store on Printify's free plan, publish the listing, and place the sample order for the hero product.

What success looks like: the sample arrives matching the mockup closely enough to sell without embarrassment, the listing is live with the full-fee math recorded beside it, and total spend so far is the sample, the listing fees, and nothing monthly. Anything more than that, before a first order, is spending ahead of evidence.

When to walk away from a design

Evening sellers need a kill rule in advance, because hope is not a schedule. A workable framework: a design earns more time when it produces clicks, favourites, or a first sale inside its first listing period — the four months before the $0.20 listing fee comes due again. A design with no signal at renewal is not renewed automatically; the fee is small, but the catalog of dead listings is how an evening shop quietly becomes an evening chore. Reinvest the next evening in a new design for the same buyer instead. The catalog grows by addition of tested ideas, not by renewal of ignored ones.

No income promises here. This is general information, not financial or tax advice. Print-on-demand returns vary by design, niche, pricing and luck, and many shops sell nothing at all. In Canada, print-on-demand income is self-employment income: the CRA expects gig workers to report all of it, file Form T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities), and keep proper records of income and expenses (CRA). Set money aside for tax from the first payout, and confirm GST/HST obligations with an accountant as sales grow.

Print-on-demand after hours works when it is run like a small, honest shop: free plans until volume argues otherwise, every fee counted before a listing goes live, a sample in hand before a stranger's money is taken. Run it on hype and subscriptions, and the only reliable income in the arrangement belongs to the platforms.
