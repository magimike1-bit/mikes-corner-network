Selling digital templates in the evenings: fees, licensing, and the not-passive parts

Hustle ideas · 9 min read

A digital template is one of the few products an evening seller can build once and sell more than once: a planner layout, a resume design, a set of social-media posts a buyer opens and edits. There is no stock to store and nothing to ship. That much of the pitch is true. The rest of the pitch — passive, effortless, set it and forget it — survives contact with three realities: a licensing rule that decides what may legally be sold and in what form, a fee stack that punishes cheap products, and buyers who ask questions in the evenings too. This guide works through all three, with fees checked on 17 October 2026, and a build procedure sized for the hours after a day job.

Why templates fit evenings — and where the passive story breaks

The strongest honest version of the case comes from the industry itself. Announcing a beginner's guide to selling Canva templates on Etsy, Davis Sarmins, Director of Growth Marketing at Printify, put it this way: “Canva templates are one of the few Etsy products where the main cost is a seller's time. Once the designs exist, nearly every sale after the first is margin” (Printify, via EIN Presswire). Read that quote carefully, because it contains both the opportunity and the warning. The main cost is time — the seller's evenings. A template that takes six evenings to build needs a lot of “nearly all margin” sales before those evenings are repaid, and the sales are not guaranteed to arrive at all.

The delivery side really is light. Etsy delivers digital files as instant downloads after purchase, with a maximum file size of 20MB per file (Findstack). There is no packing and no postage. What does not disappear is everything around the file: the instruction sheet a buyer needs to open and edit the template, the listing that must be renewed, and the message that arrives when a buyer cannot find the download link. Budgeting one short maintenance block a week — questions, small fixes, a listing tweak — is the difference between a template shop that fits an evening life and one that interrupts it.

The licence table to check before designing anything

Most template sellers design in Canva, and Canva's content rules decide what the finished product may be. Secondary guides contradict each other on this — some state flatly that Pro elements cannot be used in templates for sale at all — so the summary below follows the guide that quotes Canva's own guidance for sellers directly (Trendlytic, summarising Canva):

What is inside the designSell as a Canva template linkSell as a downloadable file
A design built around Canva Pro contentYes — Pro content may be used in template designs shared as Canva template linksNo — templates with Pro content cannot be sold as a PDF or other file types
A single Canva element with nothing designed around itNo — an element on its own is not a template
Canva elements repackaged as clipart or stockNo — elements cannot be resold as clipart or stock collections

Two definitions do the heavy lifting. Canva defines a template as a design with a pre-determined layout and style that is intended to be further edited or customised by an end-user — a lone illustration on a blank page, labelled editable, does not pass that test. And the delivery method is part of the licence, not a preference: a design containing Pro content travels as a template link, so the buyer opens a copy inside Canva, rather than as an exported file (Trendlytic). One more quirk from the same guidance summary is worth knowing before choosing a template to sell: where a template link was created using a work email domain, recipients on that same domain may need a paid plan to open it. The practical evening rule is simple — design the first products either from original work or in a way that stays clearly inside the link-sharing rule, and check Canva's current terms before every new product line, not from memory.

What a sale actually pays: Etsy vs Gumroad

Fees checked 17 October 2026. The two main routes charge for different things, and the comparison table is the whole decision in miniature:

Sources: Etsy fee math as worked by HomeHustleHub from Etsy's published US fee schedule; Gumroad's own Help Center fee page. The pattern matters more than any single row. Etsy charges rent — the listing fee, renewed every four months whether the template sells or not — in exchange for a stream of searching buyers. Gumroad charges nothing until a sale, then takes a larger slice of it, and its 30% Discover fee shows what marketplace discovery costs when the seller did not bring the buyer. An evening seller with no audience usually needs Etsy's traffic first. A seller with an audience that already trusts them may keep more per sale through direct Gumroad links — and can run both, listing where buyers search and linking direct where buyers already follow.

Price bundles, not singles: the flat-fee problem

Run the Etsy arithmetic at three price points, using the US fee schedule worked by HomeHustleHub: on a $12 template, total fees come to $1.59 — about 13% of the price. On a $3 template, fees take about 25%. On a $15 template, about 12.5%. If the $12 sale arrived through Offsite Ads, fees rise to $3.39 — about 28% (HomeHustleHub). The villain is arithmetic, not Etsy: the flat charges — $0.20 here, $0.25 there — are the same size on a $3 sale as on a $30 one, so cheap singles hand a quarter of their price to the fee stack before the seller's evenings are paid anything at all.

That is why bundling is a pricing decision rather than a growth trick. Pricing examples for digital downloads put individual items at $5 to $15, bundles at $50 to $60, and premium collections above $100 (Findstack), and the beginner guide announced by Printify covers template pricing tiers from $5 to $70 (EIN Presswire). A bundle of five related templates, sold as one listing, pays the flat fees once, renews once every four months, and gives the buyer a reason to choose it over a crowded page of near-identical singles. The evening math improves twice: more dollars kept per sale, and fewer listings to maintain.

A five-evening build-and-list procedure

  1. Evening one — pick a buyer problem, not a pretty design. Choose a template that saves a specific person a specific hour: a landlord's rental paperwork, a job-seeker's resume, a small shop's weekly social posts. Vague prettiness competes with everyone; a named problem competes with few.
  2. Evening two — check the licence before designing. Decide the delivery form first — template link or file — then design inside the licence table above. If the design needs Pro content, it ships as a link.
  3. Evenings three and four — build the template and the instructions. The instruction sheet is part of the product: how to open the link, how to edit, what a finished result should look like. Most buyer questions are instruction-sheet failures discovered late.
  4. Evening five — price the bundle and list it. Run the fee math at the intended price, confirm the flat fees leave a sensible share, write the listing around the buyer's problem, and publish.

What success looks like: a live listing whose delivery form matches the licence, a fee worksheet showing what one sale actually pays at the listed price, and an instruction sheet complete enough that a stranger could use the template without sending a message. If a stranger would need help, the product is not finished — the evenings just have not been billed yet.

The maintenance hour: questions, renewals, updates

Plan for three recurring jobs, none of which appear in the passive-income pitch. Listings renew every four months at $0.20 each, so a large catalog of unsold singles is a small but real rent bill — another argument for fewer, stronger bundles. Buyer questions arrive on the platform's schedule, not the seller's, and a short, kind, pre-written answer to the three common questions (where is the file, how is the link opened, what can be edited) turns most of them into two-minute tasks. And templates age: a date-specific design needs a new year, a platform's export sizes change, a font disappears. One maintenance block a month, spent on the best-selling listing first, keeps a small catalog earning without colonising every evening.

No income promises here. This is general information, not financial, tax or legal advice. Template sales vary by niche, quality and visibility, and many listings never sell. Licensing summarised here is general information about platform terms that change — read the current terms of any tool before selling work made with it. In Canada, template income is self-employment income: the CRA expects gig workers to report all of it, file Form T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities), and keep proper records (CRA).

Sold honestly, templates are a good evening product: build carefully, licence correctly, price so the flat fees stop eating the sale, and keep one small maintenance block on the calendar. Sold on the passive fantasy, they become unpaid customer support for a $3 download.
