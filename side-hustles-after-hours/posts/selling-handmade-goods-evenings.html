Selling handmade goods in the evenings: craft-fair costs, jurying and pricing

Making · 10 min read

Making things in the evening and selling them at weekend markets sounds like the gentlest side hustle there is — until the first fair's arithmetic arrives: a booth fee, an application fee, a table, a card reader's percentage, a tank of fuel, and a full Saturday, all spent before the first customer appears. Canadian craft-fair costs span an enormous range, from $20–$50 tables at small community events to $200–$300 or more at established juried shows, with premium holiday shows far above that. The hustle works when the making stays in the evenings where it belongs, the selling events are chosen like investments, and every item is priced from its real costs outward. This guide walks through the fair economics, the juried-application process most beginners do not expect, and a pricing method that survives contact with a slow Saturday.

Read the table as a filter. A $40 community-hall table needs only a handful of sales to clear its costs and is the correct first classroom. A $300 juried booth needs a proven product, proven prices and a display that has already survived a cheaper room. The expensive mistake is starting at the expensive show, where the lesson costs ten times more and teaches exactly the same things.

The jury: the step nobody warns beginners about

Established shows are juried — you apply, and a panel decides. The process is more formal than new sellers expect. One national artisan association describes submitting at least five high-resolution photos of each type of craft you want to bring, plus booth-display photos if you have shown before; you may sell only the categories that were juried in — accepted for candles and cards does not entitle you to bring the Christmas decorations too. A regional show series reports acceptance rates under 35 percent in competitive rounds, limits sellers per category to keep the room diverse, and waitlists the rest. Two implications for an after-hours seller. First, photography is a production task, not an afterthought: five strong photos per product line, taken in the evenings like everything else, are your admission ticket. Second, rejection is normal market information, not a verdict on the work — the jury is balancing a room, and the same products are routinely accepted on a later application with new photos and new pieces.

Pricing that survives a slow day

  1. Cost every item in four parts: materials, packaging, a labour rate for your making time that you would actually accept, and a share of overhead — booth fees, processing, market travel — spread across expected sales.
  2. Then check the price against the room. If the honest price is double everything around you, the product belongs in a different show or a different format, not at a discount that pays you $3 an hour.
  3. Build a price ladder: a few small items under the impulse threshold, the core line in the middle, and one or two showcase pieces. Small items carry slow days; showcase pieces carry reputation and the occasional excellent day.
  4. Track sell-through by show, not by feeling. A simple notebook — what you brought, what sold, what people picked up and put down — turns the second season's show selection into data instead of hope.
  5. Register the tax side early. In Canada, selling regularly is business income; track revenue and expenses from the first show, and watch the small-supplier threshold for GST/HST registration as sales grow rather than discovering it afterward.

What success looks like after a first season: three or four shows tested at increasing fee levels, a photo set and booth that a jury has accepted at least once, a price list you can defend line by line, and a sell-through notebook that tells you exactly which products earn their table space. The evenings stay making evenings — the moment production expands into every night to feed an unproven show calendar, the hustle has quietly become an unpaid second job with inventory.

Protecting the making from the selling

The structural risk of this hustle is not a bad fair; it is success at the wrong scale. A product that sells out twice will tempt you into production evenings every night of the week, at which point the craft has quietly become piecework and the hourly return — priced honestly with the method above — usually turns out to be worse than the overtime or rest it replaced. Set the production ceiling before the season starts: a fixed number of making evenings per week, a maximum inventory per show, and permission to let a popular item sell out rather than chase it. Scarcity at a market table is a mild commercial inconvenience. An exhausted maker with a garage of unsold stock for three fairs running is the actual failure mode, and it is entirely preventable with a ceiling written down in advance.

FAQ

Should I start online instead? Online marketplaces trade the booth fee for listing fees, transaction percentages and shipping labour, and they sell while you sleep — but they cannot show you a customer's face when a price makes them hesitate. Many sellers use cheap local shows to learn what people value, then list the proven lines online.

How many items should I bring? Enough depth in the lines that sell — a sold-out core product by noon is lost revenue, while a broad, shallow table of experiments is a research project. Bring depth in two or three lines and a small test batch of anything new.

Is the holiday season the best time to start? November and December shows have the most buyers and the highest fees, and they cluster costs into a few expensive weeks. Learn the ropes at spring and summer community events; spend holiday money once the product and prices are proven.

Our take: Treat every fair as a priced experiment: cheap rooms first, jury photos as a real production task, prices built from materials, labour, fees and processing — never from hope. The making is the pleasure; the arithmetic is what lets it stay one.
