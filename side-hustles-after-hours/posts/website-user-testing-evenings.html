Website user testing in the evenings: per-test pay, unpaid screeners, and the screener-time rule

Hustle ideas · 8 min read

Website user testing — recording your screen and voice while you try to complete tasks on a site or app and say what you think — sounds like the lightest evening hustle there is. No clients, no inventory, sessions measured in minutes. The part the signup pages do not headline is the funnel in front of every paid test: short screening questionnaires, mostly unpaid, that decide whether a test is offered at all. For a worker with a couple of evening hours, the screener funnel — not the per-test rate — decides what this hustle pays. This guide takes the three main platforms from their own pages, costs the unpaid minutes honestly, and sets the boundary rules that keep recorded testing away from the day job.

What the work actually is — and why evenings suit it, with limits

The task itself is deliberately ordinary. Platforms stress that no usability experience is needed — Userlytics' tester FAQ says many clients prefer testers with no prior experience, because they want realistic reactions from real users (Userlytics). What is being bought is honest, spoken reaction: narrating expectations before a click, naming confusion when it happens, and finishing the set tasks. Typical Userlytics tests run 20 to 40 minutes; Userbrain's run about 5 to 20 minutes (Userlytics; Userbrain). Sessions are short, home-based and schedulable around dinner — a genuine evening fit.

The limits are structural. Invitations are not shifts: Userlytics states plainly that it cannot say how often a tester will be invited, because selection is a random process drawing a limited number of testers from its database, and a test slot can pass to another tester if it is not completed within a short time (Userlytics). A platform review of the category adds that test availability is unpredictable — a busy day can be followed by empty ones (Earning a Living Online). This is opportunistic income that arrives when it arrives, in a quiet room, on short notice — not a rota an evening can be planned around.

The screener funnel: costing the unpaid minutes

Before a paid test comes a screener — a short questionnaire checking whether the tester matches the customer the test was written for. Screeners are the funnel's toll, and they are unpaid. One review reports screening questions disqualifying applicants from 50–80% of available tests; another, reporting its own testing, puts screeners at 1–4 minutes each with an 18% qualification rate it describes as roughly average for North American demographics (Earning a Living Online; Legit Reviews Lab). Userlytics' own FAQ underlines why gaming the funnel backfires: screeners must be answered honestly, or the tester may receive a poor rating and not be invited again (Userlytics).

Cost an evening with those reported figures — illustrative arithmetic, not a promise of invitations:

  • Screeners per paid test: at an 18% qualification rate, landing one paid test takes about five to six screeners.
  • Unpaid time per paid test: five to six screeners at roughly 2–3 minutes each is about 12–18 minutes of unpaid evening before the paid work starts.
  • The paid test: a standard recorded test reported at $10 for about 20 minutes (Legit Reviews Lab).
  • The real rate: $10 ÷ roughly 35–40 total minutes is about $15–$17 per hour of evening actually spent — respectable, and roughly half the $30-an-hour the headline math suggests. The same review's own estimate, $14–$18 an hour after screener time, lands in the same place (Legit Reviews Lab).
  • The volume caveat: the same reviews report monthly earnings in the tens of dollars for typical testers — one long-time tester averaging $40–$70 a month with consistent effort (Earning a Living Online). The hourly rate is decent; the hours on offer are few. This is grocery money with a good rate, not a rent payment.

The rule that follows is a screener budget: cap unpaid screener time at about 15–20 minutes per evening, then stop for the night regardless of whether a test landed. The cap keeps the funnel from silently converting a two-hour evening into two hours of unpaid questionnaires — the survey trap in a better suit.

The evening routine: a capped, rated, repeatable procedure

  1. Apply once, properly. UserTesting's checklist is email confirmation, PayPal setup, profile, and the unpaid practice test; note that an unsuccessful applicant may reapply only once, so the practice test deserves a quiet room and full attention (UserTesting). Userbrain's qualification test is reviewed with feedback before paid tests arrive (Userbrain).
  2. Complete the profile completely and honestly. Screeners match against it, invitations follow it, and dishonest answers risk ratings, removed payments and bans — Userlytics removes duplicate accounts and the payments they generated, and may ban the tester permanently (Userlytics).
  3. Check in during one defined window. Slots expire quickly, so one or two short dashboard checks beat an evening of refreshing. Tests that cannot be finished inside the window are left for someone else.
  4. Before recording, verify the setup: quiet room, working microphone, and a device that meets the platform's minimums — UserTesting's include 8 GB of RAM and at least 5 Mbps upload speed on desktop, and Chromebooks are not supported (UserTesting). Userlytics may deny payment for poor setup — background noise, no audio, a blocked webcam — so the check is financial, not fussy (Userlytics Terms).
  5. Narrate like it is the product — because it is. Clients rate every test, and ratings drive invitations on both platforms checked. Say what was expected before clicking, say when something is confusing, and finish every task.
  6. Log the evening: screeners attempted, minutes spent, tests landed, amounts and their payment dates. After a month, the log shows the true hourly rate and whether the screener budget needs tightening.

What success looks like: a month of logs showing screener time inside its cap, every landed test rated and paid on the platform's stated clock, and a computed hourly rate the worker can compare honestly against the other uses of the same evenings.

Day-job boundaries: confidentiality, devices and your own face on camera

Three rules keep this hustle from touching the 9-to-5. First, confidentiality: Userlytics' tester terms require testers not to copy, screenshot, record or disclose the testing application, prototypes, files or concepts to anyone, by any means (Userlytics Terms). What appears in a test — often an unreleased product — is not dinner conversation, a social post, or portfolio material. Second, equipment: tests record the screen, the voice and sometimes the tester's face by webcam, and the recorder software belongs on a personal machine. Running recorded third-party work on an employer's laptop, over an employer's network, tangles the employer's property with a stranger's confidential prototype — personal device, personal connection, every time. Third, availability honesty: a live conversation booked for a workday lunch hour is a meeting the day job did not agree to. Evening testing stays in the evening; a test that can only be done by borrowing the workday is a test to decline.

None of this is hypothetical fine print. The same terms set the other payment risks: payment can be denied for misleading profile information, substandard testing, expired tests or incomplete reports (Userlytics Terms). The protections and the obligations are the same document — read it once, before the first paid test, not after the first denied one.

FAQ

How fast does the money actually arrive? On UserTesting's own payment FAQ, 14 days after the test, automatically, with no minimum threshold; Userlytics pays approved tests once every 15 days and its terms cap payment at 30 days after completion (UserTesting; Userlytics). Any guide quoting a faster clock for these platforms is not quoting the platform.

Is the practice test paid? No. UserTesting states the practice test submitted with the application earns nothing, and Userbrain's qualification test is a review gate before paid tests (UserTesting; Userbrain). Budget both as setup time.

Can this be done from Canada? UserTesting welcomes contributors from around the world, tests in English, French and German, and pays in US dollars via PayPal, which converts at its own rate (UserTesting). Canadian testers report the same income to the CRA as any other gig income — see the note below.

No income promises here. This is general information, not financial or tax advice. Test availability is unpredictable, rates and terms change, and platforms can deny payment under their terms — confirm the current terms before relying on them. In Canada, user-testing income is self-employment income: the CRA expects gig workers to report all of it, file Form T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities), and keep proper records (CRA).

User testing in the evenings is a fair trade when its real shape is respected: a decent hourly rate on a small number of hours, a screener funnel kept on a short leash, and a hard line between recorded side work and the day job's equipment, hours and confidences.
