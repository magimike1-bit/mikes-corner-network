Weekend Furniture Assembly Gigs: The Rate You Keep, the Travel You Do Not

Local services · 7 min read

Published 2026-11-03 · Last updated 2026-11-03

Disclaimer: General information only, not financial, tax or legal advice. Earnings vary and are never guaranteed. Platform figures below were checked on 2026-11-03 and change. Check your employment contract and local rules before starting.

$25 is the door price. Taskrabbit charges a one time $25 registration fee when a new Tasker registers (s1), and a Canadian review of the platform describes that fee as non-refundable (s3). Everything after the door is a pricing and geography problem, and that is good news, because both of those are under your control. This guide runs the setup in order, then works one Saturday honestly.

Set up as an assembler, in order

  1. Check your city first. Taskrabbit in Canada is available in Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal, Calgary, Edmonton and a few other big cities, not across the whole country (s3). Outside those markets, the direct route in the comparison table below is the whole plan, not a fallback.
  2. Register and verify. The platform flow is account, profile, category selection, then identity and eligibility verification, with the $25 fee charged at registration (s1). Paying the fee buys a listing, not bookings.
  3. List furniture assembly only, at first. Furniture assembly ranked as Taskrabbit's most popular category in 2024, and IKEA customers can book and pay for assembly at checkout (s1). One category, done fast and photographed well, builds the review count that a wide, vague profile never does.
  4. Set a minimum booking before the first request arrives. Two billed hours is a workable floor for a trip across town. The worked Saturday below shows why: travel is the line that decides the day.
  5. Open availability for Saturday and one weekday evening only. A day job plus two weekend days of gigs is a seven day week. Protect one full day from the start.

What success looks like: a Saturday request arrives inside your stated area, meets your minimum, and you can accept it without checking a map or renegotiating the scope in chat.

The rate you keep, and the fee the client sees

Taskrabbit's own Canada fact sheet puts average Tasker earnings at $45 an hour in Canada and $48 an hour in Toronto, with earnings varying by location (s1). Treat those as platform averages across all categories, not an assembly quote and not a promise. A Canadian review lists IKEA assembly in Montreal at $27 an hour (s3), which is the useful warning: city moves this rate more than confidence does.

The fee structure is the friendly part. Taskers choose their own rates and take home 100% of their hourly rate and 100% of any tips (s1). The platform is paid by the client instead. Taskrabbit applies a Trust and Support fee to all invoices, calculated as a percentage of the hourly rate and applied to each hour worked, and that fee does not affect what a Tasker is paid (s2). A Canadian review puts a client paid service fee at 15% (s3).

Why should the assembler care about a fee the client pays? Because the client compares your rate plus fees against a flat quote from someone local. A rate that looks fair on your side of the invoice can look steep on theirs. Price with the client's total in mind, especially in your first ten jobs, when reviews are worth more than the last two dollars an hour.

A worked Saturday: two jobs, honest hours

Run a labelled scenario. Your rate is $40 an hour, set below the platform's Canadian average on purpose while reviews are thin (s1). Job one is a wardrobe, two billed hours. Job two is a desk and bookcase, one and a half billed hours. Gross for the day: 3.5 hours at $40, which is $140, and you keep all of it under the fee model above (s1).

Same rate, same tools, same skill. The clustered Saturday pays about four dollars more per hour worked than the scattered one, and the only difference is a map. That is the whole business model of weekend assembly: the platform handles finding the client, and you handle refusing the geography that eats the margin. Registration payback is quick on paper, $25 against a $140 Saturday, but only once bookings actually arrive, which the fee does not guarantee.

The sequence that usually makes sense is platform first for reviews and reps, then direct repeat clients for the households that will need assembly again. Keep the two channels priced the same. Discounting direct work because there is no client fee trains your best clients to expect the lower number.

Who should not do this, and the kill criterion

Skip this one if you dislike working inside other people's homes, if your vehicle cannot carry a basic toolkit and a step stool, or if fiddly instructions and missing hardware make you rush. Assembly punishes rushing in a specific way: the mistake is discovered at step 31 of 34, with the client watching.

Stay inside assembly scope. Wall mounting into unknown structures, electrical work and anything plumbed belong to other trades and other insurance. A weekend gig is not the place to learn that boundary expensively.

The kill criterion, set before you register: after eight Saturdays of real availability, divide everything kept by every hour worked, travel and messages included, using the table method above. If the figure is still below what those Saturdays earn in work you can already get, and clustering has not fixed it, stop paying for the experiment with your weekends. The $25 is gone either way. Your Saturdays do not have to be.
