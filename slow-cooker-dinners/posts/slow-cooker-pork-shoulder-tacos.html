Slow cooker pork shoulder tacos: the shred test, the safety temperature, and holding rules that work

Pork · Published Nov 7, 2026 · 8 min read

Total time: about 7 hours 30 minutes on LOW (20 minutes prep, 7 to 8 hours cooking) or about 4 hours 30 minutes on HIGH · Difficulty: easy · Yield: 6 servings, 12 tacos

The pork pulls apart beautifully at six. The last tacos get eaten at eight, from meat that has been sitting in a warm cooker the whole time. Is that fine, and how would anyone know? Taco night runs on a loose clock, which is exactly where the usual recipe advice runs out: a fork test with no temperature behind it, storage claims that disagree with each other by two full days, and nothing at all about holding. This recipe fixes the sequence. Cut first, temperature and texture as two separate checks, then a holding plan that follows the official rules where the recipes conflict.

First, the cut

Shoulder is the recipe, not a suggestion inside it. Pork shoulder, also sold as pork butt or Boston butt, carries enough fat and connective tissue to turn tender and flavourful over a long cook, and the fat melts as it cooks, which is what leaves the shreds juicy. The leaner cuts that look like tidy substitutes fail at the one job taco meat has.

CutBehaviour in the cookerVerdict for tacos
Boneless pork shoulder or buttFat and connective tissue melt down over hours, meat pulls into shredsThe cut this recipe is built on
Bone-in shoulderThe same cut with the bone left in, cut around it if chunks are wantedWorks, allow the full window and remove the bone at shredding
Pork loinLean, stays sliceable, does not shred like shoulderA different dinner, sliced pork, not taco filling
Pork tenderloinLeanest of the set, does not shred like shoulderSave it for a fast roast, not an all day cook

Ingredients and equipment

For the pork and sauce:

  • 680 to 900 g (1 1/2 to 2 lb) boneless pork shoulder
  • 340 g (3/4 lb) tomatillos, 5 to 6 medium, husked and rinsed
  • 1/2 medium yellow onion
  • 2 jalapeño peppers, stems and seeds removed
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 60 mL (1/4 cup) loosely packed roughly chopped cilantro, plus more for serving
  • 10 mL (2 teaspoons) Worcestershire sauce
  • 2.5 mL (1/2 teaspoon) kosher salt
  • 2.5 mL (1/2 teaspoon) coarse ground black pepper

To serve: 12 small corn tortillas, and toppings chosen freely, pickled red onions, lime wedges, crumbled queso fresco, avocado, and extra cilantro are the ones the checked recipe lists. Equipment is a slow cooker, a blender, a sharp knife, and tongs, plus a food thermometer, which the holding section puts to work. Substitutions, labelled as such: the sauce takes the pork as it comes, so a bone-in shoulder can stand in for boneless at the same weight range, cut around the bone. Loin and tenderloin are not substitutions here, for the reasons in the table above.

Cooking and shredding, step by step

  1. Thaw the pork in the refrigerator, overnight. USDA guidance is to always thaw meat before it goes into a slow cooker, and to keep perishable food refrigerated until preparation time. A shoulder moved from freezer to fridge the night before is ready when the morning is.
  2. Cut the pork into chunks of about 2.5 cm (1 inch), 5 minutes. Trim excess fat if wanted. Even chunks cook evenly, and the cut faces give the sauce more to hold onto than one large roast would.
  3. Blend the sauce, 5 minutes. Cut the tomatillos, onion, and jalapeños into chunks of about 2.5 cm (1 inch), halve the garlic, and blend everything with the cilantro, Worcestershire sauce, salt, and pepper until no large chunks remain. It will look like a slightly chunky salsa verde, because that is what it is.
  4. Load and cook. Put the pork in the cooker, pour the sauce over, and stir to coat. Cover and cook on LOW for 7 to 8 hours, or HIGH for 4 to 5 hours, keeping the lid in place. Searing the pork first is optional: the checked recipe finds the meat flavourful without it, so the pan is a preference, not a requirement.
  5. Run both doneness checks, in this order. First the thermometer: pork pieces and whole cuts are done at 71°C (160°F) on Health Canada's chart, measured through the thickest piece to the middle. That number is the safety floor, and a slow cooked shoulder passes it long before it is ready to eat. Then the texture: a chunk lifted with tongs should pull apart under the pressure of two forks with almost no resistance. A shoulder can be perfectly safe and still refuse to shred, and when it refuses, the answer is another 30 to 60 minutes under the lid, not more force.
  6. Shred and return to the sauce, 5 minutes. Lift the pork out with tongs or a slotted spoon, shred it with two forks on a large plate, and stir it back into the cooking sauce so every shred gets coated. Do not throw the cooking liquid away: it is the holding medium and the reheating medium, and the carnitas method is explicit that the liquid is what keeps the meat from drying out. Skim excess fat from the surface first if the sauce looks greasy.

What success looks like: shreds that hold together in soft strands rather than crumbling or bouncing back, sauce clinging instead of pooling, and a thermometer reading comfortably past 71°C (160°F) in the thickest chunk.

The crisp finish, two documented ways

Soft shreds are ready for tortillas as they are. For crisp edges, pick one finish. The broiler route comes from the base recipe's own reheating notes: spread the meat on a large rimmed baking sheet and broil until lightly browned. The skillet route comes from a carnitas method written for a 4 to 5 lb batch: shredded pork goes into a hot skillet with 1 cup (250 mL) of the cooker juices and cooks, pressed against the pan for a minute or two at a time, until the liquid has almost evaporated and the pork is brown and crispy in spots, about 10 to 15 minutes for that batch size. Crisp in a single layer either way, and work in batches rather than crowding the pan, because crowded meat steams instead of browning.

Holding, storage, and reheating without the guesswork

Three situations cover taco night, and each has a rule. Serving straight away: shred, sauce, and serve, and let the cooker hold the rest. Holding for a group: USDA guidance allows reheated or cooked food to be kept hot in a preheated slow cooker at at least 60°C (140°F), as measured with a food thermometer, and food that has been sitting between 4°C (40°F) and 60°C (140°F), the danger zone where bacteria grow rapidly, for two hours belongs in the bin, not back in the fridge. Making it ahead: cook, cool, and refrigerate, then reheat properly on the day.

Storage is where the checked recipes disagree, so the official chart decides. One carnitas recipe allows five days in the fridge. FoodSafety.gov gives cooked meat 3 to 4 days in the refrigerator at 4°C (40°F) or below, and 2 to 6 months in the freezer at minus 18°C (0°F) or below, and 3 to 4 days is the number to cook by. Store the pork in its juices, in shallow covered containers, refrigerated within two hours after cooking finishes.

Reheating carries the same correction. One checked recipe suggests reheating in the crockpot, and USDA guidance says plainly that reheating leftovers in a slow cooker is not recommended, because it warms too slowly. Reheat on the stove, in a microwave, or in a conventional oven to 74°C (165°F), Health Canada's temperature for leftovers, with a splash of the reserved cooking liquid stirred in. Once the meat is hot, it can go back into a preheated slow cooker to hold at 60°C (140°F) or above for serving.

Quick answers

The thermometer says done but the pork will not shred. What now? Nothing is wrong. The thermometer answers safety, the fork answers tenderness, and shoulder needs time past the safety floor for its connective tissue to give way. Cover it and cook another 30 to 60 minutes.

Can the meat hold on the warm setting all evening? Only if it actually holds at 60°C (140°F) or above, checked with a thermometer. A warm setting that lets the meat drift below that line is the danger zone with a lid on. When in doubt, refrigerate and reheat to 74°C (165°F) instead.

How long do leftovers really keep? Three to four days in the fridge, per the FoodSafety.gov chart, whatever a recipe page promises. Freeze in the juices for longer, and thaw in the refrigerator before reheating.
