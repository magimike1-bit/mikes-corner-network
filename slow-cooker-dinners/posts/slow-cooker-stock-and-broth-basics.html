Slow cooker stock and broth basics: chicken and vegetable stock from scraps

Basics · 8 min read

Stock is the one slow cooker project that pays for the appliance. The ingredients are the parts of dinner most kitchens throw away — a picked-over chicken carcass, onion ends, celery leaves, carrot peels — plus water and time. Yet the published recipes disagree about almost everything: one cooks a carcass in 10 cups of water for 8 to 10 hours on low, another uses 6 cups and cooks for at least 12 hours and as long as 24. Both produce good stock, because stock is forgiving about the clock and strict about only two things: how full the cooker is, and how fast the finished stock gets cold. This guide builds one system that covers chicken stock and vegetable stock, then spends its real attention where the recipe sites spend almost none — the USDA cooling and storage rules that decide whether a batch is an asset or a risk.

Stock, broth, and what the words mean here

Cooks and carton labels use "stock" and "broth" loosely, and this guide follows the kitchen habit rather than a labelling rule: stock here means the savoury liquid built from bones, scraps, or vegetables and water, used as an ingredient in soups, grains, and sauces. Nothing in the method below changes with the name on the container. What changes the result is the input — bones give body, vegetables give sweetness and aroma — and both builds run on the same procedure, the same fill rule, and the same storage rules. Learn the system once and the two versions are a single decision about what went into the scrap bag this week.

The scrap bag: what to save

The system starts in the freezer, not at the store. Keep a large freezer bag or container and add to it as you cook during the week: onion peels and ends, celery leaves and ends, carrot ends and peels, and parsley stems, along with chicken bones or a whole rotisserie or roast chicken carcass with most of the skin removed. That list is the flavour base of the scrap-stock recipes this guide draws on, and it is deliberately short — these are the scraps that give clean flavour over a long cook. Wash vegetables before they are peeled and cut as you normally would, and keep raw chicken bones in their own sealed bag, separate from the vegetable scraps, until cooking day. When the bag is full and the cooker is free, it is stock day. Nothing spoiled, slimy, or past its best goes in: a long cook concentrates whatever it is given, good or bad.

Two rules sit underneath both columns. The first is the USDA's fill rule: a slow cooker should be no less than half full and no more than two-thirds full, so the batch heats through evenly and in reasonable time — that rule, not the clock, is what makes the 8-hour and the 24-hour recipes both workable. The second is layering: vegetables cook more slowly than meat and poultry in a slow cooker, so vegetables go in first, at the bottom, with the bones and liquid on top. Slow cookers cook between 170°F and 280°F, and the direct heat, the steam under the sealed lid, and the long cooking time are what make the process safe — which is why the lid stays in place, removed only to stir or check.

The numbered procedure (chicken stock; vegetable stock skips step 2's bones)

  1. Thaw anything frozen, in the fridge. The USDA rule for slow cookers is to always defrost meat or poultry before it goes in, because frozen meat spends too long climbing through the temperatures where bacteria multiply. A frozen carcass thaws overnight in the refrigerator.
  2. Load the cooker in layers. Vegetable scraps in first, bones on top of them, then the bay leaf and peppercorns. Pick any usable meat off the carcass first and refrigerate it for another meal — stock wants the bones, not the dinner.
  3. Add cold water to cover, about 10 cups in a 6-quart cooker, keeping the total fill between half and two-thirds and leaving 1 to 2 inches of headroom below the rim.
  4. Lid on, low, 8 to 10 hours. Leave the lid in place; every lift dumps the steam the cooker spent an hour building. Longer is not wrong — one published method runs 12 to 24 hours for a darker, more gelatinous result — but 8 to 10 hours is the reliable planning number.
  5. Strain. Lift out the large solids with tongs, then ladle the stock through a fine-mesh strainer into a large bowl or pot. Pressing the solids squeezes out bitterness and cloudiness along with the liquid; a gentle drain keeps the stock clear. Discard the solids — their work is done.
  6. Cool it fast — this is the step that matters. Do not leave the pot sitting on the counter, and do not let stock cool in the cooker. Divide it into shallow containers so it chills quickly and evenly, and get it into the refrigerator within 2 hours of the cook finishing.
  7. Chill overnight, then skim. By morning the fat has set into a pale layer on the surface; lift or scrape it off and discard it. Underneath, a good bone stock has set to a soft gel.
  8. Label, date, and portion. Write the date on every container. Freeze in the portion sizes your cooking actually uses — 1-cup and 2-cup containers cover most soups and grain dishes — leaving headspace for expansion.

What success looks like: chilled stock that wobbles like a soft jelly when the container is tilted (the gel is the visible proof the bones gave up their gelatin), a clean savoury smell with nothing sour underneath it, and a label with a date on every container. Vegetable stock will not gel — there are no bones in it — so its success check is flavour: a spoonful should taste like the vegetables it came from, not like water.

Cooling and storage: the rules that decide safety

Stock is a near-perfect bacterial growth medium — warm, wet, and full of protein — which is why the official guidance is stricter than most recipe pages suggest. Perishable food belongs in the refrigerator within 2 hours of cooking, or within 1 hour when the room is above 90°F. Between 40°F and 140°F — the danger zone — bacteria multiply quickly, and a deep pot of stock cooling slowly on the counter spends hours passing through exactly that range in its centre. Shallow containers are the fix: more surface, faster chilling. Refrigerate at 40°F or below and use homemade stock within 3 to 4 days, the same window the USDA gives leftovers generally; anything not used in that window goes in the freezer, where frozen food stays safe indefinitely though best quality holds for roughly 2 to 6 months. When the stock comes back out, reheat it to 165°F — and reheat it on the stovetop or in the microwave, not in the slow cooker, which heats too slowly for reheating. A reheated batch can then be held hot in a preheated cooker at 140°F or above if dinner needs it waiting.

Is homemade stock actually cheaper? The honest answer is that it depends on one number only you can supply: what a carton costs at your store. The arithmetic below is illustrative — a worked example with a placeholder price, not a price check — because the inputs to the homemade batch are scraps that were already bought for other meals, which makes their marginal cost close to zero, while the carton's price varies by store and week. Run it with your own number: if a 4-cup carton costs $P, store stock costs $P ÷ 4 per cup. At a placeholder $3.00 a carton, that is about $6.75 of stock from ingredients headed for the compost — but if your store's cartons are $1.50, halve it. The yield and the storage life are the durable facts; the dollars are yours to fill in.

FAQ

Can stock cook for 24 hours? One published method does exactly that on low and reports a golden, gelatinous stock. Longer cooking extracts more from the bones; it does not change the cooling and storage rules that follow, and the cooker still needs its half-to- two-thirds fill and its lid left in place.

Can the carcass go in frozen? No. The USDA's slow cooker guidance is to always defrost meat or poultry first. Thaw the carcass in the refrigerator overnight and cook the next day.

Why did the stock not gel? Gel comes from bones, joints, and long extraction. A batch built mostly from meat scraps, or diluted with extra water to stretch the yield, sets weakly or not at all. It is still usable stock — the gel is a texture bonus, not a safety or flavour verdict — but the next batch will set better with the water held to just covering the bones.

How long does frozen stock really keep? Frozen food kept at 0°F stays safe indefinitely; quality is the limit, and the official leftover guidance puts best quality at about 2 to 6 months. Date every container and use the oldest first.

Save the scraps, mind the fill line, and treat the cooling hour as part of the recipe rather than an afterthought. That is the whole of stock-making.
