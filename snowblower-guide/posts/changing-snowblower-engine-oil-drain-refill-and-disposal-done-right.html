Changing Snowblower Engine Oil: Drain, Refill, and Disposal Done Right

Drain it warm, with the spark plug wire disconnected. Refill to the capacity printed for your engine — and treat the used oil as the hazardous waste it is.

← All guides

An oil change on a snowblower engine is a short job with three places to get it wrong: draining cold oil that leaves residue behind, refilling to a capacity copied from a different engine, and pouring the used oil somewhere it must never go. The sourced figures below come from the Briggs & Stratton Snow Series manual and a use-and-care maintenance schedule. They are stated for the engines those documents cover — the method travels to other engines, the numbers do not.

That capacity row is the one to read twice. The 18–20 oz figure circulates online as if it were the snowblower number. It is one engine family's number. An engine with a different sump filled to a borrowed figure is either short or overfull, and neither error announces itself until the machine is back in the snow.

Before you drain: warm oil, level ground, plug wire off

The manual's order of operations is deliberate. The oil is drained warm, because warm oil flows and carries suspended residue out with it; a cold drain leaves more of the old oil — and what it holds — coating the sump. Warm means run briefly to circulate the oil, not drained straight off a machine that has just cleared a driveway at full load.

Before any plug or cap is touched, the spark plug wire comes off. The manual puts the disconnected wire at the head of the procedure: with hands at the drain and the filler, the engine must not be able to start. Set the machine on level ground as well — a tilted engine reads wrong on its level check and drains incompletely.

The procedure

  1. Warm the oil. Run the engine briefly so the oil circulates and warms, then shut it off.
  2. Make it safe. Disconnect the spark plug wire and keep it clear of the plug. Position the machine on level ground with a drain pan that can hold the full sump capacity, with room to spare.
  3. Find your capacity before opening anything. Read the capacity in your own engine's manual and confirm the oil grade it specifies. For the Briggs Snow Series engine covered here, that is 18–20 oz (0.54–0.59 L) and Synthetic 5W-30 for winter operation. Any other engine: its manual's figure, not this one.
  4. Drain. Open the drain — plug or extended drain tube, whichever your engine is fitted with, following your manual's layout for that variant — and let the warm oil drain fully into the pan. Keep the drained oil contained from this moment on; it is hazardous waste, not rubbish.
  5. Close the drain. Refit the plug or close the tube as your manual directs. One honest gap: neither manual behind this guide states a drain-plug torque figure, so none is quoted here. Tighten to your manual's specification where it gives one — a guessed torque is how threads get damaged.
  6. Refill in stages. Add fresh oil in small amounts rather than pouring the full listed capacity in one go, checking the level against your manual's level marks as you approach it. The listed capacity is the sump's size, not a pouring instruction; the level check is the verdict.
  7. Reconnect and run. Refit the filler cap, reconnect the spark plug wire, start the engine, and let it idle briefly. Shut it off, let the oil settle, and check the level once more, topping up only if the check says so.
  8. Inspect the drain. Look at the plug or tube fitting for weeping or drips after the run. A dry fitting and a correct level are the finish line — see the success check below.
What success looks like: the engine oil level sits at the mark your manual specifies after the post-change run and settle, the drain fitting stays dry, no oil is pooled under the machine, and the old oil is sealed in a container labelled for disposal — not sitting in an open pan in the corner of the garage.

The plugless-engine gap, stated honestly

Some small engines have no drain plug and are emptied by tipping or by suction. Neither manual retrieved for this guide covers that method, so no tip-or-suction procedure is written here. Inventing one would put a fuel-and-oil handling sequence into print without a source behind it. If your engine has no plug, its own manual carries the method for that model — follow that document, and the warm-drain, wire-off, and disposal rules above still apply to it.

Disposal: used oil is hazardous waste

The manual's disposal instruction is short and leaves no room for improvisation: used oil is hazardous waste, and it goes through a local authority or a service centre — never into household waste. Pour the drained oil from the pan into a sealable container while it is still contained and accounted for, keep it separate from other fluids, and take it to the collection route your municipality or service centre provides. The oil change is not finished when the engine is full. It is finished when the old oil has somewhere legal to go.

The interval, so the next change is not a rescue

The schedule in the use-and-care manual runs on two clocks. A new engine gets its first oil change after 5 hours of operation — early, because that first oil has done the break-in work. After that, the interval is every 25 hours of operation or once a year, whichever comes first. The annual arm of that rule is the one snowblower owners actually live by: a machine that clears a short driveway may never reach 25 hours in a season, and the calendar still calls the change due.

FAQ

How much oil does a snowblower engine take? It depends on the engine — which is the point. The Briggs & Stratton Snow Series manual gives 18–20 oz (0.54–0.59 L) for the model it covers. Read your own engine's capacity from its manual and confirm it with the level check, rather than borrowing a figure from a different machine.

Why drain the oil warm? Because the manual's procedure calls for it: warm oil drains more completely than cold, carrying more of the old oil out instead of leaving it coating the sump. Warm it briefly — the job does not call for oil at full working temperature.

How often should the oil be changed? On the schedule sourced here: the first change after 5 hours on a new engine, then every 25 hours or once a year, whichever comes first.

Can used snowblower oil go in the household garbage? No. Used oil is hazardous waste. The manual directs it to a local authority or service centre for disposal.
