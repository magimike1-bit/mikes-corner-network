Clearing a Steep Driveway With a Snowblower: Traction, Direction, and Turnarounds

The most repeated slope technique on the internet is the one the Canadian safety authority tells operators not to use. Start with the limits, then decide what is left.

← All guides

The standard advice for a sloped driveway sounds confident: work across the hill in horizontal passes, climb a little with each pass, and stay on the uphill side of the machine. It is also, on the Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS) reading, the wrong place to start. CCOHS instructs operators not to clear snow across the face of slopes at all — and not to operate a snowblower on steep slopes. A technique cannot be the answer if the authority guidance rules out the setting the technique assumes. So this guide works in the opposite order from most slope advice: the limits first, the machine exclusions second, and only then the handling rules for a grade that stays inside those limits.

Mistake one: treating across-the-slope passes as settled technique

The horizontal-pass method comes from how-to guidance: clear perpendicular to the slope, work progressively upward, and keep the operator positioned on the uphill side of the machine. Presented on its own, it reads like a complete system.

Set it beside the CCOHS position and the conflict is direct. CCOHS says do not clear snow across the face of slopes, and do not operate on steep slopes. Those two sentences remove both halves of the popular method for a genuinely steep driveway: the direction is ruled out, and so is the driveway. Where a how-to page and a national safety authority disagree, the authority is the one that governs the decision — the how-to method survives, at most, as a description of what people do on gentler ground, not as a recommendation for a steep grade.

Mistake two: assuming every blower is allowed on the hill

How-to slope content tends to discuss machines as if the advice covers all of them. Manufacturer safety guidance draws a line the how-to pages skip: a single-stage snow thrower is never to be used to clear snow on an uphill slope. That is an exclusion, not a technique note. No pass pattern, pace, or operator position converts a single-stage machine into a slope machine, because the manufacturer has already answered the question.

That leaves two-stage machines as the only class even a candidate for graded work — and candidacy is not permission. The CCOHS steep-slope limit still sits above the machine question: a capable machine on a slope the authority says to stay off is still the wrong setup.

Read the table as a hierarchy, not a menu. The CCOHS rows are limits. The manufacturer row is an exclusion. The how-to row is the only one that describes a technique, and it is the row the limits overrule.

Mistake three: starting, stopping, and rushing on the grade itself

For a slope that passes the authority check — graded, but not steep — CCOHS gives the handling rules that remain: keep movement slow and gradual, and never start or stop the machine on a slope. The second rule quietly dictates the whole layout of the job. A turnaround is a stop-and-reverse moment, so a turnaround does not belong mid-grade. Plan the pattern so direction changes happen where the machine does not have to start or stop on the slope itself — at the flat at the top or the apron at the bottom — and keep the passes between those points slow and gradual, with no hurried corrections.

Short version: the grade is for travelling, not for manoeuvring.

One gap deserves stating plainly. No traction-aid specification — a chain fitment, an added-weight figure, a tire-pressure number for slope work — appears in the authority or manufacturer sources behind this guide, so none is quoted here. A traction product sold for a machine is not the same thing as a traction rating for a slope, and the figure that governs your machine has to come from its own manual.

Mistake four: solving a clog or a crowd the risky way

Slope work concentrates two ordinary hazards. A machine working hard on a grade clogs, and manufacturer guidance is absolute on the response: never clear a clogged chute by hand. The other is people. Manufacturer guidance sets the bystander distance at a minimum of 75 feet from the operating machine — on a residential driveway, that means the audience, the kids, and the neighbour watching from the property line are all part of the setup decision, and on a slope the discharge and the machine both deserve more room, not less.

The decision framework: three gates, in order

Gate 1 — Is the driveway steep? If yes, the CCOHS answer ends the inquiry: do not operate the snowblower on it. Clear that driveway by a method that does not put a running blower on the grade, or hand the job to a service equipped for it. No machine upgrade reopens this gate.

Gate 2 — Is the machine a single-stage? If yes, it does not go on the uphill slope at all, per the manufacturer exclusion. This gate is about the machine, not the operator's confidence.

Gate 3 — For a two-stage machine on a grade that is not steep: slow and gradual movement, no starting or stopping on the slope, turnarounds planned for the flat ground at either end, bystanders at least 75 feet away, and a clogged chute never cleared by hand. If any one of those conditions cannot be met on a given day — packed ice underfoot, an audience that will not move, a pattern that forces mid-grade stops — the grade fails the framework that day, whatever it did last storm.
