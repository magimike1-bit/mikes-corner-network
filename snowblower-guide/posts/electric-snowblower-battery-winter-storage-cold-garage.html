Battery Care

Electric snowblower batteries in a cold garage: two routines, not one

Job summary: this is a carrying job, not a repair. The snowblower itself can winter in an unheated garage. Its lithium ion battery and charger belong indoors, and the rules for a battery working all winter are different from the rules for one being stored for months. Difficulty: easy. Time: five minutes per snowfall, plus one storage setup in spring. Hire a pro line: a swollen or damaged pack is not a DIY item at all; it leaves service and gets recycled.

← All guides

The short answer first, because the mistakes below all come from missing it: keep the battery indoors between snowfalls, let a cold pack warm up before it goes on the charger, and save the partial charge rules for genuine long storage. Most cold garage damage is done by charging, not by cold alone. A pack pulled from a freezing garage and put straight on a charger is the failure this guide exists to prevent.

Three storage mistakes that shorten battery life

Mistake one: the battery winters in the machine, in the shed. Consumer Reports puts the split plainly: even if the tools themselves are stored in a garage or shed, bring the batteries inside, because extreme temperatures shorten the life of the cells and cause them to fail early. House Digest names the same error from the other direction, calling an unheated shed or garage a crucial mistake for a cordless snowblower's battery, since exposure to extreme temperatures reduces battery life and power. The machine is steel and plastic. The pack is the part with chemistry in it.

Mistake two: charging a cold soaked pack. EGO's manuals separate storage from charging for a reason, covered in the numbers section below: a pack can be inside its allowed storage range and still be below the temperature at which it should be charged. The EGO battery manual's own troubleshooting makes the mechanism clear. A pack that is too hot or too cold will not charge correctly, and charging begins once the pack itself returns to a normal temperature. Plugging in and walking away is fine in a warm room. In a cold garage, the pack may sit on a charger doing nothing useful, or worse, be forced through repeated attempts.

Mistake three: storing a damaged pack indoors without looking at it. House Digest's storage routine includes the inspection step most guides skip: while cleaning the pack, check for swelling, changes in colour, or chemical odours, to avoid the risk of fire during storage. Snow Joe's manual adds the handling rules around it: never expose the pack or charger to water or rain, never clean the pack with oil or solvents because the plastic casing can become brittle and crack, and store away from moisture so terminals do not corrode. A pack showing swelling, colour change or odour does not get stored under a workbench. It gets kept away from combustibles and taken to a recycling point, with its terminals covered in electrical tape before disposal, per the Snow Joe manual.

The temperature numbers, labelled honestly

Read the table vertically and the garage question answers itself. A Canadian garage in January can sit well above the EGO storage floor of minus 20 C and still be far below the ideal band, and colder than the 5 C charging floor on the SNT2110. Survival is not the same as healthy, and stored is not the same as ready to charge. Note also that two EGO models publish different optimal charging floors, 0 C and 5 C, which is why the standing rule here is the one every manual agrees on: your own pack's manual outranks any general table, including this one.

In-season routine: ready for the next snowfall

This routine covers the weeks when the machine is working. No partial charges, no storage mode, just temperature discipline:

  1. After clearing, remove the battery pack from the machine. Both the EGO and Snow Joe manuals build removal into normal storage, and a pack left in a cold machine cold soaks with it.
  2. Bring the pack and the charger indoors. The machine stays in the garage; the pack lives at room temperature, dry, out of direct sunlight and away from heating elements and high humidity.
  3. Let a pack that has been out in the cold reach room temperature before charging. The EGO troubleshooting guidance is that charging begins when the pack itself is back in its normal range, so time indoors is part of the charge, not a delay to work around.
  4. Wipe the pack and its terminals with a dry cloth, checking for corrosion as PowerSmart's guidance specifies, and glance at the case for swelling, colour change or odour while it is in your hands.
  5. Charge for use and take the pack off the charger when it is full for the next storm, rather than leaving it cooking on the charger for weeks between snowfalls.

What success looks like: on the next snowfall the pack shows a full charge indoors, seats in the machine with clean terminals, and delivers its normal working time instead of fading early. A pack that repeatedly delivers much less than it did last winter, on the same driveway, is telling you its capacity is going, and no storage routine reverses that.

Long storage routine: months, not weeks

Spring storage is a different job with a different charge target. Set the pack to a partial charge, not full and not empty. House Digest's routine is remove the pack, charge it to about 50 percent, clean it, and store it dry in the acceptable temperature range, noting that a charge between 40 and 60 percent minimizes stress on the battery and helps preserve its lifespan. Snow Joe's manual gives its EcoSharp packs a matching schedule: recharge the pack every 6 months to 50 percent capacity, shown as 2 lights, during off season storage, and as general practice unplug the charger and remove the pack when not in use. Store the pack and charger at room temperature away from moisture, per the same manual, and never in a damp spot where terminals corrode.

Replacement cost is the reason this routine earns five minutes twice a year. House Digest puts snowblower battery life at about three to five years, with replacement packs often costing over $100 and up to $360 depending on capacity and voltage. Those are that article's consumer figures, not a quote for your model, but the order of magnitude is the point: the pack is the most expensive wear part on the machine, and it wears out on a shelf as surely as it does in a snowbank.

The charge level conflict, resolved by brand

One honest conflict remains, and this guide will not pretend it away. Consumer Reports advises roughly 40 percent for any stretch of 30 days or longer, worked out on a four light gauge as running the pack until just after the 50 percent light goes off, which lands between 25 and 50 percent. The same article reports that the brands themselves disagree about upkeep during storage: EGO gear does not require midseason charging, helped by packs that perform their own self maintenance in storage, while Greenworks advises charging to full every two months in storage and then draining back down to 40 percent. Snow Joe lands in a third spot, 50 percent every 6 months.

The decision framework that resolves it without inventing a universal number: first, follow your own brand's manual where it gives a storage charge or a recharge schedule, because the figures above show the brands genuinely differ. Second, where the manual is silent, the shared ground across every source is a partial charge in the 40 to 60 percent band, indoors, dry, and checked on a calendar rather than remembered. Third, never apply the long storage routine to a working winter. A pack in weekly use gets charged for use; storage percentages are for a pack that will sit for months.

, Tom Reilly, Home & DIY Writer
