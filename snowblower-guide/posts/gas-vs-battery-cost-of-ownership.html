Gas vs. Battery Snowblowers: The 5-Year Cost of Ownership

The battery machine costs more up front and less every year after. But the real math depends on your driveway — and on the spare battery nobody puts in the brochure.

← All guides

The gas vs. electric vs. battery buying guide helps you pick the power type for your driveway. This guide answers the money question underneath it: what does each choice actually cost over five winters? The answer is less obvious than the sticker prices suggest — and it changes with driveway size.

Why sticker price lies

Gas machines typically list from around $500 for basic single-stage models to $3,500 for big three-stage units, while battery models run roughly $300 to $2,300. But purchase price is only the first line of the ledger. Gas machines consume fuel and oil every season and need plugs, belts, and carburetor attention; battery machines consume a few dollars of electricity and, eventually, replacement battery packs. The five-year view is the honest one.

The cost buckets

Strip it to the essentials. A gas snowblower's running costs are: fuel and oil each season, periodic spark plugs and belts, occasional carburetor cleaning, and fuel stabilizer for storage. A battery snowblower's running costs are: electricity (a few dollars a year), and one day, replacement batteries. That's the whole structural argument for battery over gas: no fuel system to maintain, no oil to change, no carburetor to clean out in October.

The published numbers we're working with

Every figure below comes from published, dated sources — not from our estimates. RedFlagDeals' September 2026 cost analysis puts a 24-inch-plus gas machine (Ariens/Craftsman class) at $1,200–$1,800 CAD initial, with roughly $50–$80/year in fuel and oil, and a five-year total of about $1,600–$2,300 CAD. Their battery figure (EGO/Toro/Greenworks dual-stage class, batteries and chargers included): $1,800–$2,600 CAD initial, about $5–$10/year in electricity, five-year total roughly $1,850–$2,650 CAD. Treat these as estimates from one analysis, not quotes — equipment pricing moves through the fall, and your retailer will differ.

The money argument for battery isn't that it's dramatically cheaper — it's that you pay more up front for near-zero running costs and no engine maintenance, while gas stays cheaper to buy and costs you a little every season forever.

The spare-battery year: folding the hidden cost in

Here's the line item the brochures skip. A spare set of two large batteries for a premium battery machine has been reported at well north of $800 — real money, roughly a third of the machine's price. Battery packs last several years with proper care (stored indoors, kept charged between storms), but they are consumables: if you need a spare set for a long driveway, or a replacement set in year four or five, add that figure to the battery column. On the heavy scenario above, one $800-class battery purchase in year four erases the battery machine's entire running-cost advantage. Size the battery to the job on day one — buying the bigger pack with the machine is cheaper than buying it later.

The runtime constraint that decides before the money does

For heavy driveways, cost is the wrong deciding factor. Battery machines clear on a charge budget: owners report that if the job can't be finished in roughly ten to thirteen minutes of hard clearing, a single battery set may not get you there — and then you're waiting on a charger mid-storm. Gas refuels in minutes and runs indefinitely. If your driveway is long, your storms are heavy, or you clear for neighbours too, the runtime question answers itself before the spreadsheet matters.

What success looks like

A machine whose five-year cost you understood before you bought it: battery for the short-to-medium driveway where convenience wins, gas for the long driveway where runtime is non-negotiable — and in both cases, a purchase timed for the October window, when you still have leverage.
