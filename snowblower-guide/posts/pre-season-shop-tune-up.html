The October Shop Tune-Up: What to Ask Before the First Snow

The internet will teach you to tune up your own machine. Nobody tells you how to be a good customer of the shop that does it for you.

← All guides

Every tune-up guide on the internet assumes you're doing the work yourself. But plenty of owners — rightly — hand the machine to a small-engine shop instead. The problem: no guide tells you what a proper pre-season tune-up should include, what to ask before you leave the machine, or how to verify the work at pickup. Here's the customer's version.

Why October is the appointment month

Small-engine shops run on the same calendar as the weather. Through October they have open benches; once the first real forecast lands, every machine in the neighbourhood shows up at once. Booking in October isn't just convenient — it's the difference between a machine that's ready for the first storm and one that's sitting in a shop queue while you shovel.

Battery-electric machines can largely skip this article: with no engine, there's no oil, plug, carburetor, or fuel to service. The rest of this guide is for gas machines.

What a real pre-season tune-up should include

Walk in knowing the task list, so you can tell a thorough shop from a quick one. A proper pre-season tune-up for a gas machine covers:

  • Fuel system: drain stale fuel, flush the system, refill with fresh gasoline; clean the tank and carburetor bowl.
  • Oil: change the engine oil (your manual names the grade; 5W-30 is the typical winter recommendation).
  • Spark plug: inspect and replace if the tip shows dark or white deposits.
  • Belts: inspect for cracking, stretching, or hardening; adjust tension or replace. A belt that fails in February strands you mid-storm.
  • Shear pins: check condition and replace worn ones; confirm spares are available.
  • Auger and drive: check the drive system, auger movement, and paddles or scraper bar for wear.
  • Controls: lubricate cables and grease fittings; verify the chute rotates smoothly and the safety interlocks (dead-man switch, guards) work.
  • Engine health: an RPM check and, at a good shop, a compression test — weak compression is the early warning of internal engine trouble.

If a shop's "tune-up" is oil and a plug only, you're paying for half the job. The belts, fuel system, and safety checks are the parts that actually prevent mid-winter failures.

The questions to ask before you hand it over

  1. "What's included in the tune-up, specifically?" Get the task list. Compare it against the one above.
  2. "What's the turnaround right now?" In October the answer should be short. If the shop is already quoting a long wait, call the next shop.
  3. "Do you warranty the work, and for how long?" A reputable shop stands behind a tune-up for the season. One service listing advertises warranted work explicitly — treat that as the standard, not a bonus.
  4. "Will you call before replacing parts beyond the tune-up?" Belts and plugs are consumables; a carburetor rebuild or new tires are a conversation. Agree on the authorization rule up front.
  5. "Do you pick up and deliver?" Some shops offer it in the pre-season — the Glens Falls listing advertises free local pickup and delivery. A machine you can't transport easily is a solved problem, not a reason to skip the service.

What it costs: one honest data point

Published tune-up pricing is scarce, so here's one clearly labelled example rather than a made-up range: a Glens Falls, New York service listing advertises a preseason tune-up — oil, spark plug, grease fittings, tank and carb bowl cleaning, belt and shear pin checks, carb/drive/chute/skid adjustments — at $75 USD with free local pickup and delivery. That's one US listing, not a market survey. Your local price will differ; the value of the example is knowing what $75 bought there, so you can compare task lists rather than just totals.

The honest middle path: do the easy annual tasks yourself (fresh fuel, oil, plug) and let the shop handle the full tune-up every second or third year, or any year the machine ran rough. Read the mid-winter maintenance guide for what to watch between services.

Picking it up: verify before you pay

Don't just load it in the truck. Ask the shop to start it in front of you — cold, not warmed up — and run the auger and drive briefly. Confirm which parts were replaced and keep the old ones if you want proof. Ask what they noticed: a good mechanic will tell you the belt has one season left or the scraper bar is getting thin, which is next year's shopping list, not this year's surprise. Pay, get the receipt with the task list on it, and file it with the manual.

What success looks like

A machine that starts on the first or second pull in November, a receipt listing every task performed, a shop that knows your machine by sight — and a phone number saved for February, when you'll be glad you found them in October.
