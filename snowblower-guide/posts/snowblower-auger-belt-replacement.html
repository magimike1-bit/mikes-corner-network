Replacing a snowblower auger belt (and drive belt): the single-stage and two-stage procedures

One repair, two very different jobs. Prove the belt is guilty, set up safely, follow the path for your machine type — then verify the augers stop within five seconds, the way the manuals demand.

← All guides

The engine is running fine. The machine sounds healthy. But the auger will not turn, or it turns until it meets snow and gives up. Repair guidance is blunt about the most likely culprit in that picture: a damaged or slipping drive belt. Belts are the power path between the engine and everything that moves snow — they stretch, break, and slip off their pulleys — and when one fails, the machine loses its ability to clear even though the motor sounds fine. This guide covers the full job: confirming the belt is the problem, the safety setup, the replacement procedure for both machine types, and the verification step that decides whether the repair is actually finished.

First, prove it is the belt

Ten minutes of diagnosis beats an hour of unnecessary disassembly. Work through these checks with the engine off:

  1. Confirm the symptom pattern. Engine runs, auger does not spin or spins weakly under load — that is the belt-failure signature. On a two-stage machine, note which function died: auger and impeller dead but wheels driving points at the auger belt; wheels dead but auger throwing points at the drive belt. The two belts have separate jobs — on dual-stage machines one or more belts drive the auger and impeller, and another belt drives the self-propelled wheels.
  2. Check how your machine engages. On single-stage models, squeezing the bail arm moves an idler pulley that tightens the belt around the drive pulley — a stretched belt can slip exactly because that tightening runs out of travel. If the auger engages weakly or late, belt stretch is high on the suspect list.
  3. Look at the belt before ordering anything. Remove the belt cover (the procedure below starts there anyway) and inspect: visible damage, a belt that has stretched, or a belt sitting loose on its pulleys all condemn it. Manufacturer troubleshooting tables end at the same place — an auger belt that is loose or damaged gets replaced, not adjusted back to health.
  4. Rule out the cheaper suspects. A broken shear pin stops one auger side while the belt still drives the shaft — a different symptom with a different fix, covered in the spares guide on this site. Packed snow and ice in the housing stop everything until cleared, with the engine off and the spark plug wire disconnected, using the clean-out tool — never a hand.

Tools and parts to have ready

  • The correct belt — by full model number. Belts come in cogged and non-cogged V-belt and flat types, and the right one is identified by the machine's complete model number, not by measuring the old belt (a stretched belt measures long). Parts guidance from RepairClinic and the manufacturers is consistent on this: enter the full model number, then pick the belt for that machine.
  • Both belts, on a two-stage machine. Manuals instruct owners to always change both belts at the same time — changing only one can lead to performance issues, because the old belt and the new belt stretch differently and share the work unevenly.
  • Basic hand tools. A Phillips screwdriver and a socket set cover the typical MTD-style job; some models add a belt guide or housing bolts that need the sockets. Add work gloves and eye protection.
  • A thin plastic sheet. For the tipping step: it seals the fuel filler so gasoline cannot spill when the machine goes forward onto its housing.
  • The operator manual for your model. Belt routing differs by model. The procedures below are the common patterns; your manual's routing diagram for your machine always wins.

Safety setup: the five minutes that matter

  1. Park on a flat, level surface and let the engine cool completely before any disassembly.
  2. Shut the engine off and disconnect the spark plug wire and boot so the engine cannot start accidentally while your hands are near the pulleys.
  3. On machines with a keyed ignition, remove the start key as well. On two-stage machines, work with little or no fuel in the tank.
  4. If the job involves tipping the machine forward: remove the gas cap, cover the filler hole with the plastic sheet, and refit the cap tight over it — the seal that keeps fuel in the tank while the machine is on its nose.
  5. Clear any packed snow or ice from the auger housing with the clean-out tool before you start. Never reach into the housing with a hand, engine off or not.

Procedure A: single-stage auger belt

The single-stage job is the smaller one — the belt usually lives behind a side access cover and can be swapped without splitting the machine:

  1. Complete the safety setup above.
  2. Unthread the screws or bolts securing the side access cover and pull the cover off to expose the pulleys and belt.
  3. Push the idler arm down to release belt tension, then free the old belt from the brake and the auger pulley, and unhook it from the drive pulley.
  4. If your model gives no room to slip the belt off, the auger pulley comes off instead: secure the auger so it cannot rotate, unthread the nut holding the auger pulley, and remove the pulley and belt together — keeping every washer in order for reassembly.
  5. Seat the new belt on the auger pulley first (on the pulley itself if it was removed), then loop it onto the drive pulley and under the idler pulley, making sure the belt runs under the brake.
  6. If the pulley was removed, refit it with its washers in their original order, thread the nut, and tighten it securely.
  7. Refit the access cover and its screws or bolts. Reconnect the spark plug wire and boot.
  8. Run the verification below before calling the job done.

Procedure B: two-stage auger and drive belts

The two-stage job is bigger: the belts sit in front of the engine under an access cover, and reaching the auger belt fully can mean opening the machine up. The steps vary more by model here — follow the pattern, and your manual for the specifics:

  1. Complete the safety setup above, including low fuel and the sealed filler if you will tip the machine.
  2. Unthread the bolts securing the belt cover in front of the engine and set the cover aside. Remove the belt guide as well, if your model has one.
  3. Try the simple path first: on some models the auger belt can now be detached from the drive and idler pulleys as it sits.
  4. If it cannot, tip the snowblower forward so it rests on its auger housing, unthread the mounting bolts, and release the frame cover underneath. Depress the drive lever and secure it in place, which gives the slack needed to detach the belt from the auger drive pulley; lift the drive plate if your model requires it to free the belt fully.
  5. On models that still will not release the belt, separate the blower housing from the frame: unthread the housing mounting bolts, detach the deflector cable from the chute if it crosses the joint, and pivot the frame away from the housing, resting the rear of the machine on its handlebars.
  6. Remove the old belt or belts completely. Remember the rule: on a two-belt machine, both belts get replaced in this job, not just the one that failed.
  7. Align the new belt on the auger pulley first. If the housing was separated, rejoin the two halves of the machine and secure the housing to the frame with its bolts and washers; reattach the deflector cable.
  8. Feed the belt through and align it on the drive pulley and idler pulley. Release the drive lever, refit any retainers, and realign the frame cover with its mounting bolts.
  9. Return the machine upright. Refit the belt guide if it was removed, then the belt cover. Remove the plastic from the filler neck if you sealed it, and reconnect the spark plug wire.
  10. Check belt tension and adjust if your model provides an adjustment — then run the verification below.

The verification: augers must stop within five seconds

Reassembly is not the finish line — the manuals set a measurable safety specification for this repair, and it is the real success check. Belts are adjusted so that the augers stop turning within 5 seconds of releasing the auger/impeller control lever, and after any belt change or adjustment that stop time must be re-verified: a belt routed or tensioned wrong can leave the auger creeping or spinning on after the control is released, which is a genuine hazard, not a quirk. Start the machine in clear space, engage the auger control, release it, and count. Then engage the drive and confirm the wheels pull evenly.

What success looks like: the auger starts turning the moment the control is engaged, throws snow across the full housing width without slipping or squealing under load, the wheels self-propel evenly — and when the control is released, the augers come to a complete stop within five seconds, every time.

Decision framework: adjust, replace, or hand it to a shop?

Replace it yourself when the belt shows damage, stretch, or slipping under load, and the access path for your machine type looks like the procedures above — this is one of the most owner-friendly repairs on the machine. Stop and reassess if the new belt slips immediately (wrong belt, routing error, or a worn idler), if the pulleys are visibly damaged or wobble, or if the failure came with gearbox noise — belts are cheap, gearboxes are not, and a belt that keeps breaking is a symptom, not the disease. Book the shop if the job requires splitting the housing on a machine you cannot safely support tipped, or if two new belts in a row fail quickly: something upstream is eating them, and diagnosis is the purchase, not another belt.

FAQ

Should both belts really be replaced together on a two-stage machine? Yes — the manuals say so directly. Belts stretch with age, so a new belt paired with an old one shares the work unevenly and can cause performance issues; since the machine is already open, the second belt is the cheap part of the job.

Can a belt that looks the same be used if it is not the model-specific one? Fit is by the machine's full model number, and belt types differ — cogged, non-cogged V, and flat. A near-fit belt slips, wears fast, or will not seat; match the part to the model number, not to the old belt's stretched length.

The auger turns by hand with the cover off. Does that clear the belt? No. A stretched or glazed belt can move an unloaded auger and still slip the moment snow loads it. The check is under load, plus the five-second stop test after any work.

How often do belts need replacing? There is no fixed interval in the manuals — belts are inspected and replaced on condition: damage, stretch, or slipping under load. That is why a pre-season inspection, and a spare belt on the shelf, are the standing advice on this site.
