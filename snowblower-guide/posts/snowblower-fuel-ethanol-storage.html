Snowblower fuel, ethanol and storage: why old gas kills November starts

Care · 8 min read

The most common reason a snowblower will not start in November has nothing to do with the engine and everything to do with the fuel that sat in it since March. Ethanol-blended gasoline — the E10 pump fuel sold across Canada — absorbs moisture from the air, separates from the gasoline over time, and turns to varnish that clogs carburetor jets. It also degrades rubber fuel lines and plastic components. The carburetor then needs professional cleaning, which is a $150–$300 shop visit, or a replacement. Almost all of this is preventable with a thirty-minute fuel routine. Honda's own snowblower fuel guidance reduces it to five principles, and they are worth following regardless of brand.

Why ethanol is the problem

Ethanol is blended into gasoline at roughly 10% (E10) for the standard pump grades, and it carries 28% less energy density than gasoline. More importantly for storage, it is hygroscopic: it absorbs water vapour from the air, and once it has absorbed enough, the ethanol-water mixture separates and settles to the bottom of the tank and the carburetor bowl — exactly where the fuel pickup sits. The separated fuel provides poor lubrication, corrodes components, and leaves deposits on jets, intake tracts and valves. Repair guides note that the lower the fuel level in the tank, the more air — and therefore moisture — the fuel is exposed to, which is why the “leave it half full” habit is the worst of both worlds. Small-engine mechanics report that fuel can begin to deteriorate noticeably within weeks, and untreated gasoline more than about 30 days old should not be trusted in a small engine at all.

The five principles (Honda's guidance, universal in practice)

  1. Do not use fuel with more than 10% ethanol. Higher-ethanol blends are more corrosive and attract more water; they can cause starting and running problems and, in some cases, damage to the fuel system.
  2. Store gasoline correctly. Clean, sealed, plastic, fuel-rated containers; close the vent when not in use; keep containers out of direct sunlight. If the fuel will sit more than three months before use, add a fuel stabilizer at fill time.
  3. After each use, turn the fuel valve OFF and run the engine until it stops. This empties the carburetor bowl — the small volume of fuel that deteriorates fastest, sitting in a warm, air-exposed chamber. Then fill the tank immediately after use to minimize the air (and moisture) in it. Experienced mechanics recommend draining or running dry over relying on stabilizer alone.
  4. For sporadic winter use, do not leave untreated fuel sitting. Untreated gasoline left in the system between infrequent storms deteriorates quickly; if the machine sits weeks between storms, stabilize the fuel or run it dry.
  5. At season's end, choose a full routine, not a half one. Either drain the entire fuel system completely, or fill with stabilized fuel (fresh stabilizer, run through the system so the treated fuel reaches the carburetor). The half-measures — a splash of old stabilizer, a drained tank but a full bowl — are the ones that fail.

The mechanic consensus in repair-community discussions leans practical: for a machine you use weekly, stabilizer in fresh fuel plus the run-dry habit is plenty. For the long off-season, draining or running the system dry is the approach mechanics themselves use on their own equipment. The expensive option — canned fuel — earns its price for machines that run a handful of times a year, where a $12 can is cheaper than one carburetor cleaning.

The pre-season fuel reset (procedure)

  1. Drain what is there. If the tank holds last season's fuel, drain it into a proper container for disposal — do not run it through the engine as a test.
  2. Inspect the fuel system. Check fuel lines for cracks or brittleness and the tank for rust or debris while it is empty.
  3. Refill with fresh fuel. Fresh E10 with stabilizer added at fill time, or ethanol-free or canned fuel per your chosen approach.
  4. Run it through. Start the machine and let it run long enough for the fresh, stabilized fuel to fill the carburetor bowl completely.
  5. Test under load. Engage the auger and drive in October's dry weather. A machine that starts and runs cleanly on fresh fuel in October is a machine that will start in January.
What success looks like: the machine starts within a few pulls on the first cold day, idles clean, and the fuel system inspection shows flexible lines, a clean tank, and fresh fuel — because the season's fuel work was done in October, not diagnosed in January.

What about battery-electric machines?

None of this applies to a battery snowblower's nonexistent fuel system — which is one of the design's genuine advantages, and one reason used battery machines age differently from used gas machines. But the storage principle survives the translation: energy storage degrades when it is neglected. The electric equivalent of the fuel routine is charging discipline — batteries stored neither stone-dead nor in a freezing shed, chargers verified working before the season, contacts clean. A gas owner drains a tank in October; a battery owner charges and checks a pack. Both are doing the same job: making the machine's energy supply a solved problem before the first storm turns it into an urgent one. If you are cross-shopping the two types this fall, count the fuel routine honestly on the gas side of the ledger — it is thirty minutes twice a year, not a burden, but it is not zero, and pretending either way distorts the comparison.

One last habit ties the system together: write the fuel date on the container with a marker every time you fill it. Fuel age is the variable every rule in this guide depends on — the 30-day untreated limit, the three-month stabilizer trigger — and it is the one variable nobody can reconstruct from memory in February. A date on the can turns every fuel decision after it into arithmetic instead of guesswork.

FAQ

Can I just add stabilizer to old gas? Stabilizer preserves fuel that is still fresh; it does not reverse deterioration that has already happened. Old fuel that smells like varnish needs draining, not stabilizing.

Should I fill the tank or leave it empty for storage? Either extreme is defensible — full (with stabilized fuel, minimal air) or fully drained (no fuel to deteriorate) — and both beat the middle. The run-dry method with the fuel valve closed is the most reliable single habit between storms too.

Is premium gas better for my snowblower? Higher octane does not fix the ethanol problem — most premium in Canada is still E10 — and higher octane provides no benefit to an engine designed for regular. What helps is ethanol-free fuel or canned fuel, not higher octane.
