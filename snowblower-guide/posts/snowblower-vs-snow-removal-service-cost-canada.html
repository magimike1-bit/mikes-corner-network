Snowblower vs. Snow Removal Service in Canada: The Real Cost Comparison

A service quotes you one number per winter. A snowblower quotes you a sticker price and then bills you quietly for a decade. Here is both options run through the same ledger, with Canadian prices checked 24 October 2026.

← All guides

Every fall, Canadian homeowners face the same fork: buy a machine once and do the work yourself, or sign a seasonal contract and never touch a chute again. Most comparisons online only price one side of that fork. Provider sites list what a contract costs in your city and stop there. Buying guides list what a machine costs and stop there. This guide does the unglamorous thing neither side does: it annualises ownership, puts it next to sourced seasonal contract ranges, and shows where the two lines cross.

What a seasonal service actually costs in Canada

For the 2026–27 season, a cross-Canada pricing guide compiled in September 2026 from published Canadian price guides and provider rate cards puts residential seasonal contracts for a standard driveway plus walkway in these bands: Toronto and the GTA at $600–$1,700, Ottawa and mid-size Ontario cities at $400–$900, Calgary at $350–$1,200, Edmonton and the Prairies at $400–$1,000, Montreal and Quebec at $700–$1,500, Atlantic Canada at $500–$1,100, and smaller towns and rural areas at $400–$800. Per-visit clearing, for comparison, runs about $40–$100, and monthly plans run roughly $120–$400 per month depending on the city.

Individual providers can sit above those multi-source bands, which is worth knowing before you treat any single quote as “the market.” One national provider lists a standard seasonal residential plan at $580–$960 for a typical Canadian winter, while its own city examples run higher — Toronto at $1,400–$2,200 and Ottawa at $1,300–$1,900. HomeStars, meanwhile, lists Toronto seasonal contracts broadly at $300 to $1,800 or more per winter. The honest takeaway is not a single number; it is that your city, your driveway size, and the service level move the price by hundreds of dollars, so get two or three local quotes and compare them against the band for your region, not against a national average.

One concrete anchor helps. An Edmonton provider’s currently listed example package charges $195 per month over a five-month core billing period running November 15 through April 15 — $975 for the season, before GST — with recurring residential service in that market generally quoted at roughly $180 to $350 or more per month, and one-time clearing at $70 to $90 or more per visit. That $975 figure is a useful mid-market reference point for the math below: it is a real, published package price, not a guess.

What owning a snowblower actually costs per winter

Ownership has four cash lines, and only one of them is the sticker. First, the machine itself: electric or battery models run roughly $600 to $1,500 or more, and heavy-duty two-stage machines run roughly $1,500 to $3,000 or more. Second, consumables and upkeep: fuel and oil for a gas machine, electricity for a battery machine, plus belts, shear pins, and the occasional repair. Third, ice control — the bags of ice melt or traction material a service often bundles in and an owner buys separately. Fourth, the costs that never appear on a receipt: storage space for ten months of the year, and your own time after every snowfall.

A published Edmonton comparison works the annualised version of this ledger: a $1,000 snowblower lasting five winters works out to $200 per year in equipment cost, plus roughly $150 for ice-control material and about $50 for maintenance, electricity, or replacement parts — an estimated annual cash cost of about $400, excluding the time you spend using it. That sits comfortably alongside this site’s separate five-year ownership analysis for gas versus battery machines, which put published five-year totals at roughly $1,600–$2,300 CAD for gas and $1,850–$2,650 CAD for battery — in other words, roughly $320 to $530 per winter once the purchase price is spread out. Whichever way the figures are cut, the same picture emerges: owning costs a few hundred dollars per winter in cash, every winter, for as long as you own the machine.

Your time is the swing line

The one line that can flip the result is your own labour. The published Edmonton illustration assumes 48 clearing sessions in a winter, cut from 30 minutes by hand to about 15 minutes with a snowblower — 12 hours of your winter. Valued at $25 an hour, that time is worth $300, lifting the economic cost of owning to roughly $700 against $975 for the example service package. Value your time at $50 an hour and the same arithmetic puts owning near $1,000 in economic terms — at or above the service price, before you count a single sore back or 6 a.m. alarm.

There is no correct hourly figure, and anyone who tells you there is one is selling something. The useful question is narrower: what would those 12-plus hours, at the worst moments of the year, actually be worth to you? If you enjoy being outside, are home for most storms, and clear a short driveway quickly, your honest figure may be close to zero and ownership wins easily. If you leave for work early, travel in winter, clear a long or corner-lot driveway, or are arranging this for a parent whose safety is the real concern, your honest figure is high — and the service stops looking expensive and starts looking like the cheaper way to buy certainty.

The contract fine print that changes the price

Two contracts at the same headline price can be very different products. Before signing, check five things, in writing. First, the trigger: how much snow must fall before a visit happens, and is the driveway cleared by a set morning hour? Second, the cap: some contracts quietly limit the season — capped contracts in Ottawa, for example, commonly stop at 23 events or 250 cm of snowfall, with extra visits billed at $40–$80 each, which can add real money in a heavy winter. Third, salting: de-icing is frequently extra, at roughly $30–$100 per application in published pricing, and a winter of applications can rival the contract discount you negotiated. Fourth, the scope: driveway only, or walkway, steps, and the windrow the city plow leaves at the end of the driveway as well? Fifth, the exit: what happens if the company misses visits or folds mid-season — a risk that costs an owner nothing, because the machine is already in the garage.

Ownership has its own fine print, and fairness demands listing it. The machine must be maintained before the season, fuelled or charged during it, and repaired when it fails — and machines tend to fail during the biggest storm of the year, which is precisely when a service’s backup equipment and route capacity earn their fee. Ownership also assumes you are home, healthy, and willing. A service keeps working while you are on holiday, recovering from an injury, or simply done with pre-dawn clearing.

Decision framework: which side you land on

Work through these five checks in order; the first one that points clearly usually settles it. One: driveway and snowfall — a short driveway in a moderate-snow city favours owning; a long, sloped, or corner-lot driveway in a high-snow city favours help. Two: availability — if you are reliably home after storms, owning works; if you travel, shift-work, or leave early, a service protects you from the storms you miss. Three: body and safety — if clearing heavy, wet snow is a genuine physical strain, price that honestly; no spreadsheet outranks it. Four: run your own numbers — take your city’s contract band, two local quotes, the machine you would actually buy, and the annualised method above; if the five-year cash gap is small in your case, let convenience decide, and if it is large, let the gap decide. Five: consider the hybrid year — many households own a modest machine and still hire a service for a single heavy season, a renovation winter, or a year with a new baby or a bad knee. The contract is annual; the machine is not offended.

Questions people commonly ask

How many winters until a snowblower pays for itself versus a service? Using the illustrative figures above, a mid-priced machine’s purchase is typically recovered within the first one to two winters when the alternative is a mid-band seasonal contract — but only if you count the annualised cash cost rather than the up-front outlay, and only if your time is valued low. Against a low-band contract in a smaller city, the payback stretches out.

Is per-visit hiring ever the better buy? In low-snow winters or mild regions, yes: at roughly $40–$100 per visit, paying only for the storms that actually happen can undercut a full seasonal contract. The risk is availability — per-visit customers are usually served after contract routes on the busiest mornings.

What does success look like? A winter where the driveway is clear when you need it, at a total cost — cash plus time, counted honestly — that you chose on purpose in October instead of discovering in February.
