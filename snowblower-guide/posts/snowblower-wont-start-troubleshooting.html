Snowblower won't start: a symptom-first troubleshooting diagnosis

Care · 9 min read

A snowblower that will not start is not one problem — it is at least four different problems wearing the same coat, and the fix for each is different. Pulling the carburetor apart because the ignition key was not fully inserted wastes an afternoon; replacing a spark plug when the plug was telling you the fuel system was at fault wastes money. Briggs & Stratton's benchmark is a useful starting point: a healthy snow blower should start within three pulls of the starter rope. Past three pulls, stop pulling and start diagnosing. Work in the order below — settings first, evidence second, parts last — and let each test result choose the next step.

Step zero: audit the cold-start sequence before touching a tool

A large share of “won't start” calls are a correct machine being started incorrectly, and the sequence is model-specific enough that Toro's published version is worth using as the template. On a two-stage Toro: open the fuel shut-off valve; insert the ignition key before pressing the primer bulb; press the primer a couple of times when it is 15 degrees or warmer, and four times when it is below 15 degrees; set the choke on and the throttle to fast; then use the recoil or electric starter, and move the choke toward run as the engine warms. The choke details matter too — fully closed for a cold engine, left one-half to three-quarters open when it is 30 degrees or above, and left alone entirely when restarting less than five minutes after shutdown.

Check each control against that pattern, in this order: fuel in the tank (you should see it slosh), fuel valve open, key fully inserted, throttle at fast, choke in the cold-start position, primer pressed the right number of times for the temperature. Any one of them wrong mimics a dead engine perfectly. One special case belongs here too: if you smell strong fuel, the engine is probably flooded from over-priming. Toro's recovery is to close the fuel shutoff if the machine has one, turn the choke off, pull the recoil three times to clear the excess fuel, then wait 15 minutes before trying again — with the fuel shutoff reopened for the restart.

The wet-versus-dry plug reading is the hinge of the whole diagnosis, and it comes straight from Briggs & Stratton's troubleshooting sequence: a wet plug means fuel is going through the fuel system and the problem may be ignition; a dry plug means there may be a fuel-system problem. One $0 observation splits the machine in half. Use it before buying anything.

The diagnosis procedure

  1. Make it safe. Work outdoors or in a ventilated space, engine cool, auger and drive disengaged. Before any plug work, the machine must not be able to start accidentally — follow your operator manual's instructions for disabling the ignition while servicing.
  2. Run the step-zero audit. Fuel visible and sloshing in the tank, valve open, key in, throttle fast, choke set for a cold start, primer pressed for the temperature. Try three pulls. A healthy engine should have started by now; if it did, the diagnosis is over and the lesson is the sequence.
  3. Judge the fuel itself. Fuel that sat in the machine over the summer is the single most common culprit in repair guidance: stale fuel gums and varnishes the carburetor. If the fuel is old, drain it and refill with fresh fuel rather than trying to start the engine on it as an experiment.
  4. Remove and read the spark plug. Note wet or dry before cleaning anything — that reading is evidence, and wiping the plug first destroys it. Then inspect: cracks or heavy carbon buildup cannot be repaired, so replace the plug; fuel on the plug usually means a flooded engine, so dry it before reinstalling; check the electrode gap against the spec in your manual with a feeler gauge, which should slide through with slight drag.
  5. Test for spark. With the plug question answered, check the ignition system with an inline spark tester, as Briggs & Stratton's sequence prescribes. Strong, regular spark plus a wet plug sends you back to flooding and fuel quality. No spark with a known-good plug means the ignition system itself — coil, wiring, or key switch — and that is near the end of the driveway diagnosis.
  6. Run the carburetor test. Spray a short burst of carburetor cleaner into the air intake and try to start. If the engine fires briefly and then dies, fuel is not reaching the engine through its own system — the cleaner briefly substituted for it. That result confirms the fuel-delivery branch: carburetor, fuel line, or filter.
  7. Inspect the air filter. A clogged or fuel-soaked air filter restricts the air the engine needs; replace it if it is dirty, damaged, or wet with fuel. Do this before deeper carburetor work — it is cheaper and faster than any of it.
  8. Check the fuel path. Inspect the fuel line for blockages, cracks, or brittleness and replace it if damaged or clogged. Confirm fuel actually flows from the tank toward the carburetor before condemning the carburetor itself.
  9. Clean the carburetor, in stages. Remove the air filter to reach it, spray cleaner into the carburetor opening and around the choke plate, and if the carburetor has a removable float bowl, take it off and clean out deposits. Reassemble, add fresh fuel, and try again. Heavy varnish buildup may require a full rebuild — see the stop rules before attempting one for the first time mid-storm.
  10. Respect the electric starter's limits. If the machine has electric start, Toro's manual rule is strict: run the starter no more than 10 times at intervals of 5 seconds on and 5 seconds off, and if the engine still has not started, let the starter cool at least 40 minutes before trying again. Cranking past those limits converts a no-start into a no-start plus a burnt starter.
  11. Retest from the top. After any fix, repeat the correct cold-start sequence and count the pulls. Improvement is information: an engine that now fires but dies has moved branches in the table above, and the new symptom is the new diagnosis.
What success looks like: the engine starts within three pulls using the normal cold-start sequence — no cleaner spray, no tricks — idles without surging, accepts the choke moving to the run position as it warms, and then holds speed with the auger engaged in actual snow. A start that only happens with help is a diagnosis half-finished, not a repair.

Stop rules: when the shop takes over

Knowing where driveway diagnosis ends is part of the procedure, not a failure of it. Hand the machine to a small-engine shop when any of these are true: there is no spark with a new, correctly gapped plug (ignition components and wiring are next, and they are model-specific); the engine will not pull over or cranks with abnormal resistance (compression and internal condition are part of Briggs & Stratton's diagnostic list for a reason — they are assessed with gauges, not guesses); the carburetor has been cleaned in stages and the engine still only runs on cleaner spray (a rebuild or replacement decision needs parts support); or fuel is leaking anywhere it should not be. Briggs & Stratton's own sequence ends the same way: after fuel, plug, spark test, and a replacement plug, a machine that still does not start goes in for service.

Battery and corded machines: the short version

None of the fuel-and-spark logic applies to an electric snowblower, and its no-start list is blessedly shorter. For corded machines, the causes in repair guidance are a faulty power cord or connection: inspect the cord for damage or fraying and replace it if damaged, and confirm the outlet and any extension cord actually deliver power. For battery machines, suspect a dead or weak battery first — charge it fully, and if it will not hold a charge, that is the diagnosis. The shared lesson across all three power types is the one this whole guide runs on: the machine is telling you which system failed, in the order its symptoms appeared. Read them before replacing anything.

FAQ

How many pulls before something is actually wrong? Three, by Briggs & Stratton's standard. Beyond that, more pulling adds flooding risk without adding information — switch to the procedure.

The engine starts on carburetor cleaner but dies immediately. What does that prove? It proves spark and compression are good enough to run, and that fuel is not reaching the engine through the tank, line, and carburetor. Work the fuel path; do not keep feeding it cleaner.

Can a spark plug look fine and still be the problem? Yes — which is why the procedure reads the plug (wet or dry), checks its gap against the manual, and uses a spark tester before concluding anything. A plug that is cracked or heavily carbon-fouled is replaced on sight; anything subtler is settled by the tester, not by appearance.

Why did it run fine in October and fail in January? Usually because October's test ran on fuel and deposits that were already marginal, and two more months of sitting finished the job. The prevention side of that story — fuel discipline and end-of-season storage — is covered elsewhere in this guide's maintenance section.
