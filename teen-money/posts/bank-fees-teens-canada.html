Bank fees a teen account can still charge in Canada: the $10 NSF cap and the fees around it

Banking · 6 min read

$10 is the most a federally regulated bank in Canada can now charge when a payment bounces for non-sufficient funds, down from the $45 to $48 that was typical before March 2026. That single number is the good news, and it sits in the first paragraph because it changes the worst fee a teen account can land. It does not make a teen account free. A $0 monthly fee removes exactly one charge, the monthly one, and leaves the mistake fees and the habit fees standing.

The trap first: what the $10 cap covers, and what it leaves alone

An NSF fee is charged when a transaction is declined because the account has no overdraft protection and not enough money to cover a cheque, a pre-authorized debit, a bill payment, or another withdrawal. Since March 12, 2026, federal rules cap that fee at $10 on a personal deposit account, bar a second NSF fee within a period of 2 business days on the same account, and bar the fee entirely when the amount the account is short is less than $10.

Read the limits as carefully as the cap. The rules govern the bank fee. They say nothing about the company that was supposed to be paid.

That company can charge its own dishonoured payment fee when a payment comes back, typically in the range of $20 to $40 per declined transaction, according to the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada. A gym, a landlord, a phone company, or a utility whose pre-authorized payment bounces can therefore cost two to four times what the bank itself is now allowed to charge. One declined payment can still cost $30 to $50 in total. The cap shrank the bank slice of a mistake. It did not shrink the whole mistake.

One more boundary: most fintech accounts do not charge NSF fees at all, because they simply decline the transaction. That is not the same as protection. The payment still fails, and the payee fee can still follow.

The habit fees: ATMs, paper, and per use charges

Mistakes are rare for most teens. Habits are weekly. The fees below come from one published schedule, TD's bank account fees page, checked on November 7, 2026, and the student account on that page is the comparison point because it is built for this age group. Other banks publish their own schedules, and the amounts differ. The shape of the trap is the same.

The ATM line deserves a second look. Cash is still part of teen life: a market stall, a group gift, a busker, a split bill with a friend who has no data left. A common pattern for a teen account is a weekly allowance landing in it, with the teen buying personal items from that money and planning the week around what is actually there. Two cash withdrawals a month at another bank's machine is an easy habit inside that pattern, and on the schedule above each one quietly costs $2.

Paper looks free on the student plan and is not free everywhere. Under the federal Commitment on Low-Cost and No-Cost Accounts, in force since December 1, 2025, signatory institutions must offer accounts with free digital statements and no extra charge for monthly printed statements, and youth aged 18 and under are a mandatory group for the no-cost version of that account, with the monthly fee set at $0. A teen who is paying for paper, or paying a monthly fee at all, can ask by name for the account offered under that commitment.

What a careless year adds up to

Labelled worked example, built only from the TD student schedule checked on November 7, 2026, with the habits named as assumptions so the arithmetic can be redone. Assume two withdrawals a month at another bank's ATM, at $2 each: $48 over a year. Assume one pre-authorized payment bounces during that year: $10 to the bank under the cap, plus a payee dishonoured payment fee in the $20 to $40 range, for $30 to $50 for that single mistake.

Total for the year: $78 to $98, on an account whose monthly fee is $0.

None of those charges buys anything. The $48 buys cash that the teen's own bank would have handed over for no fee, and the $30 to $50 buys a payment that still has to be made afterwards, now late. That is the honest size of the problem in 2026: smaller than the old $45 to $48 per bounce era, and still large enough to matter against a part time paycheque.

Overdraft: paying to have the payment go through

Overdraft protection flips the mistake. Instead of declining the payment, the bank covers it and charges for the cover. On the TD schedule checked above, the student account's monthly overdraft protection costs $0 per month plus 21% annual interest on the overdraft balance. The pay as you go version costs a $5 daily maximum fee plus the same 21% interest, and it is not available on Quebec accounts. A separate payment coverage fee of $5 plus interest applies when TD approves a payment that is larger than the available balance, with Quebec account holders who have overdraft protection paying only the interest.

The decision framework is a comparison of two bad days. Without cover, a declined payment costs $10 at the bank plus $20 to $40 at the payee, and the bill stays unpaid. With payment coverage, the same payment goes through for $5 plus a small amount of interest, and there is no payee fee because the payee was paid. Cover wins on the day of a rare mistake.

Cover loses when it becomes a habit, because interest at 21% on a balance that stays negative week after week is a slow fee that never shows up as a single scary line.

So the rule is about frequency, not fear. A teen whose account dips below zero once or twice a year is usually better served by alerts and a small buffer than by any paid protection tier. A teen whose balance regularly runs to zero before payday has a budgeting problem that overdraft pricing will happily monetize, and the fix sits in the spending plan, not the fee schedule.

A four step fee audit for any teen account

  1. Find the fee schedule, not the brochure. Banks must disclose all applicable fees in the account agreement and fee schedule. Search the bank's site for its fees page and read the rows for NSF, other bank ATMs, paper statements, and per use charges on the exact plan name on the account.
  2. Turn on the balance alert and set it with room to spare. Banks must send an electronic alert when a personal account balance falls below $100 by default, or below a threshold the account holder sets. Set the threshold above the largest regular pre-authorized payment, so the warning arrives while there is still time to move money.
  3. Count last year's fees from the statements. Add every NSF, ATM, and service charge line for the past 12 months. Compare that total with the worked example above. A total near $0 means the current habits are fine, whatever the schedule says.
  4. Ask for the no-cost account by name if a monthly fee appears. At signatory institutions, youth aged 18 and under qualify for the no-cost account under the low-cost and no-cost commitment, with at least 18 debit transactions a month and no minimum balance.

What success looks like: a full year of statements with no NSF lines, no other bank ATM charges, and a monthly fee of $0 that finally means what it appears to mean.
