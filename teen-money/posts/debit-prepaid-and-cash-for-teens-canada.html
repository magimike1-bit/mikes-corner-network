Debit, prepaid, or cash: how teens in Canada should pay

Banking · 8 min read

A teen's money usually lives in one of three places: folded in a pocket, sitting in a bank account behind a debit card, or loaded onto a prepaid card. All three spend like money, but they differ in fees, limits, what happens when the card disappears, and what the method quietly fails to do for the future. This guide compares them side by side, using the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) and Interac as sources.

Three ways to pay, three different pots of money

Cash is the money itself. Hand it over and the payment is finished: no account, no card, no record unless a receipt is kept. A debit card is a key to a bank account. In Canada, in-store debit usually runs on the Interac network, and each purchase pulls money directly out of the linked account — when the account is empty, the card stops working. FCAC notes that a person can open a bank account even without a job and without money to deposit right away; the bank must confirm identity with proper identification and provide information about its fees. A prepaid card is a pot of money loaded onto the card itself. FCAC describes it plainly: the card is preloaded or can be loaded with funds, and when it is used, the merchant deducts the amount from the total on the card. Cards issued by a financial institution carry a payment network logo — American Express, Mastercard, or Visa — and work at most merchants that accept that network's credit cards. That last detail causes most of the confusion: a prepaid card looks like a credit card and is swiped like one, but no borrowing is happening. The money was put there first.

Read the fee row carefully: it is where prepaid quietly differs. FCAC lists charges a prepaid card may carry across its life — activation, purchases, balance checks, ATM withdrawals, replacement, reloading, overdrafts, transferring funds to a new card, maintenance after non-use, and a monthly charge to keep the card active. Not every card charges every fee, and this guide names no product's prices, because fees vary by card and change. FCAC's own worked example shows why the categories matter: on a $50 prepaid card with a $4.95 activation fee, the amount actually available to spend is $45.05 — taken before the first purchase.

What prepaid does not do

Two limits matter for the future, and both are easy to miss because nothing goes visibly wrong.

It does not build a credit history. FCAC explains that a credit report is a summary of credit history, created when a person first borrows money or applies for credit, and built from borrowing behaviour — accounts opened, balances, and payment history on money owed. Spending loaded or banked money is not borrowing, so none of it is reported as credit use. Debit and prepaid use can teach budgeting, but a credit history eventually requires an actual credit product, used carefully — a separate decision, covered by this site's first-credit-card guide.

It does not automatically come with deposit insurance. FCAC's prepaid rights page makes the point concrete: federally regulated issuers must disclose a statement that the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) does not insure the funds when that is the case. Do not assume either way — the packaging disclosure tells the truth for that specific card. Other rules do protect the balance: federally regulated issuers cannot set an expiry date on the funds loaded onto a prepaid card (the plastic itself can expire, and moving funds to a new card may cost a fee), while promotional prepaid cards — handed out in loyalty or award programs — are different: their funds usually can expire, they cannot be reloaded, and they cannot be used for cash withdrawals. Maintenance charges on regular prepaid cards generally cannot start until a year after activation, unless the card is promotional or reloadable with the holder's express consent, and overdraft charges require express consent too.

Safety rules for each method

Interac's debit safety guidance is short and worth following literally: shield the PIN at checkouts and bank machines, insert a chip card rather than swiping when given the choice, keep an eye on the card during a transaction, check statements regularly, and contact the financial institution immediately about anything unusual — and immediately if the card is lost, stolen, or kept by a bank machine. The PIN should be memorized, unique, and never built from obvious information such as a phone number, birth date, address, or Social Insurance Number. Those habits transfer to prepaid with one addition: register the card. FCAC is explicit that registration is what protects the holder from unauthorized transactions if the card is lost or stolen, and that without it, a lost prepaid card can be exactly like lost cash. Cash's safety rule is the simplest and harshest: carry only what the day needs. One more prepaid habit: check the balance before relying on the card — a merchant can decline it when the balance is short, and hotels, rental agencies, and pay-at-the-pump stations may place temporary holds that shrink the usable balance further.

The pre-purchase card check: a numbered procedure

Before buying or loading a prepaid card — or relying on one received as a gift — run this five-minute check.

  1. Find the information box on the packaging or the issuer's page. Federally regulated issuers must disclose all charges in an information box on the exterior packaging, plus restrictions on use, the issuer's name, and a toll-free number. If the fees cannot be found, that is the answer: do not load money yet.
  2. List every fee category that applies — activation, per-purchase, balance check, ATM, replacement, reload, maintenance, monthly — and note when each one can start, especially any maintenance charge and the date it becomes possible.
  3. Check the expiry lines separately: the card's expiry date, and whether the funds expire. For a regular financial-institution prepaid card the funds should not expire; for a promotional card, assume they do until the agreement says otherwise.
  4. Read the insurance and loss lines: the CDIC disclosure statement, and what happens on loss or theft, including whether registration is required for protection and what a replacement costs.
  5. Register the card the same day it is activated, record the card number and the toll-free number somewhere separate from the card, and keep the packaging until the balance is spent.

What success looks like: before money is loaded, the family can state the card's possible fees, when any maintenance fee could begin, whether the funds expire, whether they are CDIC-insured or explicitly not, and the number to call if the card disappears — and the card is registered, with its number recorded off the card itself.

The decision framework: match the method to the job

Everyday spending near home, with a bank account available: debit is usually the strongest default — a full transaction record, a lost card can be stopped, and no stack of per-use card fees shrinking the balance, just the account's own disclosed terms to check once.

Online purchases, app stores, or a gift for a teen without an account: this is prepaid's real job. It works where its network logo is accepted and caps the damage from a compromised number at the loaded balance, because the card is not a key to a whole bank account. Run the card check first and register it.

Small, in-person, one-off spending — a market stall, a school trip, a first allowance: cash still wins on simplicity. It charges nothing, needs no terminal, and makes a budget physically visible. Its weakness is total: no record, and no recovery if lost.

Money that must last: none of the three is a savings plan. Larger amounts belong in an account, not on a prepaid card with maintenance fees ticking toward them. The payment method is for spending; the account is for keeping.

Frequently asked questions

Is a prepaid card just a debit card without a bank? Not quite. A debit card pulls from an account that can receive deposits and hold savings. A prepaid card holds only what was loaded onto it, may charge fees a bank account does not, generally cannot be cancelled for a refund of the balance — FCAC notes the usual way to access the funds is to spend them — and may leave the holder unprotected if lost before being registered.

Can a prepaid card go into overdraft or debt? The card spends down a loaded balance, and federally regulated issuers can only charge overdraft fees with the holder's express consent. It is not a credit product and using it does not create a credit record — in either direction, good or bad.

The card expired but still had money on it. Is the money gone? For prepaid cards from federally regulated financial institutions, the funds do not expire even when the card does. Contact the issuer: it may send a new card or a cheque for the balance, and may charge a fee for doing so. Promotional cards are the exception — their funds can expire.

Which is safest for a first card? Each method fails differently: cash fails silently and completely, prepaid fails if it is unregistered, and debit exposes an account — which is why safety habits matter more than the label. Families often use both debit and prepaid, for different jobs, which is what the framework above is for.
