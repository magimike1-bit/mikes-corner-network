Gift cards, store credit, and refunds: what teens need to know in Ontario

Consumer rights · 8 min read

Most teens meet three kinds of "store money" early on: the gift card in the birthday envelope, the store credit offered at the returns counter, and the refund they expected and did not always get. They look similar — value tied to a store instead of cash — but the rules behind them are completely different. Ontario's consumer protection pages spell out those rules in business-facing language; this guide translates them for teens and parents.

Three kinds of store value

A gift card is value bought in advance and stored on a card usable at a retailer or group of retailers. In Ontario it is governed by gift card rules under the Consumer Protection Act, so its protections come from the law. Store credit is value created at the returns counter: the store does not hand over cash, and instead gives credit usable in that store. It is a creature of the store's own return policy, which is why its terms vary so much. A refund is money back — cash, or a reversal to the original payment method — the only one of the three that returns actual money. Whether one is owed depends on the situation, not on asking nicely.

The Ontario gift card rules, in plain language

Ontario's rules, described in the province's business guide to consumer protection, work in the consumer's favour. Expiry: most retail businesses cannot put an expiry date on gift cards, gift certificates, or vouchers; if one is printed anyway, it does not count. Fees: a business cannot charge activation or service fees to buy or use the card, or dormancy fees that shrink the balance over time. The charges it may impose are narrow: customizing a card, and replacing a lost or stolen one. Tax: gift cards are not subject to tax when sold — tax applies to what is bought with them.

Three exceptions keep this honest. One: a gift card for one specific service — Ontario's example is a gift certificate for a massage at a spa — may carry an expiry date. Two: cards issued for a charitable purpose are exempt. Three: open-loop cards usable at multiple unaffiliated retailers, such as many mall gift cards, follow a special rule in Ontario's regulation: a dormancy fee of up to $2.50 per month may be charged, but only starting 15 months after the end of the month the agreement was entered into — or 18 months if the holder asks for an extension during the 15th month — and only with the fee clearly disclosed on the card and at purchase. A mall card holds value for well over a year, but it is not the permanent, fee-free instrument a store gift card is.

The return myth: policy is not law

Here is the sentence that surprises most people, straight from Ontario's returns guidance: the Consumer Protection Act sets out refund requirements in some circumstances, but it does not provide a general right to return or exchange goods. A store refusing a change-of-mind return is not breaking the law — it is following its own policy, which is why Ontario says to learn the policy before buying and get it in writing. Teens learn this the hard way at the counter; learning it in advance is cheaper.

That is not the whole story, because refunds are required in specific situations. If a business misrepresents its product or service, the consumer has the right to ask for a refund. Certain types of agreements — door-to-door sales and some contracts made away from the business's premises, among others — carry cooling-off rights and refund obligations under the Act. And a store that posts a return policy is expected to honour it. The decision tree at the counter is: what did the store promise (policy), what kind of purchase was this (specific rights), and what is wrong with the item (misrepresentation changes everything)?

The return procedure: a numbered run-through

Before buying, and before returning, run this sequence — the procedure Ontario's guidance recommends, in the order a teen would need it.

  1. Before paying, ask the three policy questions: full or partial refunds, exchanges, or store credit — which does this store offer? What proof of purchase is needed? Is there a time limit?
  2. Get the answer in writing — the policy printed on the receipt, a photo of the in-store sign, or a saved screenshot of the online returns page. Ontario recommends the written form for exactly this reason.
  3. Keep the receipt, tags, and original packaging until you are sure the item is a keeper. Proof of purchase is what the policy questions are really about: no proof, much harder return.
  4. If returning, go back with the item, the receipt, and the policy evidence from step 2. State what you are asking for — refund, exchange, or store credit are different outcomes, and the store may offer only some.
  5. If the item was misrepresented — it does not do what the store said — say so specifically, because that invokes a stronger refund right than change-of-mind.
  6. If the store refuses what its own policy promised, ask for the refusal in writing and keep it; that is the point where a consumer complaint becomes appropriate.

What success looks like: before purchase, the teen can state the store's refund, exchange, and store-credit terms and has them saved in writing; the receipt and packaging are kept; and if a return is needed, the request names a specific outcome and carries proof of purchase — with a misrepresentation stated plainly if one occurred.

When a store closes with your gift card on it

This is where the no-expiry rule meets its limit: the law keeps the card valid, but a closed store cannot take it. Ontario's consumer protection site notes, in words quoted by the Consumers Council of Canada, that if you have a gift card for a business that has closed, your options may be limited. The Council explains why: holders are likely to be treated as unsecured creditors — near the back of the line when the business's debts are sorted out.

The practical procedure, ordered by likelihood of helping:

  1. Spend it at the first credible sign of trouble. Closing businesses often give a short window for gift cards. Using the card early beats researching rights later.
  2. Check for other doors. A mall card still works at the mall's other stores; a chain card may work at other locations or online. Read the card's terms for which entity stands behind it.
  3. Contact the business or, in a bankruptcy, the trustee. Cardholders may be able to file a claim; recovery is uncertain and consumers are typically paid late in the order.
  4. Report the problem to Ontario's consumer protection department — the Council notes provincial departments can sometimes exert non-binding influence with the merchant or franchisor. Keep the card, the purchase receipt, and any balance records.
  5. If the card was bought very recently on a credit card, the purchaser can ask their card issuer about their options — another reason the buyer, not just the recipient, should keep the receipt.

The preventive habit costs nothing: treat gift cards like money with a weak safe. Do not stockpile them — a card for a store a teen rarely visits should be spent, swapped, or given away promptly. The no-expiry law protects the card's value; only the holder protects its usability.

The decision framework: before a gift card enters the picture

Receiving one: check what kind it is — store, mall/open-loop, or single-service — because expiry and fee rules differ by type. A mall card gets a calendar note at month 14, before any dormancy fee window can begin; a store card gets spent, not saved.

Buying one as a gift: prefer cards from stable retailers, read the terms for who issues the card, and keep the purchase receipt — it is the proof that makes every later step possible. The province bans activation fees on standard retail gift cards, so a "purchase fee" on one is worth questioning.

At the returns counter: the three outcomes are not equal. A refund is real money; store credit is store-bound value on store terms; an exchange is a swap. Ask what is being offered before accepting.

Hearing closure rumours: use the card now. Rumours are the cheapest early warning a gift card ever gets.

Frequently asked questions

Can a teen buy a gift card for themselves to use later? Yes — for teens without a bank account it is a legitimate way to carry store money. The same rules apply: most Ontario retail gift cards cannot expire and fees are tightly restricted, but the balance is vulnerable if the store closes, so buy for a stable store and use it promptly.

Is store credit the same as a gift card? No. A purchased gift card is protected by Ontario's gift card rules; store credit from the returns counter follows the store's own policy, so its expiry and fees are whatever that store says. Treat it as weaker value and spend it first.

A store's sign says "no refunds." Can they do that? For a change-of-mind return, generally yes — Ontario gives no general legal right to return or exchange goods. The sign does not erase stronger rights: misrepresentation, and cooling-off rules for certain agreements, still apply.

What if the gift card was bought in another province? Gift card rules are provincial, and this guide covers Ontario's. Other provinces have their own versions, many with similar no-expiry rules and different exceptions — another reason to keep the purchase receipt.
