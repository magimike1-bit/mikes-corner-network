Part-time job scams targeting teens: fake offers, cheque tricks, and the SIN test

Jobs & earning · 8 min read

A first part-time job is supposed to be where money starts flowing toward a teen. Job scams reverse the direction: the "job" is a machine for taking a young person's money, bank account, or identity, and it is aimed precisely at people who have never been hired before and do not yet know what a real hiring process looks like. Most published guidance is written for university students. This guide is written for the high-school version of the same problem, using the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's scheme catalogue and the federal SIN rules, both checked on October 22, 2026.

Why first-job hunters get targeted

Every scam in the Anti-Fraud Centre's job-fraud catalogue exploits the same gap: a first-time job hunter cannot tell an unusual request from a normal one. An experienced worker knows employers do not mail cheques to new hires or hire "financial agents" by text message. A sixteen-year-old does not know that yet, and may also be flattered enough by being "selected" that the questions stop. Scammers also know where teens look: the offers arrive through unsolicited email and text, online classified ads, job posting websites, social media ads, and messaging apps — often using the names of real Canadian companies to borrow their credibility.

The protection is not suspicion of everything. It is knowing the real sequence well enough that a wrong step stands out. That sequence is short, and it is the backbone of this guide.

Real hiring vs scam hiring, step by step

StepHow real hiring goesHow the scam version goes
First contactThe teen applies, or a real posting on a verifiable company site leads to an applicationAn offer arrives unsolicited — for a job never applied for — by text, email, or messaging app
InterviewA real conversation, in person or by video, with someone whose name and role can be verifiedNo interview at all, or a chat-only exchange, and the offer comes immediately
PayAn hourly wage in line with the work and the province's normsHigh pay promised for minimal work, "boosting" tasks, or surveys
Personal informationSIN requested only after hiring — by law, no later than 3 days after the start dateSIN, date of birth, ID photos, or banking details demanded up front, often through a form
Money movementMoney moves one way: from the employer to the teen, as payThe teen is asked to send money: deposits, training or equipment fees, forwarding payments, buying gift cards
Contact detailsAn official company domain for email and a website that matches itA free web-based email address instead of a company domain; a "company" that cannot be found or called

One mismatched row is a question to ask. Two or more is a verdict: stop. The money-movement row is the heaviest of the six — in a real job, a teen never pays, deposits, forwards, or converts money as part of being hired. The moment money is asked to move out of the teen's account, the job is the scam.

The SIN test: one rule that exposes most fake offers

Canada's SIN Code of Practice gives first-job hunters an unusually clean test. Two facts, both official:

  • A SIN is not required when completing an employment application — that is, before a job offer. The Code of Practice lists exactly that situation among the times a SIN is not legally required.
  • Once hired, the timing runs the other way: an individual must provide their SIN to their employer no later than 3 days after the employment start date, and not having a SIN yet does not prevent them from starting work.

Put together, the rule a teen can carry into any conversation is one sentence: a real employer asks for my SIN after I am hired, within my first three days — never on an application, never by form before an offer. Any "employer" who needs a SIN, a photo of ID, or banking details before an offer exists has failed the test, whatever the story attached. The SIN is also confidential and is not an identity document — and the law does not allow a business to require it for purposes other than income reporting, or to deny a service because someone refused to provide it where the law does not require it.

The cheque trick and the money-mule job, in plain terms

The counterfeit-cheque job. It arrives dressed as mystery shopping, personal assistant work, caregiving, data entry, or car wrapping. A cheque shows up with instructions: deposit it, keep a portion as pay, and send the rest onward — to a specific account, or out as gift cards whose numbers get reported back as "proof." The deposit appears in the account, which feels like confirmation. It is not. The cheque is counterfeit; when the bank finishes processing and finds that out, the deposit is reversed — and the victim is responsible for the funds already withdrawn and sent. The money that left was the teen's own. The Anti-Fraud Centre's summary of the mystery-shopper version is blunt: you later find out the cheque was fake and you now owe money to the bank.

The money-mule job. Called "financial agent" or "client manager," this one skips the cheque: the job is to receive e-transfers or wire transfers into a personal bank account and forward the money on, sometimes converted through Bitcoin, keeping a commission. The Anti-Fraud Centre states what that money actually is — funds from compromised accounts or other fraud victims — and what the "employee" has become: a money mule who could be arrested for money laundering. A commission does not change that; it is the bait, not a defence.

The task job. The newer variation offers freelance work "boosting" products, apps, or videos, or running a web-survey or online-store job through social media and messaging apps. Small early payments build trust, the on-screen balance climbs, and then deposits are required to unlock higher levels or withdraw earnings — deposits that can never be recovered, into balances that were never real. The Anti-Fraud Centre logged an increase in this crypto job fraud pattern in 2024, using the names of real Canadian companies.

The ten-minute verification procedure

Run this together — teen and parent — before responding to any offer that did not come from a known, local employer:

  1. Find the company's official website independently — search for it; do not use a link from the message or posting.
  2. Check whether the job is posted on that official site. If the company has no posting and no careers page mentioning the role, treat the offer as unverified.
  3. Call the company's official phone number from its website — the Anti-Fraud Centre's own recommended step — and ask whether the offer and the person named in it are real.
  4. Compare the contact details: the email should come from the company's domain, not a free web-based address, and the named recruiter should be findable as part of the company.
  5. Apply the SIN test and the money test: anything requesting a SIN, ID, or banking details before an offer, or any movement of the teen's money in any direction other than being paid, ends the process.
  6. If any step fails, stop responding. Silence is a complete answer to a scammer.

What success looks like: the offer is confirmed on the company's own site and by a call to its official number, the hiring steps match the left-hand column of the table above, and the first document the teen is asked for is nothing more sensitive than a resume.

Already replied or sent money? Do this now

  1. Stop contact and stop moving money. Do not deposit any cheque that arrived, and do not forward anything further.
  2. Tell a parent and call the bank. Report the incident to the financial institution involved; if a cheque was deposited or money sent, the bank needs to know immediately.
  3. Gather the evidence: the posting, messages, emails, phone numbers, and any receipts. The Anti-Fraud Centre's victim guidance starts with collecting exactly this.
  4. If a SIN or ID was shared, place flags on the accounts, change passwords, and report identity fraud to both credit bureaus, Equifax and TransUnion.
  5. Report it: local police (ask for a file number) and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, online through Report Cybercrime and Fraud or by phone at 1-888-495-8501, Monday to Friday, 10 am to 4:45 pm Eastern. And if a "recovery agent" later offers to get the money back for a fee, that is the same fraud returning — never send recovery money.

Frequently asked questions

Can a real employer ask for a SIN on the application form? No. The SIN Code of Practice lists completing an employment application (before a job offer) among the situations where a SIN is not required. A real employer requests it after hiring, and the deadline is 3 days after the employment start date.

The cheque cleared and the money is in the account — isn't that proof it is real? No. A deposit appearing is not the same as a cheque being genuine. In the Anti-Fraud Centre's counterfeit-cheque pattern, the cheque is discovered to be fake after the victim has sent money onward, and the victim owes the bank for what was withdrawn.

Is it ever a real job to receive money and forward it for a company? No. The Anti-Fraud Centre describes that exact arrangement — "financial agent" or payment-processor roles — as money-mule recruitment: the funds come from compromised accounts or other victims, and forwarding them can lead to arrest for money laundering.

Where should a suspicious offer be reported? To local police, and to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre online through Report Cybercrime and Fraud or by phone at 1-888-495-8501.
