Phone plans and subscriptions: a teen's first recurring-cost audit

Budgeting · 8 min read

Ask a teen what their phone and apps cost each month and the answer is usually a guess — and usually low. That is not a teen failing. Recurring costs are designed to be forgettable: small amounts, automatic renewals, and charges scattered across a phone store, a payment service, and a family phone bill. Research reported in Canadian coverage of subscription habits found that people think they have roughly half the subscriptions they actually hold. The fix is not willpower. It is a short, repeatable audit that a teen runs with a parent, once, and then on a schedule — the first real recurring-cost check of a teen's financial life.

Why recurring costs are different from other spending

A snack is bought once. A subscription is bought every month until someone actively stops it, including months when it is never opened. That single difference changes the math of teen money. Allowance and part-time pay arrive in visible chunks and feel large; recurring charges leave in small pieces and feel like nothing. Left alone, the pieces set a floor under a teen's spending — money that is gone before any decision is made — and the floor only rises, because signing up takes a minute and cancelling takes an audit.

The phone plan belongs in the same conversation, even when a parent pays it. A teen line on a family plan is often the largest recurring cost attached to a teen, and it carries its own traps: data use, add-ons, and device payments. A teen who has never seen that cost cannot judge whether a $15 app charge is big or small. The audit below therefore covers both worlds together — the charges a teen pays directly, and the phone line a family pays on the teen's behalf.

The five hiding places

Recurring charges hide by location, not by size. Checking only a bank statement misses the charges billed through a phone store; checking only the phone misses the ones billed to a card. Work through all five places, every time.

1. The bank and card statements. Pull the last two to three months — three months catches charges that bill quarterly — and scan for any charge that repeats on about the same date. Check every account a charge could land on, not just the main one.

2. Apple subscriptions. On an iPhone or iPad, open Settings, tap the name at the top, then tap Subscriptions. Every active subscription billed through Apple is listed there, including ones whose apps were deleted long ago. Deleting an app does not cancel its subscription — the list, not the home screen, is the truth.

3. Google Play subscriptions. On Android, open the Google Play Store, tap the profile picture, then Payments and subscriptions, then Subscriptions. Charges billed through Google live here and nowhere else on the phone.

4. PayPal automatic payments. Some services bill through PayPal even when a card was the original payment method. On desktop, the gear icon opens Settings, then Payments, then Automatic payments shows the recurring arrangements still active.

5. Amazon memberships and subscriptions. Under Your Account, Memberships and subscriptions catches memberships and subscriptions billed through Amazon, a common home for a family streaming or reading charge a teen uses daily and a parent forgot signing up for.

Add a sixth glance for the phone bill itself: open the family's most recent wireless bill and find the teen's line, its plan cost, any device payment, and any add-on or overage line from the last three bills.

The audit: step by step

  1. Gather the sources before judging anything: three months of statements, the phone opened to its subscription list, and the last wireless bill.
  2. Write every recurring charge on one list — a notebook page or a phone note — with four columns: service, monthly cost, where it bills, and when it renews.
  3. Add the phone line as its own row: plan share, device payment if any, and add-ons, each as a separate line so nothing hides inside a total.
  4. For each row, write the date it was last actually used. The working question from audit practice is simple: was this used in the last 30 days?
  5. Convert every monthly cost to an annual cost by multiplying by 12, and total both columns. Decide nothing until the annual column exists.
  6. Mark each row keep, downgrade, or cancel using the framework below, and cancel the cancels in the same sitting, in the place where each subscription lives.
  7. Record what was cancelled and the date, set a calendar reminder to repeat the audit in six months, and move the first month of savings somewhere deliberate — a savings goal, not general spending.

What success looks like: one written list accounts for every recurring charge across all five hiding places plus the phone line, each with a monthly and an annual cost, a last-used date, and a decision. The teen can state the total monthly floor out loud. If a charge surfaces next month that is not on the list, the audit missed a hiding place — repeat that section, do not abandon the system.

Monthly is marketing, annual is truth

Sellers quote monthly prices because $15 sounds painless. The audit's most powerful step is also its simplest arithmetic: multiply by 12. A $15 monthly charge is $180 a year. A $25 charge is $300 a year. Three forgotten charges at those sizes approach the cost of a meaningful savings goal — the kind of goal teen saving guides build plans around, such as a laptop, a bike, or the first stretch toward a car. None of those multiplications is a claim about any specific service's price; they are the teen's own statement amounts, converted. Whatever number sits in the monthly column, the annual column is the honest one to decide on, because a year is the scale at which teen goals live.

The same conversion disciplines the keep decisions. A service used daily that costs $180 a year may be an easy keep. A service used twice that costs $300 a year is a cancel wearing a small monthly disguise. Without the annual column, both feel identical — a line on a statement — and that feeling is precisely what the audit exists to replace with arithmetic.

The phone line itself, and a Canadian protection worth knowing

On a shared family plan, data is the risk that turns a fixed cost into a surprise. Canada's Wireless Code provides a backstop: data overage charges are to be suspended once they reach $50 in a monthly billing cycle for the account, and data roaming charges at $100 in a cycle, unless the account holder consents to further charges. Two teen-relevant points follow. First, the cap is per account, and the account holder is the person whose consent matters — on most family plans, that is the parent, which is why a teen should never treat an overage prompt as theirs to approve without the account holder. Second, a cap is a ceiling, not a target: $50 of overage is still $50. The audit's job is prevention — checking the plan's data amount against the teen's real use on the bill, and flagging a mismatch to the account holder before the next cycle, not after a capped surprise.

While the bill is open, check two more rows. A device payment that has finished should disappear from the bill; if a completed device balance is still being charged, that is a question for the provider with the bill in hand. And add-ons attached to the teen line — extra features added during a promotion or a store visit — deserve the same last-30-days test as any app subscription.

Keep, downgrade, or cancel: a decision framework

Decide each row with three questions, in order. Was it used in the last 30 days? If not, cancel — re-subscribing later is always possible, and paying for the option to use something is the most expensive way to not use it. If it was used, would a cheaper tier, a family or shared version, or an annual price the family already pays cover the same use? If yes, downgrade rather than cancel, and note the renewal date so the cheaper tier gets the same test next audit. If it was used and no cheaper route covers it, keep it — consciously, at its annual cost, written on the list. A kept subscription is a chosen expense; the audit's enemy was never spending, it was spending nobody chose.

One family rule makes the framework stick: any new subscription gets written on the list the day it starts, with its renewal date, including free trials. A trial is a subscription with a delayed first charge, and the list is only useful if it is complete. When the six-month reminder fires, the audit takes minutes, because the list already exists — the first audit is the only hard one.

Frequently asked questions

Does deleting an app cancel its subscription? No. Subscriptions billed through Apple or Google Play continue until cancelled in the subscription list for that store, which is why the audit checks those lists directly.

How often should a teen run this audit? Once fully, then every six months by calendar reminder — and whenever a new subscription or trial starts, add it to the list the same day.

What if a charge is on a parent's card? It still belongs on the list, marked with where it bills. The decision conversation is a family one, and the account holder — the parent — makes any change that affects a shared plan or a parent's payment method.
