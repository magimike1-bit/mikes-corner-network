RRSP basics for teen workers: file first, contribute later

Tax Basics · 6 min read

The common advice gets the order backwards. A teen with a part-time job does not usually need to open a registered retirement savings plan (RRSP) this year, and putting money into one is often the least useful step available. The useful step is quieter: file a tax return. Filing is what creates RRSP contribution room, room that never expires and will be genuinely valuable later, when income is high enough to be taxed. This guide separates the three decisions that are usually mashed together: file, build room, and contribute. For a teen worker, only the first one is urgent.

Filing is the move that builds the room

RRSP room is not granted by age. It is calculated from earned income reported on a tax return. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) builds a person's deduction limit from unused room carried forward from earlier years, plus new room, minus any pension adjustment from a workplace pension. No return, no calculation, a teen who never files leaves the room unbuilt even though the income was earned.

There is no minimum age in tax law for an RRSP, but the practical gate is the same either way: the holder needs earned income, a social insurance number, Canadian residency, and a filed return. A bank's summary puts it plainly, opening an RRSP requires having filed an income tax return in previous years. So the sequence for a teen worker is fixed: earn, file, and let the room accumulate on paper until it is worth using.

How much room a teen worker actually gets

New room each year is the smaller of two numbers: 18% of the previous year's earned income, or the annual dollar limit, $33,810 for 2026. Unused room carries forward indefinitely and is added on top, and the personal total appears on the notice of assessment and in CRA My Account.

Worked example, with the arithmetic shown: a teen earns $6,000 from a summer and part-time job in one year and files a return. New RRSP room for the following year is 18% of $6,000, which is $1,080. The dollar limit is nowhere in sight at teen earnings, the 18% figure is the binding one, and will be for years. That $1,080 does not expire. It waits, and every filed year adds another layer beside it.

Earned income is the key phrase. Employment and self-employment earnings count toward room; investment income does not. Interest earned on savings, however welcome, builds no RRSP room at all.

Contribute now or later? The deduction-timing decision

An RRSP does two separate things that are worth keeping apart. Contributions go in and grow sheltered, with tax deferred until withdrawal. Separately, each contribution generates a deduction, a reduction of taxable income, and the deduction does not have to be claimed in the year the money goes in. It can be carried forward and claimed in a later year.

That timing is the whole decision for a teen. A deduction is worth exactly the tax it removes. A teen whose earnings fall under the basic personal amount owes no income tax, so a deduction claimed now removes nothing, it is spent for zero benefit. The same deduction claimed in a year with real tax owing is worth real money. Financial commentary on exactly this point advises teens to file, build the room, and save the deduction for a higher-income year rather than claiming it against income that was never going to be taxed.

None of this makes contributing at 17 or 18 wrong in every case. It makes it a decision with a price: money inside an RRSP is retirement money, withdrawals are taxed as income, and the deduction's value depends entirely on when it is claimed. A teen who cannot name the tax the deduction will save is not ready to make the contribution.

The over-contribution line, stated plainly

Contributing more than the available room carries a tax of 1% per month on the excess, with one cushion. The CRA allows contributions to exceed the deduction limit by up to $2,000 without that tax applying; the 1% monthly tax falls on the amount above the limit plus the cushion. At teen scale this is mostly a reason to check the notice of assessment before contributing, not a reason for alarm, and the cushion is a safety net, not extra room to plan around.

A teen worker's order of operations

  1. File every year there is income, even when no tax is owing. The return is what converts a paycheque into recorded RRSP room.
  2. Read the notice of assessment after filing and note the RRSP deduction limit. That figure, also visible in CRA My Account, is the only room number that counts.
  3. Keep near-term savings reachable, in a savings account, or a TFSA once eligible, rather than locking goal money into a retirement account.
  4. Revisit contributing when income is actually taxed. That is when a contribution, and the choice of when to claim its deduction, starts doing real work.

The RRSP rewards patience twice over for a teen worker: the room built by filing never expires, and the deduction saved for a taxed year is worth more than the same deduction spent against untaxed income. Filing is a half-hour habit. It is also, at this age, the entire strategy.
