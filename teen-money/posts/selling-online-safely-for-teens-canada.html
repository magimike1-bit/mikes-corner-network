Selling online safely for teens in Canada: payments, meetups, and the tax line

Jobs & earning · 9 min read

Selling your own stuff online — the outgrown bike, last year's games, the jacket that no longer fits — is most teens' first taste of earning money from strangers. It is also a transaction with three separate risk systems that adults routinely mix up: the payment can be faked, the meetup can be unsafe, and the tax rules change completely depending on what kind of selling you are doing. This guide keeps the three separate. It is about peer selling — you, your belongings, a buyer you have never met — not about job hunting; hiring scams are a different risk and are covered in this site's guide to part-time job scams. The safety procedure below is built from Canadian police guidance, and the tax section from the Canada Revenue Agency's own definitions.

Two kinds of selling

Everything in this article turns on one distinction. Selling your own belongings means items you bought for yourself, used, and are now passing on — almost always for less than you paid. There was never a plan to profit; you are recovering part of a cost. Selling as a business means activity carried on for profit: buying things in order to resell them, making things in order to sell them, restocking, advertising, repeating. The CRA's definition of business income is exactly that — income from an activity you carry on for profit, where there is evidence to support that intention. The safety rules in the next two sections apply to both kinds equally. The tax rules in the last section mostly bite on the second kind — which is why knowing which kind you are doing matters before the money gets large, not after.

Payment safety: how the money tricks work

The seller-side payment tricks share one mechanism: a fake appearance of payment, designed to make you hand over the item or send money before you notice nothing arrived. The classic is the overpayment: a buyer "accidentally" sends more than the price — often with a fake e-transfer confirmation email as the proof — and asks you to refund the difference. The original payment was never real, so the refund is the theft. Vancouver Police give sellers the counter-rule in one line: if someone forwards you proof of payment by email, text, or app message, double-check your own bank account to confirm the money actually arrived. A screenshot is not a payment; your account balance is. The same skepticism covers the newer variants: QR codes or links sent "to verify your identity" that ask for sensitive information such as your date of birth, and any buyer who steers you toward gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency — payment methods fraud guidance flags precisely because they offer the other side almost no protection. For in-person sales, cash verified in your hand is the simplest settlement there is; check that cash is not counterfeit before the buyer leaves. And the mirror-image rule from police buy-and-sell guidance: never pay or send a deposit for an item you have not received, and never hand over an item against a promise to pay later.

The meetup procedure

  1. Set the listing up safely. Use clear photos of the item itself, describe its condition honestly, and keep personal information out of the listing — no home address, no full name, nothing that identifies where you live or go to school.
  2. Keep the conversation on the record. Message inside the platform where possible, and screenshot the listing, the buyer's profile and contact details, and the conversation before you meet. Police guidance recommends exactly this paper trail.
  3. Choose the place: public, daylight, ideally a designated exchange zone. Many Canadian police services run Buy and Sell Exchange Zones — marked, video-monitored spaces at police stations (Peel Regional Police, for example, maintains them at several of its divisions; Winnipeg Police has them at all four of its stations). Officers do not supervise or help with the transaction itself; the cameras and the location are the protection. Any busy public place in daylight is the fallback standard.
  4. Do not go alone, and tell someone the plan. Bring a parent or a friend. Before you leave, a family member should know where you are going, who you are meeting, and when you expect to be back.
  5. Treat refusals and changes as signals. A buyer who refuses to meet at a public place or an exchange zone is a red flag in itself, and a last-minute change to a private address or an isolated spot is a reason to cancel, not to adapt. Your home and the buyer's home are both off the list.
  6. Complete the exchange in the open. Let the buyer inspect the item, confirm the payment in your own account (or take the cash and check it) before the item changes hands, and bring no more cash than the transaction needs.
  7. Walk away when it feels wrong. Every police guide on this page ends with the same instruction: trust your instincts, and if something feels off, end the meeting. No sale is worth overriding that signal, and a legitimate buyer will accept a cancellation without argument.

What success looks like: the exchange happened in a public, preferably camera-covered place, in daylight, with another person present or nearby; the payment was verified in your own account or counted in your hand before the item left it; and a screenshot record of the listing and the conversation still exists afterward. If any one of those four is missing, the procedure — not the buyer — is what needs fixing next time.

The tax line: when selling becomes a business

The CRA does not tax hobbies and closet-clearing the way it taxes businesses, and the dividing line is intention, evidenced by behaviour. For income tax purposes, the CRA defines a business as an activity where there is a reasonable expectation of profit and evidence to support that intention — and business income must be reported, all of it, on the tax return. Applied to a teen seller, the signs that selling has crossed the line are practical ones: buying items specifically to resell them, selling the same kind of item repeatedly and continuously, advertising and promoting what you sell, keeping inventory, and running the activity in a businesslike way with records aimed at making a profit. Occasionally selling your own used belongings for less than you paid shows none of those signs; a weekend flipping operation shows most of them. Two more rules belong on a growing seller's radar. Records: once selling is a business, keep the records a business keeps — what you paid, what you sold, what it cost you to sell — because business income is reported as gross and net figures, and memory is not a filing system. And GST/HST: a seller whose revenues from taxable sales pass $30,000 in a single calendar quarter, or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters, stops being a "small supplier" and must register for the GST/HST. Almost no teen closet sale comes near that line — but a teen whose reselling has genuinely become a business can reach it faster than expected, and it is measured on total sales, not profit.

A decision framework before you scale up

Run four questions, in order, whenever selling starts to feel like more than decluttering. Source: are the items yours, bought for your own use — or are you acquiring things in order to sell them? The second answer is the business line. Pattern: is this occasional and irregular, or regular, repeated, and promoted? Regular and promoted is business behaviour regardless of the amounts. Records: if the CRA asked you to show your numbers, could you? A seller who cannot reconstruct a season of sales from records should build the spreadsheet before the next sale, not after a letter arrives. Scale: are total sales, across everything, drifting anywhere near $30,000 over four quarters? If the honest answer to the first two questions says "business," the right next step is a conversation at home and, for anything beyond the basics, a qualified tax professional — the cost of that conversation is small next to filing years of business income as if it were pocket money.

FAQ

Do I have to report selling my old phone? Selling your own used belongings, occasionally and for less than you paid, is not an activity carried on for profit under the CRA's definition — there is no business income to report on a sale like that. The reporting duty attaches when the activity becomes a business, per the test above.

A buyer sent an e-transfer confirmation but nothing is in my account. What now? Nothing in your account means no payment, whatever the screenshot says. Do not hand over the item and do not "refund" any overpayment — that combination is the overpayment trick. Verify in your own account, every time.

Is it safe to meet at my house if a parent is home? Police guidance says no: meet in a public place, use an exchange zone where one exists, and do not invite strangers to your home or go to theirs. A parent present is good; a public location is the rule that makes it unnecessary to rely on that.

When exactly does flipping become taxable? There is no dollar threshold for income tax — the test is the activity itself: carried on for profit, with evidence of that intention. The $30,000 figure in this article is the GST/HST registration threshold, a separate rule. A small but genuine flipping business has business income from its first profitable pattern of sales.
