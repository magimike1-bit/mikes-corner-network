Teen bank accounts in Canada: ages, parents, and fees

Banking · 8 min read

A first bank account is where allowance, birthday money, and a first paycheque stop living in a drawer and start behaving like real money. For Canadian families the good news is that the system is more welcoming than most parents expect. The confusing part is not whether a teen can have an account — it is who has to be there to open it, what it should cost, and how much independence the account should give on day one. This guide answers those three questions using rules and offers checked on October 13, 2026, so a family can walk into the opening appointment already knowing what a fair outcome looks like.

There is no legislated minimum age in Canada

Start with the fact that surprises most parents: there is no legislated minimum age to open a regular savings account in Canada. A child does not have to wait for a birthday milestone set by law. What changes with age is the practical process around the account, not the right to have one. Most financial institutions require a parent or guardian to apply in person for children under 12 or 13, and the account is typically held jointly until the child reaches the age of majority.

That age of majority is provincial. It is 18 in Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Quebec, and Saskatchewan, and 19 in British Columbia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Northwest Territories, Nova Scotia, Nunavut, and Yukon. A family that moves provinces, or a teen heading to school in another province, should keep that difference in mind: the same 18-year-old is an adult account holder in one province and still a minor in another.

The federal consumer rules add a second reassurance. A person has the right to open a bank account even without a job, without money to put in the account right away, and even after a bankruptcy. A teen with no income history is not a special case that needs special pleading — no job and no opening deposit are ordinary, expected starting points.

What a parent actually does at opening

Parental involvement in Canada is mostly about identification, consent, and presence at the youngest ages — not about a parent controlling every later transaction forever. The bank must be able to confirm identity through proper identification, and it must obtain express consent and enter into an agreement before opening the account. At opening, the bank must also provide a copy of the account agreement and a list of all charges that apply. That list of charges is the single most useful document in the folder: keep it, because it is the dated record of what the account was promised to cost.

How much presence a bank requires depends on the teen's age and the bank's own rules, and the two verified examples below show how different those rules can be. One bank lets mid-teens open with or without parental signing authority; another requires an in-person branch appointment for anyone under 16. Neither approach is wrong. The lesson for families is to check the specific bank's age bands before booking the trip, rather than assuming a friend's experience at a different bank will repeat.

A practical pattern that works well at home, once the account exists, is to give the teen real jobs for the money in it. For example, a parent can pay a weekly allowance into the account and ask the teen to buy their own everyday necessities from it, adding the cost of an agreed necessity back into the next allowance when the teen messages about it. The account stops being a museum for savings and becomes practice: money arrives on a schedule, spending decisions are real, and planning ahead matters because the balance is visible.

Fees to watch even on a $0 account

A $0 monthly fee covers the rent, not every possible charge. The account agreement's list of all charges is where the rest lives, and a teen should learn to read it before the first surprise, not after. Sit together and look for charges that daily behaviour can trigger: using another bank's cash machine, sending a transfer in a way the account does not include, ordering paper items, or replacing a card. This guide does not list amounts for those charges, because they differ by bank and change over time — the dated list from the opening appointment is the only version a family should rely on.

One more habit protects the $0 promise over time: when a bank increases a fee or introduces a new charge, it must give written information about it. Teach the teen that bank messages about charges are not junk mail. A two-minute read when a change notice arrives beats discovering a new charge three statements later, and it builds the adult skill of treating terms as something that can change and must be re-checked.

Savings deserve a place in the same conversation. A useful household suggestion is that, while living at home, a teen puts at least half of what they earn into a savings account they do not tap while out spending, keeping it for a larger goal such as university, a car, or a laptop. Whether a family uses that exact split or a smaller one, a separate savings home makes the split visible — and a youth chequing account paired with savings makes the transfer a tap, not a trip.

Opening the account: a step-by-step procedure

How-to, in order:

  1. Pick two candidate accounts — ideally one where the family already banks and one challenger — and open each bank's official youth page together.
  2. Fill in the comparison rows above for each candidate: age group, monthly fee, included transactions, and how a teen of your teen's exact age opens it.
  3. Check the age band for your teen. Note whether opening is online, at a banking centre, or by booked branch appointment, and whether parental signing authority is optional or expected.
  4. Gather identification before booking anything. Confirm on the bank's page what the teen must show; for the CIBC offer above, that is one piece of government-issued photo ID.
  5. Book or start the opening, and at the appointment ask for the account agreement and the full list of charges in writing or by email.
  6. Before leaving, set up the app or online access, confirm how pay or allowance will arrive (transfer or direct deposit), and agree at home on the savings split for money that lands in the account.
  7. File the agreement and charge list with the date. That dated packet is the reference if a charge ever looks wrong.

What success looks like: the teen can state, without guessing, what the account costs per month ($0 on both offers above), how their next pay or allowance gets into it, what happens at the age of majority in their province, and where the dated list of charges is filed. If any of those four answers is a shrug, the opening is not finished yet — it just needs one more conversation.

A decision framework for parents

If the choice still feels stuck, decide in this order. First, age fit: does the bank's opening route match your teen's age today, not next year? Second, daily fit: are debit and Interac e-Transfer transactions unlimited, since those are the two actions a teen actually performs weekly? Third, family fit: does a parent already bank there, making transfers and troubleshooting simpler? Fourth, growth fit: what happens at 18 or 19, and at 25 when the youth pricing above ends? A bank that wins on the first two questions and is honest on the fourth is a sound choice, even if a rival advertises a perk your teen will never use.

Frequently asked questions

Can a teen open an account without a parent? Sometimes, depending on age and bank. In the verified CIBC example, teens 14 to 15 can open at a banking centre with or without parental signing authority, and teens 16 to the age of majority can open online or in person the same way. In the Scotiabank example, under-16 opening starts with an in-person branch appointment. There is no single Canada-wide answer — the bank's age band decides.

Does a teen need a job or money to open an account? No. Federal consumer information confirms a person can open an account without a job and without money to put in right away.

Are youth accounts really free? The two offers checked here charge no monthly fee for their youth groups — until age 25 at CIBC, and $0 per month under 25 or as an eligible student at Scotiabank. Other charges can still exist, which is why the written list of charges at opening matters more than the headline.
