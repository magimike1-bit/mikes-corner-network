Teen car insurance in Canada: own policy or listed on a parent policy?

Cars · 6 min read

A first licence does not have to mean a first policy. The common assumption, that a teen driver needs car insurance in their own name from day one, is backwards for most households: a teen can typically be covered under a parent or guardian policy, and there is usually no added premium for listing a learner driver at all. A separate policy is the exception, forced by who owns the car, not the starting point. The comparison belongs up top, because the route is chosen before any quote makes sense.

The comparison up top: listed vs own policy

Listed on a parent policyOwn policy
Who it fitsA teen living at the parent address who drives a parent owned carA teen who is the registered owner of the vehicle
Driver roleOccasional (secondary) driver, or primary driver of one family carPrimary driver by definition
Price patternUsually the most affordable route as an occasional driver; the premium jumps when the teen becomes the primary driver of a carMuch higher premiums, because a primary driver with little experience carries the full risk rating alone
Claims effectAn at fault accident by the teen can raise the parent premium and sits in the parent claims history until the teen holds a policy aloneThe record builds, and follows, the teen alone
Hard ruleWorks only while the living and driving facts match what the insurer was toldA registered owner must have their own policy; using the parent insurer may still earn a multi-vehicle discount

Two middle facts keep the table honest. First, the jump from learner to solo driver is where money appears: listing a learner usually adds no premium, and that can change once the driver earns a probationary licence and starts driving alone. Second, owning a car changes the options. If a learner owns their own car, that car can sometimes be added to a parent policy when everyone lives at the same address, depending on the provider. Registered ownership in the teen's name, though, points to a policy of their own.

Licence class matters separately from age. A new driver can hold a learner licence, a novice licence such as Ontario's G1 or G2, or a fresh full licence, while a young driver means anyone under about 25. Learner drivers can face restrictions on buying their own policy at all. Novice drivers can usually buy one, at higher prices.

What actually drives the price

Insurers price risk, and a new driver starts with the weakest evidence in the system: little experience, no insurance history, and no record of safe driving to point to. Teenage drivers are also statistically more likely to be involved in accidents than older drivers, so the starting premium is high even for a careful driver. Nothing about that first fact is personal, and none of it can be argued away at quote time.

Around that core sit the factors a family can see in advance. Insurers set prices by looking at the type of car, where the driver lives, and the driving record. Depending on the provider, age, gender, commute distance, and credit score can also have an effect. Vehicle choice carries more weight than most teens expect: a car's make, model, year, value, and repair costs have a major impact on the price, and a car that is less likely to be stolen, damaged, or in a collision, and cheaper to repair, costs less to insure.

The Insurance Bureau of Canada runs a tool, How Cars Measure Up, that ranks popular models by the frequency and cost of claims, built from claims data covering at least 1,500 insured vehicles per model between 2019 and 2024. Checking a shortlist there costs nothing and happens before any purchase.

Province can outweigh every other factor. In British Columbia, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba, basic insurance is bought from the public insurer, so the base price does not change from seller to seller. Sample figures for 19 year olds show the spread plainly: $1,285 a year in Saskatchewan and $1,865 in Manitoba, against $3,858 in British Columbia and $4,499 in Ontario. Those samples come from a February 2020 rate comparison, so treat them as evidence that province matters, not as prices anyone can buy today.

Reading a sample quote without fooling yourself

Dated price check, November 7, 2026: Ratehub's young driver page quotes $10,792 a year for an 18 year old male and $10,128 for an 18 year old female, falling to $6,077 and $6,247 by age 24 on the same profile. Those are real published sample quotes. They are also one driver wearing one set of clothes.

The profile behind them is a driver in Toronto, driving a 2020 Toyota Corolla, with a clean driving history, a $1,000 deductible on comprehensive and collision coverage, $1,000,000 of liability coverage, and a telematics discount already opted into. Change the city, the car, the deductible, or the record, and the number moves. Read every headline premium that way: as a single profile with its assumptions attached, useful for scale and for the shape of the age curve, useless as a prediction for a teen in another province driving a different car.

The age curve itself is the reliable part. On that same profile the male quote drops $4,715 between 18 and 24, and premiums typically keep falling after 25 for a driver with a clean record. Time, driven carefully, is the largest discount in the whole system.

The levers a teen can actually pull

Some levers work before the first quote. Completing an accredited driver training course earns a discount with most providers in Ontario, while no such discount applies in Quebec, where driver training is mandatory. Choosing a car with a calmer claims record, using the IBC ranking above, changes the quote before any negotiation starts.

Other levers sit inside the policy. Raising the deductible lowers the premium, because the deductible is the part of a loss the driver pays when a claim is made. Driving less can bring savings, since vehicle use affects the rate. Usage based insurance, where an app or device records driving activity, can personalize the price: Ratehub's sample profile showed a $662 annual difference, about 10.5%, from opting in, with the warning that poor driving data can push a price the other way. On an older car, dropping collision coverage is another saving route, while the legal liability protection for damage caused to others remains.

None of these levers turns a teen premium into an adult one. Together they decide whether the first year lands at the painful end of the range or the merely steep end.

Which route fits which household?

Work through four questions, in this order. Who is the registered owner of the car? If the answer is the teen, the route is an own policy, and the remaining work is shopping for it and checking for a multi-vehicle discount at the parent insurer. Where does the teen live? The listed route rests on the same address; a teen living away needs the insurer told, and the answer priced honestly. How much will the teen actually drive? Occasional trips in a parent car fit occasional driver listing, while the teen who is the main driver of a family car should expect primary driver pricing on that car.

What happens to the parent policy if the teen has an at fault accident? If the parent cannot absorb that increase, that risk belongs in the decision now, not after a claim.

One last check belongs to every route: a driver does not need a personal policy to drive someone else's car, as long as that car is already insured, and young drivers must carry the same required coverage as experienced drivers, including third party liability, accident benefits, and uninsured motorist coverage, with exact requirements varying by province. Get the household facts onto the policy first, then compare prices. A cheap quote built on the wrong facts is the most expensive outcome on the table.
