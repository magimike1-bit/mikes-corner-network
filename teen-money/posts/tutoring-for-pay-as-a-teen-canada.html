Tutoring for pay as a teen in Canada: rates, runbook, and the tax line

Earning · 6 min read

Yes, a strong student can charge for tutoring — and the honest starting rate is lower than the tutoring ads suggest. Student tutors in Canada charge about $10 to $20 an hour, while tutoring companies charge roughly $42 to $55 and certified teachers $57 or more (rate survey checked November 1, 2026). The gap is not an injustice to fix by pricing at $40. It is the market describing what each seller includes: a company screens its tutors, handles scheduling and billing, and replaces a tutor who quits. A teen selling directly offers none of that — and keeps every dollar instead of sharing it with an agency. Both facts belong in the price.

The rate on the poster is not the wage. A session carries unpaid time around it: preparing examples, travelling or setting up the call, messaging the parent, recording the payment. Labelled scenario, with the assumptions shown: a teen charges $20 for a one-hour session, spends 30 minutes on preparation and travel combined, and the effective rate is $20 divided by 1.5 hours — about $13.30 an hour. Two back-to-back sessions for siblings or neighbours on the same trip cut the travel cost per session and push the effective rate back toward the headline number.

That calculation is the pricing tool. A rate that looks generous at $20 can lose to a part-time job once prep and travel are counted, and a rate of $15 for a student next door — no travel, materials already owned — can beat both. Price the hour actually worked, not the hour on the clock.

The startup runbook, in order

  1. Pick one subject and one grade band. "Math, grades 7–10" is a sellable offer. "Homework help, anything" is not — parents pay for a specific weakness fixed.
  2. Prove the teaching before charging for it. Help a classmate, a sibling, or a younger student for free first. One learner whose marks improved is the entire early marketing file, and it confirms the work is tolerable before money is involved.
  3. Set the rate and the rules together. Rate, session length, where sessions happen, and a cancellation rule — stated before the first booking, in a message the parent keeps. Rules invented after a no-show protect nobody.
  4. Find the first two clients close to home. Neighbours, family friends, a school noticeboard where the school permits it. Close clients keep travel near zero while the routine is being learned.
  5. Run each session to a small structure. What was covered, what is still shaky, what to practise before next time — two lines sent to the parent after each session. That note is what separates a paid tutor from a helpful classmate in the parent's mind, and it is what gets the tutor recommended.
  6. Record every payment the day it lands. Date, client, amount, method. The notebook that holds session notes holds income too; tax season is built from it (see below).
  7. Review after the first month. Recalculate the effective hourly rate from the actual records — sessions, prep, travel, messages — and adjust the rate, the grade band, or the travel radius before adding more clients.

What success looks like: two or three steady clients, a rate that survives the effective-hourly calculation, and a parent who can describe exactly what improved.

Records and the tax line, without the scare stories

Tutoring income is business income, and it gets reported. Business income and expenses are reported on Form T2125, the statement of business or professional activities filed with the tax return. The CRA's guidance on that form also sets the expense rule: a reasonable expense incurred to earn the income can generally be deducted, and only the business part of any expense counts — personal spending stays out. For a teen tutor the expense list is short (printing, supplies, a transit pass portion if it is genuinely for sessions), which is exactly why the payment records matter more than the receipts.

On GST/HST, the line is far away and worth knowing precisely: a business whose taxable sales do not exceed $30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters is a small supplier and does not have to register for a GST/HST account. Crossing $30,000 inside a single calendar quarter ends that status immediately, with GST/HST charged on the sale that crosses the line. A teen tutor will almost certainly never approach the threshold — but knowing it exists replaces a vague worry with a number, and the payment notebook is what would prove which side of it the business sits on.

Who should not do this, and the kill criterion

Skip tutoring if the subject is a recent pass rather than a genuine strength — a tutor who is one chapter ahead of the student gets found out by the second test, and a reputation in a neighbourhood market is the whole business. Skip it, too, if evenings are already committed: tutoring clients buy the same after-school hours every week, and a tutor who cancels often is building the opposite of a reputation.

Kill criterion: after six weeks of paid sessions, calculate the effective hourly rate from the records. If it sits below what the same hours would earn at an available part-time job — or if there are still fewer than two paying clients — stop, reprice, or switch the offer (different grade band, online sessions, paired students) before investing more unpaid preparation time. Tutoring earns when it is steady and close. Chasing distant, irregular clients at a student rate is a part-time job with worse hours and no pay stub.
