Understanding sales tactics: clearance, bundling, and fake discounts aimed at teens

Money traps · 6 min read

A crossed-out price next to a lower one does not mean money was saved. It means a seller typed two numbers. The saving exists only if the higher number was ever real — and a surprising share of the time, it was not. That single misconception powers most of the tactics below, whether the pitch arrives as a clearance tag in a mall store, a countdown inside a game shop, or a discount link in a social feed. The good news: every tactic here fails under one of three quick tests, and none of the tests needs an app, an account, or more than a minute.

The tactic wall: what each one looks like

Clearance that never ends. A clearance tag implies a final markdown — stock being cleared out, gone when it is gone. Yet shoppers working with Consumers' Checkbook found items labelled "clearance" still available at the same price from the same store months later. A markdown that lasts for months is not a markdown. It is the regular price wearing a red tag.

The countdown. A timer ticking down beside a price is built to do one job: stop the buyer from comparing prices before the clock runs out. Fake deadlines and limited-time offers are used precisely because a shopper who feels rushed skips the two minutes of checking that would expose the deal. The pressure is the product.

The inflated "was" price. "Was $80, now $35" sounds like a 56% saving. If the item never actually sold at $80 anywhere, the real saving is zero and the discount is a story. The anchor number costs the seller nothing to print, which is exactly why it has to be checked rather than believed.

The bundle label. Two items shrink-wrapped together under a "deal" banner borrow credibility from each other. Bundling paired with a discount label can mislead buyers who are already prone to impulse buying — the label does the persuading before the arithmetic gets a chance. The next section does that arithmetic.

The too-good promo code. This one is not ordinary marketing; it is a safety problem. Promo codes circulated by unverified social pages can redirect to phishing or data-harvesting sites — pages built to collect personal details rather than sell anything. A discount that asks for unusual information before showing a price is not a discount. Close the tab.

The bundle trick, in actual numbers

One documented construction works like this: the seller doubles the regular price, then applies a "2 for the price of 1" label. The buyer sees a free item. The unit price — the only number that matters — never moves.

Worked example, with round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic easy to follow. A bag of chips normally sells for $3:

How it is soldSticker priceWhat you take homeReal price per bag
Regular single bag$31 bag$3
Price doubled, labelled "2 for the price of 1"$62 bags$3
A genuine 2-for-1 on the regular price$32 bags$1.50

Rows one and two cost exactly the same per bag. Only row three is a saving, and it is the row the label is imitating. The check takes seconds: divide what you pay by how many you get, then compare that figure with the ordinary single price. If the two figures match, the "deal" is a relabelling — and the second bag is not free, it is simply the second bag, bought at full price, whether it was wanted or not.

Three tests you can run in the store or the app

Run these in order. A deal that fails any one of them has told you enough.

  1. Refresh the timer. If a countdown is running, reload the page or leave and come back. A fake countdown timer often resets on reload, starting the same "last minutes" over again. A genuine offer does not need theatre: it has clear start and end dates. A timer that restarts is a timer that was never counting down to anything.
  2. Cross-check the "was" price. Search the same product at established retailers. An inflated original price falls apart here, because the cross-check shows what the item actually sells for elsewhere. If every other store sells it at the "sale" price, the sale price is the price.
  3. Do the unit-price division. For bundles, multi-buys, and clearance packs: total price divided by the number of items (or by weight or volume, when sizes differ). Compare the result with the regular single item. Lower per unit is a saving. Equal per unit is a label.

What success looks like: inside a minute, the offer has been sorted into one of two piles — the numbers survived all three tests, or one test exposed the trick and the decision made itself. Either way, the decision was made by arithmetic, not by a clock.

When a sale is worth trusting

Real sales exist, and skipping every one of them is its own waste of money. The signs worth trusting are boring, which is the point:

  • The offer states clear start and end dates instead of a looping timer.
  • The sale price is genuinely lower than the price at other established retailers — checked, not assumed.
  • The unit price, after the division in test three, is actually lower than the regular unit price.
  • The item was already on a want-list before the sale appeared. A real discount on an unwanted item is still money spent on an unwanted item.

None of this requires distrusting every store or skipping every promotion. It requires one habit: the seller supplies the claim, the buyer runs the tests. A teen who divides before buying will find that most "deals" quietly reclassify themselves — and the few that survive the tests are the ones actually worth the money.
