Avocado Toast, Costed: Cafe Price vs Home Price, With Receipts

Toast economics · 8 min read

Avocado toast is the most accused dish of the century. It has been blamed, in print and at family dinners, for a generation's housing difficulties — a charge usually delivered without a single price being checked. This study checks the prices. Every figure below was observed at a named retailer or on a named cafe menu on 18 October 2026, in Canadian dollars, and the arithmetic is shown, because an accusation this famous deserves the dignity of a receipt.

The existing coverage does not provide one. The top-ranked cost breakdowns build their tables from estimated ranges — cafe totals of $5.50 to $15.00 against home totals of $1.50 to $3.50, with no retailer named — and the best-known explainer of the housing charge works from an unsourced average avocado price of about $1.50 US. Estimates are not evidence. What follows is evidence, from Toronto, where the toast and the housing are both genuinely expensive and only one of them is toast's fault.

The home version, costed from actual shelves

Start with the avocado, because everything starts with the avocado. At Walmart Canada, a pack of five avocados was a flyer feature at $4.98 on the check date — $1.00 per fruit, which makes the standard half-avocado serving a $0.50 ingredient. At Real Canadian Superstore, a single avocado listed at $1.75, which makes the same half serving $0.88. The gap between those two numbers is the entire bulk-buying question, and it is worth 38 cents a slice.

The bread is a Farmhouse sourdough loaf, 675 g, listed at $4.49 — $0.67 per 100 g. Costed at that rate, one thick slice of roughly 50 g carries $0.34 of bread. The convenience alternative prices the slicing labour honestly: a pre-sliced sourdough loaf, 404 g at $3.50, runs $0.87 per 100 g, a premium of about 30 percent over the unsliced loaf for the same bread in thinner company. Even at the premium rate the slice costs $0.44, which changes no conclusion in this study and is recorded here because this study records everything. The upgrades price out the same way: a No Name large egg from a 12-pack at $3.93 costs $0.33, and a lemon at $1.00 yields roughly eight wedges at $0.13 each. Assembled, the home slice costs $0.84 in its basic form with the bulk-bought avocado, or $1.22 with the single-fruit avocado; fully loaded with egg and lemon it reaches $1.30 or $1.68 respectively. Every version of the home dish, fully dressed, costs less than the single avocado it is built on would cost a person who paid cafe prices for the fruit's company.

The cafe version, priced from actual menus

Three Toronto-area menus, checked the same day, bracket the market. At the bottom, YOU Made It Cafe prices avocado toast at $8.99: toasted sourdough, avocado spread, cherry tomatoes and julienne red onions with a balsamic glaze — a dressed plate, honestly specified. In the middle, Eggsmart's Toronto menu lists avocado toast at $16.00 in its Light & Right section. At the top, 7 West Cafe in Toronto lists avocado toast at $22.00 on a brunch menu served Saturday and Sunday from 10 to 3. These are menu prices before tax and tip, and they are real in the way the estimated ranges are not: a person can walk in and be charged exactly that.

For calibration, the American coverage that dominates search results lands in the same territory once the currency is admitted: avocado toast with two eggs at a breakfast chain near Philadelphia at about $14 US, a slice in Greenwich Village at around $16 US, and a home slice at around $2 US. The cafe markup over ingredients is not a Toronto invention. It is the standard economics of sitting down.

The annual arithmetic

A single slice proves little; habits are where money goes. Run the habit. A daily cafe avocado toast at the mid-range Eggsmart price is $16.00 × 365 = $5,840 a year. At the top of the checked range the habit becomes theatre: a daily 7 West brunch toast, if a person could even brunch daily, is $22.00 × 365 = $8,030 a year. The same daily toast made at home, loaded, from bulk-bought avocados, is $1.30 × 365 = $474.50 a year. The difference is $5,365.50 annually — real money, and also the price of 365 occasions on which someone else selected a ripe avocado, which anyone who has played the grocery-store ripeness lottery will concede is a service and not a scam.

The weekly version is the more honest comparison, because weekly is how brunch actually happens. One cafe toast a week at $16.00 is $832 a year; the home equivalent is $67.60; the gap is $764.40. That gap buys a respectable appliance, a weekend away, or — the comparison the original accusation insists on — approximately one five-hundredth of a Toronto house per year of abstinence. The toast was never the obstacle. The toast is, at worst, a rounding error with good public relations.

When the cafe price is honestly worth it

The arithmetic convicts the habit, not the dish. A cafe price buys things the home costing deliberately excludes: a ripe avocado chosen by someone whose job is choosing ripe avocados, a table, a plate that arrives warm, and the absence of a counter covered in avocado skin and sourdough crumbs. Paid occasionally, that is a fair trade openly made. Paid daily, it is a $5,365.50 annual subscription to not washing a knife. The decision framework is therefore short: if the occasion is the point, pay the menu price and enjoy it; if the toast is the point, the toast costs $1.30 and the toaster is already paid for.

FAQ

Why is cafe avocado toast so expensive if the ingredients cost $1.30? Because the menu price is not an ingredient price. On the evidence above, ingredients account for roughly a twelfth of a $16 plate; the remainder is the kitchen, the staff, the room and the ripe-avocado guarantee. Whether that remainder is worth it depends on the occasion, which is a judgement, not a costing error.

Do these prices still apply? They were checked on 18 October 2026 at the retailers and menus named, and grocery and menu prices drift — the avocado 5-pack was a flyer feature, which by definition ends. The method survives the prices: halve the fruit, cost the bread by weight, and compare against the menu in the window.
