Cinnamon Toast Three Ways: Sprinkle, Paste, and Broiler Glaze

Recipes · 7 min read

50 grams. That is the sugar in one documented cinnamon toast recipe — a quarter cup (50 g) of granulated sugar mixed with a single teaspoon of cinnamon for six slices. Another documented recipe, for just two slices, uses 3 teaspoons of sugar against 2 teaspoons of cinnamon: proportionally, a cinnamon load many times higher. Both recipes are presented as the classic. They cannot both be mild, and neither source mentions the other. Cinnamon toast has three documented methods and no agreed ratio, so this piece puts the methods and the ratios in one place and gives a rule for choosing.

The dish, up top

Cinnamon toast: buttered toast carrying cinnamon and sugar, eaten straight away while hot. Documented preparation times run about 10 to 12 minutes. Documented yields run from 2 slices to 6 slices (serving 2 to 3 people). Difficulty is low; the only step that punishes inattention is the optional broiler finish, where sugar moves from melted to burnt quickly.

The history is older than the breakfast table expects. As Joy of Baking recounts from Jane Grigson's English Food, a recipe for “Cinnamon Toafts” appears as far back as 1660, in The Accomplisht Cook: a mixture of cinnamon, sugar, and claret put on toast and warmed over the fire. The claret has retired. The structure — spiced sugar on toast, finished with heat — has not changed.

Two ratios that cannot both be mild

Per slice, the second recipe carries roughly six times the cinnamon of the first, while the first carries far more sugar per batch. Neither is wrong; they are different dishes sharing a name. The practical lesson is to decide which dish is wanted before mixing anything — a sweet glaze with warmth behind it, or a spice layer that happens to be sweetened — because adjusting “to taste” after the fact, which both sources suggest, only works once that starting direction is chosen.

The sprinkle method is the childhood version the paste recipe itself describes: bread popped in the toaster, butter spread on, cinnamon and sugar shaken onto the still-warm toast. Its weakness is distribution — a shaker decides where the cinnamon lands. The paste method solves distribution by mixing everything into the butter before the bread is involved, and it scales: the paste source notes a batch can be multiplied to fill a baking sheet. The glaze method keeps the sprinkle's speed and adds one controlled risk — a short, watched broiler finish that turns loose sugar into a melted, bubbling layer.

The paste procedure, step by step

Quantities below are the paste source's documented recipe for 2 slices. Scale the whole mixture, not individual ingredients, for a larger batch.

  • 2 slices white bread
  • 4 teaspoons butter, softened
  • 3 teaspoons white granulated sugar
  • 2 teaspoons ground cinnamon
  • 1/8 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • Optional: a pinch of salt, especially with unsalted butter
  1. Heat the oven to 350°F (175°C). Soften the butter until it spreads easily.
  2. Mix the paste. Combine butter, sugar, cinnamon, vanilla, and salt if using, in a small bowl, until the cinnamon is evenly distributed — no dry pockets.
  3. Spread edge to edge. Cover each slice completely with the mixture, butter-side up on a baking pan. Bare corners dry out in the bake; covered corners do not.
  4. Bake for 10 minutes. The bread toasts in the oven while the paste sets into the surface.
  5. Broil for 1 minute, watched. Broil until the mixture is bubbling. The source's warning is explicit: do not walk away — this happens very quickly.
  6. Serve immediately. The published nutrition for this version is 182 kcal per serving, as listed by the source.

The sprinkle and glaze procedures

For the sprinkle and glaze methods, the documented mixture is 1/4 cup (50 g) sugar to 1 teaspoon cinnamon, for 6 slices of white or whole wheat bread, with butter at room temperature. Mix the sugar and cinnamon in a small bowl first — separate shakers cannot produce an even ratio. Toast the bread until golden brown on both sides, under a broiler, on a grill, or in a toaster oven. Butter one side while the toast is hot, carrying the butter right up to the edges of the crust, and sprinkle the cinnamon sugar generously over it.

For the plain sprinkle version, that is the finish: cut the slices in half and eat straight away. For the glaze version, return the sugared toast under the broiler until the sugar has melted and starts to bubble, forming a glaze, then remove it at once. The difference between the two finishes is texture, not flavour — loose crystals that crunch against soft butter, against a thin set layer that cracks.

Choosing a method in under a minute

One or two slices and no patience: sprinkle. A tray for several people: paste — it is the only documented method designed around a baking sheet, and its mixing step is also its quality control. A small batch where the top matters most: glaze, provided someone is willing to stand at the oven for the final minute. Storage needs no procedure of its own in the sources: both documented versions are written to be eaten straight away, hot, and the glaze version's own source notes that cooled leftovers turn soft and chewy — a different pleasure, recorded as an observation, not a storage instruction.

What success looks like

The cinnamon sugar reaches the edges, with no bare corners and no dry cinnamon pockets. The paste version bubbles evenly across the surface rather than in one scorched patch. The glaze version shows melted, bubbling sugar across the whole slice before it leaves the broiler — and leaves the broiler at that moment, not a minute later. Underneath, the toast stays toast: crisp at the surface, still recognisably bread in the middle, and eaten while that contrast lasts.

— June Callahan, Food Writer
