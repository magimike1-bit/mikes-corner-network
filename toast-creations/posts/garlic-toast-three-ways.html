Garlic Toast Three Ways: Butter, Olive Oil, and Cheese-Crusted

Bake-offs · 6 min read

1/2 cup (125 mL) of fat, 4 cloves of garlic, an oven at 400°F (200°C), 5 minutes plus 1 to 2 minutes under the broiler. Those are the documented numbers behind the butter version of garlic toast — and they are the control conditions for a comparison the recipe results never actually run: butter, olive oil, and cheese-crusted, side by side, on the same bread, at the same temperature.

The facts line: three versions, one oven setting · base quantity: 1/2 cup (125 mL) softened butter or olive oil with 4 cloves of garlic · breads that work: French, Italian, sourdough, or Texas toast · difficulty: easy; the discipline is in the comparison.

The test setup: one bread, three fats, one oven

A bake-off only means something if the variables behave. Hold the bread constant — pick one loaf from the documented list: French, Italian, sourdough, or Texas toast. Hold the oven constant at 400°F (200°C). Hold the garlic quantity constant at 4 cloves against 1/2 cup (125 mL) of fat. Then change exactly one thing at a time: the fat itself.

One timing in the sources breaks ranks and deserves a flag before the cooking starts. An olive-oil and garlic-salt version is documented at the same 400°F (200°C) but for 20 minutes — four times the 5-minute base timing, with no other source matching it. Treat that figure as an outlier, not a third option. Garlic toast held in a hot oven for 20 minutes is a different, darker product, and the 5-minute-plus-broiler procedure below is the one the remaining sources support.

Butter: softened, never melted

The butter guidance is unusually specific: use softened butter, not melted. Softened butter spreads as a paste, carrying the garlic with it in an even layer that stays where it was put. Melted butter runs, pools, and soaks in unevenly, so one bite is rich and the next is bare bread. The instruction is about distribution, not flavour loyalty.

  1. Soften, do not melt. Start with 1/2 cup (125 mL) of softened butter and 4 cloves of garlic, minced.
  2. Mix the garlic through the butter until the paste looks uniform — no garlic clusters hiding in one corner.
  3. Spread edge to edge on slices of French, Italian, sourdough, or Texas toast bread.
  4. Bake at 400°F (200°C) for 5 minutes.
  5. Broil for 1 to 2 minutes at the end, watching closely, until the surface deepens and crisps.

Success check: an even, golden surface with no pale, unbuttered patches and no dark, bitter spots. The garlic should read as part of the butter layer, not as scattered debris on top of it.

Olive oil: the crisp-edged version

Olive oil is the documented swap-in for the butter: the same 1/2 cup (125 mL) measure, the same garlic, the same breads. The handling changes because the fat changes state. Oil brushes or drizzles rather than spreads, so coverage is the cook's responsibility in a way a butter paste handles automatically.

  1. Combine 1/2 cup (125 mL) of olive oil with 4 minced cloves of garlic.
  2. Brush the mixture across each slice, working to the edges, and turn the brush back for any dry patches.
  3. Bake at 400°F (200°C) for 5 minutes, then broil 1 to 2 minutes, matching the butter version so the comparison stays honest.

The garlic-salt variant belongs to this branch of the family: olive oil paired with garlic salt instead of fresh cloves. Its flavour logic is sound — seasoned oil, evenly brushed — even though its documented 20-minute timing is the outlier flagged above. Borrow the pairing if it appeals; keep the shorter timing.

Cheese-crusted: the fat is the topping

The third way replaces the fat question entirely: cheese works as the fat alternative. Instead of a garlic fat spread under nothing, the cheese layer sits on the slice as both richness and crust, and the broiler finish stops being a flourish and becomes the step that defines the result.

  1. Start from the same slice — same bread, same thickness, so the crust is the only new variable.
  2. Apply the garlic first, in a light butter or oil base, so the cheese seals flavour in rather than replacing it.
  3. Cover with cheese as the top layer, edge to edge.
  4. Bake at 400°F (200°C), then give the slice its 1 to 2 minutes under the broiler until the cheese crust bubbles and colours.

Success check: the cheese reads as a crust, not a blanket — coloured in spots, crisp at the edges, with the toast still audible underneath when the slice is tapped.

The honest summary of the garlic variable: fresh minced cloves carry the only fully documented dose in the research, garlic salt carries a documented pairing, and the remaining forms are choices to make by coverage — spread thin, spread even, and let the fat do the carrying.

The verdict, by bread and by meal

Butter wins on control. The softened paste spreads evenly, holds the garlic in place, and produces the most predictable result across all four documented breads — French, Italian, sourdough, and Texas toast. For a first batch, or a batch that has to match the last batch, butter is the reliable hand.

Olive oil wins on edges. A brushed oil layer favours crisp perimeters and a lighter centre, and it is the version with a documented seasoned shortcut in the garlic-salt pairing. Sourdough, with a crust that already means business, suits it particularly well as a matter of structure: a sturdy slice under a light, crisping fat.

Cheese-crusted wins when garlic toast is the dish rather than the side. It is the richest of the three, the least dependent on perfect spreading, and the one where the broiler minutes matter most. Serve it where a plain slice would look underdressed — alongside soup it has to hold its own against, or as the base for a topping that needs a crust to sit on.

Three ways, one oven, no loser. The argument was never really about garlic. It was about what carries it.

— June Callahan, Food Writer
