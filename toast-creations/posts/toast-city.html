Building a Toast City

Urban planning · 6 min read

Every child who has ever arranged food on a plate understands the foundational principle of urban planning: things look better when they're in the right place. Building a toast city is simply this instinct, scaled up and given a zoning board. What follows is the definitive municipal planning guide for a metropolis with a population of zero and a land area of one plate.

Site selection and zoning

Begin with a large, flat plate — the bedrock of your civilization. A dinner plate supports a modest town; a serving platter accommodates a proper city; a baking sheet is, frankly, showing off.

Every functional toast city needs districts. We recommend:

The Breakfast District (downtown core): dense, high-rise construction of stacked slices, close to the coffee infrastructure. Land values are highest here and the rents are a tragedy.

The Residential Crust Zone (suburbs): single slices laid flat in neat rows, each with a small buttered lawn. Quiet. Respectable. Slightly dull, which is the whole point of suburbs.

The Industrial Crumb Belt (outskirts): where the broken pieces go. Nobody visits the Industrial Crumb Belt, but the city cannot function without it, which makes it the most honest district of all.

The Dessert District (entertainment quarter): jam-glazed structures, cinnamon-sugar rooftops. Lovely to visit, ruinous to live in, like every entertainment district ever planned.

Infrastructure: roads, transit, and utilities

A city is only as good as its infrastructure, and toast infrastructure is where the true planner shines. Butter roads — thin, carefully spread lanes of softened butter — connect the districts and glisten invitingly in the morning light. Jam canals serve as the city's waterways, though they are sticky in a way that suggests the water department is underfunded.

Public transit in a toast city is provided by crumb buses: individual crumbs arranged in convoy formation along the butter roads, departing whenever they feel like it, which is to say the schedule is aspirational. The commuters have no complaints. There are no commuters.

Utilities deserve a mention. The city's power grid is the ambient warmth of the toaster, long since disconnected — a decommissioned power station that the locals speak of with reverence. Street lighting is provided by the kitchen ceiling fixture, an arrangement the residents accept without question.

Municipal politics

No city is complete without governance, and toast cities are no exception. The mayor is elected by acclamation and serves for life, because the mayor is a slice of rye standing upright in the town square and nobody has the heart to vote it out. City council meetings are held whenever someone walks past the plate and pauses. Attendance is excellent. Participation is zero.

The great political debate of every toast city is the same: preservation versus appetite. The preservationists argue the city should stand forever as a monument to human creativity. The appetite faction argues it is, at the end of the day, toast. History records that the appetite faction wins every election, usually around 8:15 in the morning.

Disaster preparedness

The responsible planner prepares for catastrophe. The primary natural disaster in a toast city is the Table Bump — a seismic event of devastating magnitude that levels entire districts in a fraction of a second. Building codes require all structures to be rated for Table Bumps up to "someone reaching for the coffee," but enforcement is lax and everyone knows it.

The secondary threat is humidity, the slow creeping decay that turns crisp infrastructure soggy over hours. There is no defense against humidity except speed: build fast, photograph faster, eat fastest.

The legacy question

All toast cities are temporary. This is not a flaw in the planning; it is the entire philosophy. Real cities take centuries to build and are never finished. Your toast city takes eleven minutes to build and is finished the moment you decide it is. In an age of endless construction projects and eternal delays, there is something quietly radical about a city that is complete, perfect, and gone before lunch.
