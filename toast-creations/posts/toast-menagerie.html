A Toast Menagerie

Zoology · 5 min read

Zoos exist to bring the wonders of the animal kingdom to people who cannot visit the savanna. A toast menagerie exists to bring the wonders of the animal kingdom to people who cannot be bothered to leave the kitchen. It is the world's least necessary zoo, and it is open whenever the toaster is warm.

The bear

The bear is the entry-level toast animal and the gateway species of the entire menagerie. Take one round slice of bread — a bagel slice or a carefully trimmed sandwich round — and give it two ears: small crust circles cut from a second slice. The ears attach with a dab of peanut butter, which is both adhesive and, conveniently, the bear's brown coloring.

The face is spread-work: a lighter circle of cream cheese for the muzzle, two raisin eyes, a chocolate-chip nose. Stand back. You have made a bear. It is not a good bear. It is a bear in the same way a child's drawing of a bear is a bear — which is to say, completely, if you squint and you love it.

The owl

The owl is the bear's more distinguished cousin. Begin with an oval slice — trim the corners off a square slice if you must, though the owl will judge you for it. The defining feature is the eyes: two large circles of cream cheese or banana slice, each with a raisin pupil, set wide and staring. The owl's expression should communicate quiet disappointment. This happens naturally if your toast work is bad enough.

Feather texture is achieved with the edge of a fork dragged through a thin layer of spread — a technique the old masters called "having a fork." The beak is a single triangle of crust, or a small piece of cheese if the owl is feeling fancy. Owls are nocturnal; serve at breakfast for maximum irony.

The fish

The fish is the minimalist's animal. One oval slice, pointed at one end by trimming — the body. A triangle of crust at the back — the tail. One raisin eye. Done. The fish requires perhaps ninety seconds of effort and delivers a full ninety seconds of satisfaction, a ratio unmatched in the arts.

Advanced practitioners add scales: overlapping circles of thin spread, or a neat grid of sesame seeds pressed into a buttered surface. The sesame-seed fish is widely considered the Fabergé egg of the toast menagerie — beautiful, pointless, and produced by someone with too much time and not enough hobbies.

The rabbit

The rabbit is the boss fight of beginner toast animals. The ears are long strips of crust, and crust is brittle, which means the rabbit has a failure rate of roughly one ear in three. This is not a defect in your technique. Rabbits are simply like this. Ask anyone who has owned one.

The body is a round slice, the head a smaller round, the tail a single marshmallow or a heroic blob of cream cheese. The rabbit's whiskers are the finest detail work in the menagerie — thin lines of jam drawn with a toothpick — and they will be the first thing to go wrong. Accept this. The rabbit accepts this.

Techniques of the trade

Three methods cover nearly every animal in the menagerie. Cookie cutters are the honest approach: press, lift, done. They produce clean shapes and clean consciences. Subtractive sculpting — the careful nibbling of edges into shape — is the romantic approach, and also the approach that ends with you eating half the animal before it is finished. Spread work, the application of peanut butter, jam, and cream cheese as color and texture, is where the real artistry lives, because it is the only method that cannot be blamed on the tools.

Conservation notes

The toast menagerie faces the same conservation crisis as the toast city: everything is temporary, and the primary predator is whoever made it. There are no breeding programs. There are no rewilding initiatives. When the bear is gone, the bear is gone, and a new bear must be toasted from scratch. This is the circle of life, compressed into a single breakfast and seasoned with cinnamon.
