AI Slop Floods the Feed: A Field Guide to the Beige Internet

Roast · 7 min read

Every era of the internet gets the wallpaper it deserves, and this era's wallpaper is beige. Scroll any feed and the texture asserts itself within a minute: a video of something almost happening, an image of someone almost real, a post that almost says something, captioned with total confidence. This is slop — and it is no longer a slang term muttered by the annoyed. It is a dictionary-certified condition of the feed, with a word-of-the-year crown to prove it. This roast is not about whether machines may make things; some machine-made things are excellent, and some human-made things are slop with a pulse. It is about the flood itself: content produced in quantity, on purpose, to occupy space — and about the only filter that will ever be installed on it, which is a reader's thumb.

The dictionary has entered the chat

In December 2025, Merriam-Webster named "slop" its Word of the Year, defining it as "digital content of low quality that is produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence." The word is centuries older than the problem — it meant soft mud in the 1700s, food waste in the 1800s, and a product of little or no value after that — which makes its coronation feel less like an invention and more like a homecoming. The dictionary's own flood report for 2025 lists "absurd videos, off-kilter advertising images, cheesy propaganda, fake news that looks pretty real, junky AI-written books… and lots of talking cats," concluding, with admirable restraint, that people found it annoying, and people ate it up. When the referees of the language stop defining a phenomenon and start sighing about it, the phenomenon has won the volume war.

The term's recent history is short and telling. "Slop" in its AI sense circulated in online communities from 2022, was championed into mainstream discourse by programmer Simon Willison in May 2024, and within eighteen months had gone from insider shorthand to the dictionary's headline — a promotion speed that says more about the feed than about the word. Slop now proliferates across YouTube, TikTok, Facebook, Instagram, and X: generic posts, stock-like images, scripted videos, and automated articles, all sharing one production philosophy — quantity first, and let the algorithm sort it out. The algorithm, it must be said, is not sorting it out. The algorithm is serving it, chilled, in a beige bowl.

Anatomy of the beige

Glossaries that define slop converge on the same five tells, and they are worth naming precisely, because "I know it when I see it" is how the feed wins. Slop is vague and generalised — it could be about anything, so it is about nothing. It is repetitively structured, with rhythmic phrasing and uniform sentence lengths that produce a distinctly robotic calm. It carries no original insight, regurgitating what already exists rather than adding a perspective. Its tone is a neutral corporate smoothness with no distinctive voice and no opinion anyone could disagree with. And its signature move is unearned profundity: the grave transition, the meaningful pause, the "and that's why it matters" — bolted onto content that has not earned a "that," let alone a "why."

The advertising world, which funds much of the flood, has produced the sharpest one-line test. A creative-strategy head quoted in industry coverage puts it this way: slop "can look glossy and well-structured, but the giveaway is how quickly it collapses under scrutiny" — generic, bland, and interchangeable, language that could belong to any brand, anywhere. And the downstream effect lands on readers, not just brands: the flood, in the same assessment, "trains audiences to scroll faster, to trust less, and to expect the worst." That is the beige internet's real product. Not the posts. The reflex.

The scroll-or-stop triage

Detection tools exist, but they live in browser tabs, not in the feed, and the feed is where the two seconds happen. So the working filter is a triage the thumb can run at scrolling speed:

  1. The specificity sniff (one second). Glance for a checkable detail — a name, a date, a number, a place. None present? The post is asking for attention without collateral. Keep scrolling.
  2. The scrutiny squeeze (two seconds). If it looked glossy, reread the first two sentences. Slop collapses under exactly this pressure; a real point survives being read twice and usually gets better.
  3. The substitution test (one second). Could this post appear, word for word, on a thousand other accounts about a thousand other topics? Interchangeable means industrial. Industrial means it was made for the slot, not for a reader.
  4. Stop only for friction. A disagreement, a surprise, a specific fact worth stealing, a voice with a pulse — friction is the texture slop cannot fake, because friction requires a mind on the other end with something at stake.

Run honestly, the triage is not really about catching machines. It catches the intent — content produced in quantity, with minimal human oversight, designed to harvest a pause rather than reward one. A human can produce that by hand and a machine can be wielded with care; the beige is a production philosophy, not a species. Which is the roast's last concession, and its point: the flood will not be drained by better detectors, because the flood is profitable exactly as it is. It will be drained, if at all, one unrewarded post at a time — by thumbs that learned to charge for their attention. The dictionary has done its part. The word exists. The rest is scrolling.

FAQ

What does "slop" officially mean? Merriam-Webster, which named it the 2025 Word of the Year: "digital content of low quality that is produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence."

Is all AI-generated content slop? No — the defining traits are low quality, mass production, and minimal human oversight, not the tool. Carefully made work with specifics and a voice fails every tell in the table above.

Why does slop matter if it is easy to scroll past? Because the cost is the reflex it trains: audiences taught by volume to scroll faster, trust less, and expect the worst — a tax on every genuine post that follows.
