De-Influencing Is Still Influencing: A Roast of the Anti-Haul

Satire · 7 min read

The most successful sales movement of the decade arrived wearing a disguise so clever that the disguise became the brand: it told everyone to stop buying things. Not quietly, in a diary, where anti-shopping advice traditionally lives. Loudly, on the same apps, in the same format, with the same ring light, under a hashtag. De-influencing had arrived, and it was, by every measurable standard, influencing beautifully.

This roast is not an accusation of hypocrisy aimed at any individual creator, plenty of whom mean every frugal word. The target is the format paradox itself: a rebellion against the haul that still needs the haul as its opening act, its villain, and its search ranking.

A brief history of saying no, loudly

The first visible wave of de-influencing is generally placed in January 2023, when TikTok videos began discouraging purchases of popular products, mainly in beauty and fashion. The anti-haul idea was older, a direct precursor in which creators used video platforms to argue against consumption, but 2023 gave the argument a name, a tag, and a growth chart. By September 2025, data cited in reference coverage counted roughly 93,900 TikTok posts under the de-influencing hashtag. Saying no had become a category, and categories on the feed behave like categories anywhere: they compete, they trend, and they develop house styles.

The house style settled quickly. A creator faces the camera with the energy of an intervention. A famous product is named. The verdict lands: do not buy it, it is not worth it, save the money. The comments fill with gratitude, relief, and the particular joy of being talked out of a checkout at the last responsible moment. It is genuinely useful television. It is also, structurally, a review with the polarity reversed, which means it inherits the review entire machinery: authority, taste, audience trust, and a creator whose recommendations now carry weight precisely because some of them say no.

Trust, on the feed, is a currency that spends in both directions. That is the paradox in its purest form. The de-influencer who convinces thousands of people not to buy a serum has demonstrated the exact skill the serum brand was renting from somebody else last quarter.

The swap: every no with a smaller yes attached

If the pure refusal were the whole format, the paradox would stay tidy. Enter the swap. Commentary on the movement has noted that de-influencing often trades pricey picks for budget-friendly alternatives, and still leans on personal branding and algorithmic boosts like any other genre. The expensive moisturiser is out; the humble one is in, held to the camera with a link-shaped glow. The spending did not leave the video. It changed outfits and got a round of applause for humility.

The swap is not automatically dishonest. Recommending the cheaper adequate thing over the famous expensive thing is real consumer help, the kind friends have given each other forever. The comedy is in the framing. A video that opens as a jailbreak from consumption and closes as a smaller, friendlier shopping list has not escaped the mall. It has found the outlet section and declared it a philosophy.

The swap also explains why brands adjusted so calmly. A movement that ends in alternative purchases is a movement marketing departments can work with, and work with it they did, because the anti-haul audience is, from a certain spreadsheet angle, simply a haul audience that prefers its recommendations with a conscience garnish. The rebellion was focus-grouped by its own comment section: more thrift, same links, better story.

Underconsumption core and the proudly boring shelf

In the summer of 2024, the sequel arrived, and sequels in this universe always get a core suffix. Underconsumption core is a TikTok trend first noted in 2024, presenting anti-haul style content that emphasises using, reusing, repairing, and repurposing existing possessions instead of buying new ones. Reference coverage describes it as an evolution of de-influencing, distinct from strict minimalism because it celebrates lived-in utility rather than an empty, curated aesthetic. The hero images are anti-haul catnip: the same sneakers worn every day for two years, walls left blank while decor is found secondhand slowly, a shelf that looks like a shelf instead of a showroom.

As values, all of this is admirable, and the roast pauses to say so without a punchline attached. Using things until they are finished is how objects were treated for most of human history; it only looks radical next to a haul. The comedy is not in the worn sneakers. The comedy is in the worn sneakers needing a suffix, a hashtag, and a content calendar to be allowed to exist. Ordinary thrift had to rebrand as a core before the feed would seat it.

And once seated, the proudly boring shelf faces the genre oldest temptation: performance. A blank wall filmed once is a choice. A blank wall filmed weekly, with updates on its continued blankness, is a series, and series need arcs. The arc that pays is usually a purchase. Somewhere around episode nine, the wall tends to meet a tasteful secondhand lamp, and the lamp, whatever its merits, is a finale the algorithm understands.

Read the last row slowly, because it is the entire roast in one line. The anti-haul best episodes genuinely break the pattern, ending with nothing sold at all. Its average episodes merely renegotiate the basket. The genre is healthiest exactly where it is least profitable, which is a sentence that could describe most of the internet wearing different shoes.

The nothing-was-sold test: a decision framework

For viewers deciding whether a piece of anti-consumer content is guidance or marketing in a hair shirt, four checks do most of the work. They take less than a minute, which is still faster than the average haul.

  1. Follow the ending. Did the video close with a product, a link, or a discount code? A refusal with a checkout attached is a redirect, not an exit.
  2. Count the swaps. One sensible cheaper alternative is advice. A full replacement routine is a haul travelling under an assumed name.
  3. Check the hero item age. Genuine use-it-up content shows wear: scuffs, refills, repairs. A pristine prop performing poverty is set design.
  4. Ask what grew. If the only measurable growth is the creator audience and sponsorship rate, the movement being built may be a media business. That can coexist with good advice, but it should change how literally the advice is taken.

Why no fix procedure here? There is no broken setting to repair; the anti-haul is a format to read critically, not a device to reset. The test above is the practical tool for this topic: it separates the sermon from the storefront without needing to doubt anyone sincerity in public.

FAQ

What is de-influencing? A social media movement, visible from January 2023, in which creators discourage followers from buying popular or hyped products, often in beauty and fashion.

What is underconsumption core? A TikTok trend first noted in 2024 showing anti-haul content built on using, reusing, repairing, and repurposing what people already own.

Is de-influencing fake? Not as a whole. Its best content saves people real money. The roast targets the format drift by which refusal content quietly becomes recommendation content with better public relations.

The anti-haul deserves its audience, and also its roast, because a movement strong enough to survive being teased is stronger than one that needs reverence. Keep the worn sneakers. Keep the blank walls. Just notice, kindly, when the sermon ends and the shop opens, because the most honest de-influencing video ever posted is the one nobody monetised, and by definition, almost nobody saw it.
