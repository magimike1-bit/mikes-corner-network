Everyone Is a Coach Now: A Roast of the Coach for Everything

Roast · 8 min read

There is a new unit of measurement for modern adulthood: how many coaches a person has. Not employs — has, the way one has a dentist or a landlord. A coach for the career, a coach for the money, a coach for the mindset, a coach for the parenting, and — the era's signature move — a coach for the coaching business. Everyone is a coach now, and everyone else is a client-in-waiting. A fair roast checks the numbers first, and here the numbers do something wonderful: they prove the flood using the flood's own paperwork. The coaching industry counts itself, proudly and precisely, and its official census is the funniest document in the whole trend.

The census: the flood, counted by the flooded

Start with the International Coaching Federation's 2025 Global Coaching Study, the industry's own count, produced with PwC from a survey of more than 10,000 coaches. It reports a record 122,974 coach practitioners worldwide, up 15% from 2023, and industry revenue that has soared to $5.34 billion. Growth of 54% since 2019, by the statistics roundups' arithmetic. Impressive figures — and here is the part the launch posts quote quietly: that census counts the certified. Industry statisticians note plainly that the 122,974 figure undercounts the total, because many coaches practise without any formal certification at all. The official number is the floor, not the flood. One insider critique estimates four million people worldwide now call themselves coaches — weekend-certified practitioners, influencers with a course, and everyone in between. Four million. There are not four million plumbers in the United States — one coaching-defence blog, arguing the field is not saturated, helpfully lists 537,211 American plumbers as a comparison point. The coach population may outnumber the people who fix the pipes by an order of magnitude, and the pipes, at least, have a licensing exam.

That licensing gap is the roast's load-bearing fact. As one LinkedIn critique of the profession puts it, because the industry is largely unregulated, the title gets applied to almost everything, from strategic mentoring to basic line management. There is no gate. A statistics roundup aimed at aspiring coaches states it as a market condition: the low barrier to entry creates increased competition with no gatekeeping, and because the market is unregulated, inconsistency in quality is a real issue. In other words, the word "coach" currently certifies exactly one thing — that someone typed it into a bio.

A coach for every friction point: the taxonomy

The second act of the trend is not the volume; it is the coverage. One clinic's account of coaching's expansion notes, without apparent irony, that there is now a professional coach for nearly every major friction point in modern life. The taxonomy deserves a table, because a list this complete is its own punchline:

Friction pointThe coach for thatThe tell
The jobExecutive, leadership and career coachesThe respectable ancestors — corporate budgets, actual training, the original species

The vibeMindset coaches, feminine-energy experts, relationship strategists, healing mentorsTitles one opinion piece describes as sounding professional while backed by nothing — no supervision, no ethics framework, no responsibility when things go wrong

The pricing is as sprawling as the niches — the same clinic account calls it a bit of a Wild West. At the shallow end, digital platforms offer texting a professional for $50 a week. Entry-level life and fitness coaches typically charge $75 to $200 per session; mid-level certified coaches run $200 to $400 per hour; executive coaches command $500 to $1,000 per hour, with corporate engagements reaching five or six figures. Notice what the ladder actually measures: not results, which are nowhere in the price list, but proximity to a corporate expense account. And notice the business model underneath, named by the insider critique with unusual honesty: most coaches get paid by the session, not results, so the standard model is built around time, not outcomes — recurring sessions, open-ended timelines, and no inherent incentive to reach a finish line, because a finish line ends the billing relationship. The client who gets fixed is a churn statistic. The client who almost gets fixed is a subscription.

The pyramid eats itself: coaches coaching coaches

Every mature trend develops a meta-layer, and coaching's is perfect: an estimated share of those four million coaches are, in the critique's phrase, coaches coaching coaches to coach coaches. The feed's newest native species is the twenty-something creator an opinion writer describes from a single afternoon's scrolling — ten different people mentoring ten completely different ways to live, many with zero training in the subjects they lecture on, armed with good production, punchy captions, and job titles invented last week. The writer's diagnosis is the sharpest line in the whole debate: one person's very specific life story gets repackaged as a universal rulebook, and the algorithm loves confident absolutes, so the rulebook outranks the résumé. The defence, to be fair, exists and is not nothing: coaching's own recruiters argue saturation is a myth — that 34,200 certified coaches in North America is a small profession next to 202,536 dentists in the United States, and that a crowded field proves demand. Both things can be true, and the reconciliation is the roast: the certified profession is a modest, growing trade, while the word has escaped the profession entirely and now lives wild in the feed, where anyone can wear it and the census cannot follow.

Coach or costume: the vetting test

So the practical question for anyone being sold transformation: which of the four million is standing in front of you? The sources assemble into a test sharper than either the scolding or the sales pitch:

QuestionCoachCostume
What certifies the title?A named credential from a body with standards — the ICF census exists precisely because certification is countableThe title itself, self-issued, in a field the industry admits is unregulated
How does the money work?A defined engagement with an outcome and an endOpen-ended sessions billed by time — the model insiders flag as rewarding slow progress
What is the expertise?A specific friction point they have demonstrably workedA personal story repackaged as a universal rulebook, with confident absolutes for every life
Who are their clients?People with the problem the coach solvesOther aspiring coaches — the recursion is the business

Pass the left column and the roast withdraws its objection: a certified practitioner, hired for a defined outcome, in a $5.34 billion industry, is a professional doing a job. Fail it, and what is being purchased is not coaching but proximity to confidence — the costume, the mug, the moody background. The trend will keep minting coaches either way; the census only counts the ones who filed the paperwork. Everyone is a coach now. The only say anyone gets is whether theirs is one of the 122,974 — or one of the millions the counting gave up on.

FAQ

How many coaches are there, really? The ICF's 2025 Global Coaching Study counts 122,974 certified practitioners worldwide and $5.34 billion in revenue — and industry statisticians note that figure undercounts, since many coaches practise uncertified. One insider estimate puts four million people worldwide calling themselves coaches.

Is coaching regulated? No — the industry's own commentators describe it as largely unregulated, with the title applicable to almost anything and no gatekeeping on entry. Certification exists (the ICF is the big counter), but nothing requires it.

What does a coach cost? Published ranges run from $50 a week for text-based platforms, through $75–$200 per session at entry level and $200–$400 per hour mid-level, up to $500–$1,000 per hour for executive coaches.

So should nobody hire a coach? The roast targets the word, not the profession: certified coaches with a defined outcome and a finish line are the counted minority doing real work. The test above is how to tell them from the costume.
