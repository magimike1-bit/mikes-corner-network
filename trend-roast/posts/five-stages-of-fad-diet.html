The Five Stages of a Fad Diet (a.k.a. the Villain-Food Rotation)

Satire · 6 min read

Satire only — this is comedy about a cultural phenomenon, not health advice. Roast the trend, spare the dieter.

Every decade elects a dietary villain. The 1980s pointed at fat — federal guidelines said cut it, and a thousand reduced-fat products were born. The 1990s kept it fat while Atkins revived and carb-counting became a hobby. The 2000s went full Atkins: Atkins, then the Zone, then South Beach, appearing in succession like boy bands. The 2010s voted for gluten; the early 2020s nominated the carnivore diet. The ballot never closes; it just gets recycled.

This is not a conspiracy; it is a business model. Roughly 14 to 15 percent of Americans say they've used a fad diet for short-term weight loss, and the industry built on that appetite is worth about $35 billion a year in the US, with more than 1,500 diet books published annually. When the market is that large and the product keeps failing, the product is not the diet. The product is the cycle.

The villain-food rotation

The rotation never produces a new mechanism, only a new villain. The 1980s pointed at fat, saturated fat, and cholesterol; the early 2000s at carbohydrates; the 2010s at gluten — a fad-diet framing for anyone without celiac disease or a genuine allergy; the early 2020s at nearly everything except meat. Same movie, new bad guy — the only thing that reliably changes is which grocery aisle gets a new religion.

The opening act is genuinely exciting: in one classic analysis, dieters lost five to ten percent of their weight in the first six months. Then, per the UCLA team that analyzed 31 long-term studies: "Diets do not lead to sustained weight loss or health benefits for the majority of people." About two-thirds regained it all — plus more — within four to five years; roughly 80 percent of big losers don't keep it off for twelve months.

The diet doesn't fail the dieter. The dieter is the recurring customer.

The Celebrity Vessel is a documented hallmark: commercial fad diets lean on "gurus" — celebrities or celebrity doctors who profit from branded products, books, and speaking engagements, selling exaggerated claims about a food's benefits or harms. The diet is the marketing; the merchandise is the business.

The monetization machine

Here is what never appears on the meal plan: the cycle is the customer-acquisition engine. The before-and-after photo is the advertisement. The 30-day program is the funnel. The supplement is the recurring revenue. When a meta-analysis shows dieters regain more than half within two years, that's not a product recall — it's a re-engagement opportunity. A $35-billion industry can afford the failure rate, because the failure rate is the plan.

In 1997 the American Heart Association formally declared war on fad diets to warn the public about misleading weight-loss claims. The fad diets noticed, said "thank you for the publicity," and kept the ballroom dancing.

The quiet funeral

Every fad diet dies the same way: not with a scandal, but with a clearance rack. The cookbooks migrate to the donation bin. The branded powders go chalky in the back of the cabinet, next to the spiralizer and the juicer from the last rotation. The app stops sending notifications, which is how you know it has given up on you, too.

The funeral is private — no press release, no livestream. But the grief is measurable: the UCLA analysis found two-thirds of dieters regained everything, plus more. The villain food gets exonerated, the guru moves on to a new villain, and the rotation spins again, smooth as a turntable — cicadas with a marketing department.

Is it a fad diet? Ask these five questions

  1. Does it name a single villain food? One ingredient blamed for everything is a casting decision, not a diagnosis.
  2. Does it promise results in 30 days or less? Biology does not run on billing cycles.
  3. Does the website have a store tab? When the plan needs a powder, the plan is the powder.
  4. Is the founder's main credential a before-and-after photo? Admire the lighting. Question the sample size of one.
  5. Would it survive if nobody could post about it? If no, it's a content strategy wearing a lab coat.

Score it: 0–1 "yes" — probably fine, just dramatic. 2–3 — proceed with a snack of skepticism. 4–5 — it's a fad, and the store tab was the whole point.

FAQ

Is this article medical advice?
No — it's satire about a cultural and commercial phenomenon. The statistics are real; the roasting is original. Talk to an actual professional about your actual body.

Why do fad diets keep coming back if they keep failing?
Because the failure feeds the machine: most dieters regain, a $35-billion industry sells the next rotation, and roughly 14 to 15 percent of Americans have bought in at least once. The villain changes; the economics don't.

Has any diet fad ever apologized?
The fad itself? Never. Individual gurus have issued Notes-App screenshots, which we have filed and roasted in the appropriate venue.
