Influencer Apologies: A Field Guide

Satire · 6 min read

Welcome, naturalist. Today we observe one of nature's most fascinating recurring events: the apology video. Not any specific one — no, we would never. We study the genre, the way ornithologists study a flock without naming individual birds. Observe the patterns. Admire the plumage. Do not make eye contact.

Species 1: The Notes-App Screenshot

Identifying features: a block of centered text, posted as a photo, usually beginning with "hey guys" in lowercase, as if grammar itself were part of the problem. The font is the default. The margins are tragic.

Behavior: This is the apology for people who find cameras "overwhelming" but found the original behavior quite filmable. It always includes the phrase "I want to take accountability" and never, in any recorded specimen, includes the actual accountability. It is typically liked by 40% of followers and screenshotted by the other 60%.

Species 2: The Tearless Weep

Identifying features: no makeup, dim lighting, voice lowered two full octaves. The subject stares just past the lens, as though gazing into the comment section itself.

Behavior: The performance peaks at minute three with what experts call "the swallow" — a visible gulp that says I'm human while the eyes remain as dry as a terms-of-service agreement. Bonus points if a pet wanders into frame, providing what biologists call "sympathy cover."

Species 3: The Pivot

Identifying features: begins as an apology, ends as an announcement. "I've learned so much... which is why I'm so excited to share my new merch line."

Behavior: This species has evolved past the apology entirely and now uses the apology's gravitational pull as a launchpad. Scientists remain divided on whether this is bravery or simply a marketing funnel with extra steps. The prevailing theory: extra steps.

Species 4: The Vanishing Act

Identifying features: silence. Total, absolute, feed-goes-quiet silence — punctuated by a single story post of a sunset with no caption.

Behavior: The subject retreats to an undisclosed location (usually a beach house) to "reflect," emerging exactly one news cycle later with a vlog titled something like "healing era." The apology is implied, like a landlord's promise to fix the plumbing.

Anatomy of the apology script

All specimens share the same skeleton. "I never intended to hurt anyone" — the classic opener, roughly translating to "I intended to do the thing, I just didn't intend the screenshots." Then comes "I'm still learning," delivered by adults well into their thirties. Then "I hope you can forgive me," directed at an audience whose forgiveness will be measured in engagement metrics by end of quarter.

The truly advanced specimens include the phrase "out of context," which is the apology equivalent of a get-out-of-jail-free card that expired in 2014 but everyone keeps playing anyway. Close behind it: "it was a different time," deployed for events that occurred, on average, eleven months ago.

Occasionally a specimen breaks the mold with what researchers call "the pre-apology" — apologizing in advance for a video that hasn't dropped yet, a kind of futures market in accountability. Bold. Efficient. Completely unhelpful.

How to observe safely

Do not interact with the specimen in its natural habitat. Commenting "this is fake" contributes to the engagement the apology was designed to harvest — it is the ouroboros of the internet, a snake eating its own tail while monetizing the footage. Instead, enjoy the field guide. Appreciate the ritual. And remember: the apology video is never about the mistake. It's about the comeback tour, and the tour always sells out.
