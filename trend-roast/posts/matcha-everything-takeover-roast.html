Matcha Everything: The Green Takeover Gets Roasted

Satire · 6 min read

800 percent. That is roughly how much Yelp searches for matcha powder rose in a year, with searches for matcha itself up 180 percent, per figures reported in spring 2025. Read those numbers slowly. A powdered tea, made by shading leaves for weeks, picking spring buds by a process refined over centuries, and stone grinding the result, is now being searched for like a concert ticket drop. The tea did not change. Everything around the tea changed, at high speed, in bright green.

The roast here is not of the drink. The drink has centuries of craft behind it and an amino acid, L-theanine, that fans credit for its calm reputation. The roast is of the takeover: the moment a single ingredient stopped being a beverage and became a format, a colour scheme, and a lifestyle badge that can be applied to literally anything, including armpits.

The facts box: a small crop with a huge second career

Start with scale, because scale is the whole joke. Matcha accounts for only 6 percent of the tea grown in Japan. Six percent. The ingredient currently flavouring half the cafe menus in the Western world starts as a small slice of one country's tea output, produced by a farming workforce whose average age is reported at 69. Against that modest base, consider the demand side. The matcha industry was valued at $4.3 billion in 2023 and is expected to nearly double by the end of the decade. Shipments of tea from Japan to the United States jumped 200.9 percent in value in a single year, and US retail sales rose 86 percent in three years.

Chains have reorganised themselves around the powder. Starbucks reported a 40 percent jump in matcha sales in early 2025. Blank Street went further and dropped the word Coffee from its name, with matcha drinks accounting for roughly half its business. A coffee chain renamed itself, in part, after a tea. Somewhere, a tea ceremony master is owed an apology and a royalty cheque, in that order.

Everything is matcha now, including things nobody asked for

The documented product list has escaped the drinks menu entirely. There is matcha deodorant. There are matcha patches for under-eye bags. Collagen now comes in green. Home cooks are pointed toward matcha hummus, and Dunkin' sells a green tea dusted donut, because of course it does. On the dessert side the sprawl is almost respectable: matcha tiramisu has become a fixture in the Los Angeles area, matcha affogato updates the gelato and espresso classic, and cafes layer the powder into cheesecake lattes and strawberry concoctions built to be photographed before they are tasted.

Notice what unites the list. Deodorant does not need to taste of anything. An under-eye patch is not a beverage occasion. These products are not selling flavour, they are selling the colour and the calm reputation attached to it, in categories where the ingredient is functionally a costume. Matcha has become the wellness era's favourite shade of green, and a shade can be printed on anything. The tea is almost incidental, which is a strange fate for an ingredient whose entire tradition is about paying attention to the tea.

Meanwhile, the actual tea is being rationed

While the costume department expands, the real supply is tightening in ways that read like satire on their own. Two of the best known Uji producers, Marukyu Koyamaen and Ippodo, introduced quantity limits on matcha purchases for the first time, and caps of one or two items per customer became normal at specialty shops in Kyoto and Tokyo. A check of 15 Japanese tea brands in October 2026 found 6 of them limiting matcha sales. Rationing. For tea. In the country that grows it.

The secondary market has noticed. Resellers flip tins at inflated prices, in the manner of limited edition sneaker drops, and counterfeiters package lesser tea in look-alike tins. Japan is producing more of the base leaf than it used to: tencha output reached 6,278 tonnes in 2025, about 3.2 times the 2014 level, per the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries. Demand simply grew faster than a shaded, slow, hand tended crop can. A newly planted tea field takes about five years before it can be harvested properly, so the supply answer to a viral moment arrives roughly half a decade after the moment. The algorithm moves in weeks. The shrub disagrees.

The grade on the tin is doing marketing work

One label deserves its own moment under the lights. Ceremonial grade, the phrase printed on premium tins across the West, is described in industry reporting as a marketing invention of the United States. It means little in Japan, where teas are classified by harvest season, region, and shading technique instead. None of this makes a given tin bad. It makes the label a story the buyer is paying to be told, a two word promise of ceremony that the actual classification system never issued.

Pair that with the counterfeits and the flipped tins and a pattern forms: the scarcer and more storied the real product becomes, the more value pools in the storytelling around it. The takeover runs on narrative as much as on leaf, and narrative, unlike tencha, can be produced in unlimited quantities overnight.

A leaf that costs roughly five times its everyday cousin, sells under purchase caps, and takes five years to expand is now the house flavour of donuts and deodorant. That is not a supply chain. That is a magic trick performed on a crop.

Who the takeover serves, and who can skip it

The honest ledger has two columns. On one side, cafes get a high interest ingredient, drinkers get a gentler caffeine option than coffee, and Japanese producers finally get global respect, and global prices, for a craft that was undervalued at home for decades. Reporting from inside the industry makes that point plainly, and it is a fair one.

On the other side sits everything wearing the colour without carrying the substance: the deodorant, the patch, the dusted donut, the tin whose grade was invented by a marketing department an ocean away from the farm. The takeover's best trick is making those two columns look like one product. They are not. One is a tea with nine centuries of patience behind it. The other is a shade of green with a very good agent. Drink the first. Roast the second. The shrub has waited this long; it can wait out the costume phase too.

, Rex Bennett, Entertainment Writer
