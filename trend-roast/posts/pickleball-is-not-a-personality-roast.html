Pickleball Is Not a Personality: A Roast

Satire · 6 min read

Pickleball is a sport. That sentence should not be controversial, and yet it now needs saying out loud, because somewhere between the paddle, the league nights, the matching gear, and the fifth consecutive year of being called the fastest growing sport in America, pickleball quietly applied for a second job: personality. A hobby is something a person does. A personality is something a person leads with, organises weekends around, and defends in neighbourhood disputes. This is a reception check on which of the two the numbers actually support.

The sport itself gets a genuine pass up front. It is accessible, social, and played by millions who were not doing much sport before it arrived. The roast targets the title, the takeover energy, and the volume, in every sense of that word.

The reception: five years at the top of the growth chart

The headline numbers are real and they are large. The Sports and Fitness Industry Association, in its 2026 report, puts US participation at 24.3 million players in 2025, up from 4.2 million in 2020, more than fivefold in five years. The same association calls pickleball the nation's fastest growing sport for five consecutive years. Growth of 171.8 percent across the 2022 to 2025 window backs the title over that span, and this is not a one demographic fad: players ages 13 to 24 post the highest participation rates of any age segment, women account for 42.9 percent of players, up from 38.6 percent in 2020, and core players, those playing eight or more times a year, grew from 1.4 million to 7.5 million.

Those are the numbers of a sport that earned its boom. They are also the numbers that got promoted, in the retelling, from a growth statistic into an identity. Fastest growing stopped being a measurement and became a self description, the sporting equivalent of introducing yourself by your job title at a barbecue.

The newer numbers, read at a lower volume

Reception checks exist for the part of the record the celebration skips. In the SFIA rolling 12 months ending June 2026, pickleball participation grew 1.3 percent, while padel grew 14.5 percent over the same period. Read that carefully: pickleball still leads the three year comparison, and 1.3 percent growth is still growth, not decline. But the association itself described the result as the sport's first sign of moderation after five years at the top.

Moderation is a perfectly normal thing for a maturing sport with 24.3 million players. Percentage growth slows as the base gets huge; arithmetic insists on it. The comedy is in the branding problem this creates. An identity built on being the fastest growing thing in the room does not age gracefully into being the large, established, slightly slower growing thing in the room, even when the large established thing is objectively the success story. The title was a rocket stage. Rocket stages are supposed to fall away.

The other reception: the neighbours can hear it

Every personality eventually meets an audience that did not buy a ticket. Pickleball's is the neighbourhood. The paddle strike sound is documented as sharp and repetitive, noticeable through bedrooms, living rooms, and balconies when courts sit near homes, and the dispute record has grown alongside the participation record. Legal reporting catalogues complaints, homeowners association fights, and lawsuits across condominium communities. In Delaware, the Court of Chancery declined in March 2024 to dismiss nuisance and injunctive relief claims tied to pickleball noise and activity. In Florida, homeowners sued after tennis facilities were converted for pickleball, alleging the noise interfered with the use and enjoyment of their homes.

Then there is Walnut Creek, California, where the pattern achieves its final form. At Rudgear Park, two tennis courts became eight pickleball courts, beside homes sitting 50 to 90 feet from the nearest court. Forty two neighbours signed a petition before the fight reached a courtroom. The neighbours' amended complaint, dated May 1, 2025, puts the noise as high as 88 decibels, described as roughly a leaf blower at close range. That figure is an allegation in a complaint, not a court finding, and the litigation is ongoing. What is not alleged but documented: the city cut playing hours by 20 percent and closed the courts for two afternoons, and the dispute has outlasted years of negotiation. A sport so social it generated its own docket of noise law. Tennis never needed a legal defence fund for its sound.

Neither column is false about the sport. Only one of them fits on a paddle bag.

Sport or personality: a decision framework

  1. Check the introduction. If the first fact offered about a person is a paddle sport, the sport has been promoted to personality. Enjoying a game at volume is a hobby; leading with it is a brand.
  2. Check the title's expiry date. Fastest growing was accurate for five straight years and remains accurate over the three year window. Quoting it without the newer 1.3 percent figure is not enthusiasm, it is selective accounting.
  3. Check the court math. Two tennis courts becoming eight pickleball courts is a win for players and a real change for everyone who used the old courts or lives 50 feet away. Both halves belong in the story.
  4. Check the exit condition. A sport survives its growth spurt and becomes a fixture. A personality has to keep growing or find a new title. The healthiest outcome available is the boring one: a big, popular, moderately growing sport that no longer needs to introduce itself.

Why no fix procedure here? Nothing about the game needs fixing; the framework above is the tool, a way to tell the sport apart from the identity wrapped around it. The verdict, for the record, is fond. Pickleball took millions of people off couches and onto courts, broadened well past its original demographic, and built a core of 7.5 million regular players, which is the part of the story that will still matter in a decade. What earns the roast is the insistence that a paddle, a league, and a five year growth title amount to a personality. Personalities are supposed to be interesting at rest. A sport this good does not need the costume. It just needs courts, ideally at a respectful distance from bedroom windows, and hours the neighbours agreed to.

, Rex Bennett, Entertainment Writer
