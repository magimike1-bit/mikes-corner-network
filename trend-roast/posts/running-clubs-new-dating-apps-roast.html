Running Clubs Are the New Dating Apps: A Roast

Satire · 6 min read

Six in the morning. The light is grey, the pavement is damp, and a crowd has gathered at a start line dressed with the careful effortlessness usually reserved for a first date — because, statistically, that is what a meaningful share of them are at. Nobody present will say so. The official story is cardio. The survey results, read out loud, tell a second story underneath the first, and the second story has much better numbers.

This is a reception check, not a hit piece. Running clubs are, by every documented account, booming, social, and genuinely good at getting people out of the house. The roast is narrower and fairer than that: the culture insists it has replaced dating apps by rejecting everything about them, while quietly re-importing their entire operating system — signals, churn, ghosting and all — at a pace of nine minutes a mile.

The survey results, read out loud

Start with growth, because growth is the alibi. Strava's 2024 Year in Sport reporting puts run club membership up 59 percent, with new clubs up 3.5 times. A 59 percent jump in one year is not a fitness habit spreading at jogging speed. It is a social format going viral, and social formats go viral for social reasons.

Then the dating figures, which the coverage reports approvingly and then hurries past. One in five runners has dated someone they met at a run club. One in five. If a dating app posted a one-in-five conversion rate from showing up to going on a date, its founders would never work again — they would not need to. A cited survey of Gen Z run-club members puts the motive in plain language: 72 percent joined primarily to meet people, not for fitness. And the surrounding economy agrees: athletic singles events are up 49 percent year over year, per Eventbrite figures reported via Fast Company.

None of these numbers describe a running boom with incidental romance. They describe a dating market that found better lighting, a healthier dress code, and a plausible reason to be standing next to someone at dawn. The cardio is real. The cardio is also the cover story, and the cover story is doing a lot of public-relations work for the numbers underneath it.

Colour codes: the swipe is a t-shirt now

Every dating platform needs a signalling system, and the run club has evolved one so literal it deserves its own exhibit. One documented club system uses colour-coded signals: red means dating, green means single, yellow means complicated. Read that again. The availability status — the core database field of every dating app ever built — has been ported to sportswear.

The coverage treats this as a fun detail, a bit of trivia for the trend file. It is not trivia. It is the app-ification of the pavement, complete with a user interface you can sweat through. The swipe did not disappear when people deleted the apps in disgust. It got up early, put on a colour-coded top, and started doing laps. Yellow for complicated deserves special respect: even the run club, a format marketed as the honest alternative, ships with a relationship-status setting that means please do not ask follow-up questions.

The app dynamics followed you here

The defence rests on one claim: in person is different. People are real at a run club, the argument goes, not profiles. True — and incomplete, because the documented downside reporting shows the apps' social mechanics arriving right on schedule. Clubs reproduce dating-app dynamics including ghosting, romantic churn, and friend groups splitting after breakups. Ghosting, at a run club. You cannot unmatch someone who knows your usual route.

And the most telling detail: members switch clubs to avoid exes. That is the delete-and-reinstall pattern, performed on foot, at dawn, in colour-coded sportswear. The platform changed. The behaviour filed a change-of-address form and moved in. None of this makes run clubs bad — the same reporting frames them as real communities with real friendships in the mix. It makes the marketing claim false. The run club is not the opposite of a dating app. It is a dating app with a warm-up, a water station, and considerably better plausible deniability.

A word for the actual runners

Somewhere in every club is a contingent who came to run, train, and go home, and whose long run is now threaded through other people's meet-cute. The documented numbers spare them a thought: if 72 percent of one surveyed generation joined to meet people, the remaining share joined for the stated purpose and now trains inside a singles event that denies being one. Their complaint writes itself, and it is the most sympathetic one in this story. They did not leave the apps. The apps followed them to the one place that was supposed to be about splits and shoes.

Cardio or cover story: a decision framework

  1. Check the signalling system. If the club publishes a way to broadcast availability — colours, wristbands, themed singles runs — it is a dating format with running in it. Join with that knowledge, whichever answer you prefer.
  2. Read the motive numbers. With one in five runners dating a clubmate and surveyed majorities joining to meet people, treating a club as a pure training group is a choice, not a default.
  3. Plan the exit before you need it. The documented pattern is switching clubs after a breakup. Knowing the second-nearest club in advance is the run-club equivalent of not texting your ex.
  4. Respect the runners. Every club contains people who are only there for the run. The framework's last rule is the first one socially: a no at the water station is complete information.

Why no fix procedure here? Nothing is broken that a settings menu could repair. The useful tool is the framework above — a way to read a club's format honestly before your heart rate, in either sense, gets involved.

The verdict is friendlier than the title. A dating format where people are punctual, outdoors, doing something genuinely good for them, and identifiable by a published colour code is, honestly, an upgrade on the swiping era in several documented respects. What earns the roast is the costume: a 59 percent membership surge, a one-in-five dating rate, and a colour-coded availability system, all insisting with a straight face that this is simply about cardio. Cardio is the cover story. The cover story is fine. Just stretch before it, because the warm-up is now part of the date.

— Rex Bennett, Entertainment Writer
