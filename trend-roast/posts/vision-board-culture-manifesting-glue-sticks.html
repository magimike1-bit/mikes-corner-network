Vision Board Culture: A Roast of Manifesting With Glue Sticks

Satire · 6 min read

What, exactly, is the glue for? Ask the question in any January living room and watch the room divide. On one side, the believers: the glue is ceremonial, the final seal on a message to the universe. On the other, the eye-rollers: the glue is arts and crafts, and the universe has never once RSVP'd to a craft project. Both sides are holding the same cork board. Both sides are partly right, which is what makes vision board culture such a satisfying roast. The board works. Just not at the job printed on the poster.

The claim, stated fairly

A roast that misstates its target is just bullying with better fonts, so here is the claim in its own strongest form. The pop version of manifesting, the one that floods short-form feeds every January, holds that thoughts carry a vibrational frequency, that the frequency attracts matching circumstances, and that thinking abundance, and feeling it as if it were already true, prompts the universe to rearrange itself and deliver. Vision boards are the practice's flagship tool: a collage of images of dreams and desires, as one student newspaper summary put it, assembled alongside written affirmations repeated out loud.

The standard method, described by a manifesting site's own guide, is disarmingly simple: take a board, pin or attach pictures and visual aids representing what you want, and hang the finished object where you will see it constantly. That is the whole liturgy. Scissors, a stack of magazines or a printer, a board, and a wall with a view of your desk. The universe, according to the brochure, handles shipping.

What the board actually does while nobody is watching

Now the awkward part, awkward for the eye-rollers. Strip the cosmic packaging and the board is doing real work, of the unglamorous, well-named kind. Psychologists have plain vocabulary for the mechanisms: implementation intentions, selective attention, self-fulfilling prophecy, and goal salience, which means, in human language, a goal kept in front of your attention instead of buried under a hundred competing demands. A person who looks at their goal every morning for six months is not being beamed anything. They are being reminded, daily, at scale, and reminders change behaviour in ways that look, from a distance, suspiciously like fate.

The same essay that lays out the vibrational claim is blunt about the evidence: there is no evidence that thinking about something creates it externally, and belief in the practice predicts people's dispositions and biases rather than any measurable external effect. The ritual does one more honest thing. It forces an admission. Writing the want down, cutting it out, gluing it in public, requires saying out loud what you actually want, and that admission, the essay argues, is mostly the work. The candles and crystals are the container that made the admission feel safe. The glue stick, it turns out, is a confessional with a cap.

Which produces the culture's signature irony. The board gets credited for the outcomes that attention and effort produced, and the credit flows to the frequency. The person did the reps. The poster took the bow.

The ritual calendar: construction, ignoring, reckoning

Vision board culture runs on a liturgical year, and the calendar is half the comedy. Construction season opens in late December and peaks in January, when, as one essay on the ritual notes, the making of moodboards and vision boards intensifies online, assembled from Pinterest cutouts, with the same recycled images recirculating year after year. The same villa. The same passport flat lay. The same stack of cash, clipped by ten thousand strangers, an image so widely manifested that the photograph itself has now been to more vision boards than most people go on holidays.

Then comes the long middle of the year, the ignoring season, during which the board performs its one documented function, sitting in the sightline, radiating reminders, while its owner walks past it to the fridge. And then December delivers the reckoning, the annual audit that the culture performs in public: the side-by-side post, board versus year, matches celebrated, misses quietly cropped. The essay on the ritual names the failure mode precisely: a board can stop functioning as support for thinking and become a substitute for action, where instead of acting, one curates, and intention stays stored rather than spent.

The criticism also comes from inside the tent, which keeps everyone honest. A site written by a manifesting practitioner argues that a board hung where it is seen every day is also seen on the frustrated, conflicted, unhappy days, where it can trigger the lack-focused thought, why don't I have it yet, and work against the very mindset it was built to install. Even the supply shop has read the returns data.

Read the right-hand column twice. It is not a debunking. It is a job description, and it is a better one than the claim, because every item in it can actually be done on purpose.

The glue-to-calendar audit: a decision framework

Keep the board. The board was never the problem. The audit below, run once per board, converts clippings back into the machinery that actually delivers them.

  1. Pair every picture with a verb. Each image gets one next action written under it in pen, unglamorous and schedulable. A picture of a passport gets the renewal form. A picture of a studio gets the first lesson. An image with no verb is decoration; that is fine, but label it honestly and stop billing it as a plan.
  2. Move one verb to the calendar this week. Goal salience is the documented engine, and a calendar entry is salience with a date on it. If the board produces no calendar entries, it is functioning as the substitute the critics warned about.
  3. Schedule the reckoning in advance. Pick the December date now, and write down how each picture will be judged: done, in progress with evidence, or released without self-blame. The culture's cruelty is the improvised audit; a scheduled one is just a review.
  4. Retire images that start billing you. The practitioner's warning stands: a picture that now triggers only lack is no longer a reminder, it is a debt notice. Swap it for the next step's picture. The board serves the year; the year does not serve the board.

Why no fix procedure here? A vision board is not broken equipment, and manifesting is not a settings menu. The topic earns a framework instead of steps: an audit that keeps the ritual, which people plainly love, and returns the credit to the only agent in the room with a documented track record, the person holding the glue stick.

The last word belongs to the calendar

So, the verdict on manifesting with glue sticks. The glue is innocent. The scissors are innocent. The board is a to-do list wearing a costume, and the costume is doing one useful thing: it makes the list emotionally possible to look at. Where the culture earns its roast is the invoicing, the habit of billing the universe for work that attention, admission and an ordinary Tuesday of effort actually performed, and of blaming the customer's frequency when the delivery van, which was always the customer's own calendar, never left the depot.

Make the board. Make it beautiful. Then do the most radical act in the entire manifesting catalogue: write a date under one of the pictures, in pen, where the universe can see it.

— Rex Bennett, Entertainment Writer
