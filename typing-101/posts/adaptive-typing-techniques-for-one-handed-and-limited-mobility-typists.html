Adaptive typing techniques for one-handed and limited-mobility typists

Published 2026-10-27 · Last updated 2026-10-27

The myth goes like this: lose the use of a hand, or the range in one, and typing becomes one-finger hunting at half speed forever. Search results feed the myth — they are mostly phone one-hand modes and gadget lists. The desktop reality is older and better than that: keyboard layouts designed specifically for one hand were documented by assistive-technology researchers decades ago, the settings that unlock them are already built into current systems, and none of it starts with buying hardware.

General information only, not medical or occupational-therapy advice. For an individual setup — especially after injury or surgery — a qualified professional who can see how you move is worth more than any article.

Start with your situation, not a product list

A six-week injury, permanent one-hand use, and limited range of motion are three different problems wearing one label. They want different tools, in a different order, and confusing them is how people buy hardware for a problem a free setting would have solved — or spend weeks relearning a layout for an injury that heals next month.

Read your row, then work down this page in order: free settings first, layout changes second, mirrored setups third, hardware last. Each tier is cheaper and more reversible than the next, and a surprising amount of the problem usually dissolves in tier one.

Free first: Sticky Keys, Filter Keys, Slow Keys

Three built-in accessibility settings cover the most common mechanical problems, and they cost nothing to test for an afternoon.

  1. Turn on Sticky Keys. On Windows: Settings > Accessibility > Keyboard — or tap the Shift key five times. Sticky Keys makes modifier combinations sequential instead of simultaneous, so a shortcut like Ctrl+P becomes two separate taps, one key at a time, instead of a chord one hand may not span.
  2. Add Filter Keys if keys repeat or bounce. The Windows Filter Keys setting ignores brief or repeated keystrokes, which is the tremor and accidental-brush problem: the keyboard stops punishing touches you did not mean.
  3. On a Mac, try Slow Keys. Slow Keys adds a delay before a keypress registers, so a hand crossing the keyboard does not type everything it passes over; only keys held for the set moment count.
  4. Test against your real work for a day. Write the email, fill the form, use the two-key shortcuts your software demands. Settings that help in a settings panel and hinder in your actual applications get switched back off, one at a time, so you learn which one was doing what.

What success looks like: you can produce your normal shortcuts as separate taps without a missed or doubled key in the sequence, and a full document goes out with no more corrections than your two-handed baseline. If modifier chords were the whole problem, you may be finished at this tier — stop here and bank the win.

One-handed Dvorak and Chubon: layouts built for this

The one-handed Dvorak layouts — separate left-hand and right-hand versions — come out of assistive-technology design, documented in a 1996 Delaware Assistive Technology Initiative newsletter that remains the deepest layout source in the search results. Their principle is direct: place the most frequent letters under the fingers on the home row, and cut the distance the hand travels. The documented saving is up to one third less finger travel than one-handed QWERTY.

Chubon takes the same idea further toward limited motion. Its layout clusters the frequent keys near the space bar, designed for restricted range — one finger, a headstick, or a mouthstick covering a small area instead of a whole keyboard. Where one-handed Dvorak optimises a working hand, Chubon optimises a small reachable zone.

Two practical notes from the same research tradition. Ordinary keyboards can be rearranged to a new layout at low cost — this is a settings-and-practice tier, not a shopping tier. And most one-hand typists are not touch typists on the standard layout, which lowers the real cost of switching: there is less old muscle memory to fight than a two-handed touch typist would face. Before assuming anything needs purchasing, open your system's keyboard-layout list and look for the one-handed Dvorak options there; layout switching is a standard operating-system feature, and the check takes a minute.

Mirrored keyboards: both halves under one hand

A different answer suits people who already touch-type. A mirrored — or mirrorboard — setup lets one hand cover both halves of the keyboard: hold or toggle a key and the hand's home keys produce the opposite half's letters, mirrored, so the muscle memory built by the other hand transfers in reflection instead of being discarded.

The tooling reported by users is remapping software rather than new boards: QMK firmware's swap_hands feature on keyboards that run it, the kmonad remapper, and AutoHotkey scripts. In the same community reports, a programmer with an arm amputation used Karabiner-Elements to remap development-environment shortcuts onto the remaining hand — a reminder that the shortcut layer often matters as much as the letter layer, and Sticky Keys thinking applies here too.

Choose between the two layout philosophies by history. No strong QWERTY touch-typing to preserve: one-handed Dvorak is the cleaner start. Years of touch-typing in the hand that remains: mirroring reuses the surviving half of that investment, and the toggle becomes the new skill to drill.

The hardware tier — and an honest note on prices

Hardware earns its place when the free and layout tiers leave a real gap. The categories the sources document: chorded keyboards, which produce letters from key combinations pressed together and suit voice-first and amputation setups; large-key adaptive boards, which trade key count for target size; and light switches and alternative pointing devices, including ergonomic mice and trackballs, for the cursor half of the job.

No prices appear in this section on purpose. The only price ranges found during research came from a gadget blog's table and were never verified against a retailer, so quoting them as facts would mislead exactly the readers comparison-shopping for medical-adjacent equipment. Check current retailer listings for the specific category your situation names, and treat any blog price table — including the one behind this research — as a rough range to verify, not a budget.

Voice dictation as a partner

Dictation belongs in the plan from the start for two situations the sources name: the broken-wrist case, where it removes load from the healing side entirely, and voice-first setups for typists whose keyboard options are narrowest. For everyone else it still works as a pressure valve — long first drafts spoken, then edited at whatever keyboard speed the day allows. Editing is the lighter keyboard task: shorter bursts, more pauses, both settings and layouts cope with it better than sustained composition.

Realistic speeds, and how to practise

Expectations, with their sources attached: community reports gathered in a Hacker News discussion summary describe users reaching about 40 words per minute on one-handed Dvorak within a couple of weeks of switching. That is a forum-reported figure, not a study — treat it as evidence that useful speed arrives in weeks rather than months, not as a promise with your name on it. The travel saving is the structural reason speed rebuilds at all: a layout that cuts finger travel by up to a third gives one hand less distance to cover on every word, permanently.

Practise on the layout you chose, in short daily sessions, and measure on that layout with the same test each time so the numbers compare against each other. Standard speed tests assume a standard layout's letter distribution; your score on a one-handed layout is still a valid measure of your progress, as long as the comparison is this week against last week on identical terms — not against two-handed QWERTY figures from before the switch.

The sequence is the whole method: name the situation, spend nothing first, change the layout only if the situation is permanent, mirror if there is muscle memory worth keeping, and buy hardware only for the gap that remains.
