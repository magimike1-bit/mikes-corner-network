Common bad habits to unlearn

Troubleshooting · 6 min read

Most people don't learn to type — they accumulate years of improvised workarounds and call the result a typing style. If you're converting from hunt-and-peck to touch typing, or you've hit a plateau, one of these habits is almost certainly the reason.

1. Looking at the keyboard

This is the big one. Every glance at your hands breaks the brain-to-keyboard map you're trying to build, and as long as looking works, your brain will never bother learning the layout by feel. The fix is absolute: no peeking, ever, during practice. Slow down until you can type without looking. Cover the keys with a towel or turn off the room lights if willpower alone isn't doing it. Within a couple of weeks of enforced no-looking, the panic fades — and then it never comes back.

2. Wrong fingering — the "efficient" shortcut

Many self-taught typists use two or three fingers per hand, or cross fingers over each other for reaches that feel long. It feels faster in the moment, but it caps you: those fingers have a limited range, and the system doesn't scale to real speed. The fix is tedious but simple: relearn each key with its assigned finger, one column at a time, starting with the keys you use most. Drill the correct finger until it beats the old shortcut, then move to the next key. Expect a temporary speed drop — that's the old wiring being overwritten.

3. Tension: shoulders, hands, and the death grip

Beginners type like they're arm-wrestling the keyboard: shoulders up by their ears, fingers striking keys with ten times the needed force. Tension slows you down and is the main source of typing-related soreness. Fixes:

  • Consciously drop your shoulders every few minutes until it becomes automatic.
  • Use minimum force. Modern keyboards need a light touch. If your keycaps are getting shiny from pounding, you're over-pressing.
  • Keep fingers curved and relaxed, hovering just above the keys — not flattened, not clawed.

Tension also shows up in the wrists (see ergonomics).

4. Hovering too far above the keys

Some learners hold their hands a full inch above the keyboard "ready to strike," which doubles the travel distance for every keystroke and wrecks rhythm. Your fingers should rest just above the keycaps — close enough to feel them — so each press is a small tap, not a dive.

5. Burying the spacebar under one thumb

Beginners often assign spacebar to whichever thumb feels natural and let the other thumb do nothing. Better practice: use both thumbs, alternating — right thumb after left-hand words, left thumb after right-hand words, roughly speaking. It balances the load and keeps your rhythm smooth at higher speeds.

6. Correcting every typo mid-word, instantly

Slapping backspace the instant a wrong letter appears is natural, but it trains your brain to fear errors instead of preventing them. During practice, try this instead: finish the word or sentence, then go back and fix it. You'll still correct mistakes — you just stop interrupting the flow of correct typing to do it. (During timed tests, never go back at all; it costs more than it saves.)

7. Inconsistent practice posture

Motor skills are context-sensitive. If you practice with perfect posture at a desk but do all your real typing slouched on a couch with the laptop balanced on your knees, your hands are learning two different skills. Keep your practice setup and your everyday setup reasonably similar — or at least keep the hand and wrist position consistent.

How to actually break a habit

Habits don't die from knowledge; they die from replacement. Pick one habit, drill its replacement slowly for a week, and ignore the others for now. Trying to fix all seven at once guarantees you'll fix none. A good order: no-looking first, then correct fingering, then tension, then the rest. And give it time — a habit built over years of hunt-and-peck takes weeks, not days, to replace.

Once your fundamentals are clean, set targets with typing speed goals by use case — realistic numbers for students, office workers, and programmers.
