Breaking through a typing speed plateau: diagnose the cause before you drill

Speed · 8 min read

A plateau feels unfair: the practice continues, the number does not. New typists improve quickly at first, and then — as Typing.com's own plateau guide puts it — learners get stuck at a given words-per-minute rate regardless of how much they keep practicing. The usual response is more of the same practice, harder. That works only if the practice was the problem. Most plateaus have a specific cause, and each cause has a different fix. This lesson works in that order: prove the plateau is real, diagnose which cause is yours, fix that one thing for two weeks, then retest under exactly the same conditions.

First, prove the plateau is real: a controlled baseline

Typing scores are noisier than they look, because tests do not all count the same way. Most tests count a word as five keystrokes, including spaces, and report gross WPM as (characters typed ÷ 5) ÷ minutes. But net WPM subtracts the errors you left uncorrected, and accuracy-adjusted WPM multiplies gross speed by accuracy — so one performance scored at 60 gross WPM and 95% accuracy is 57 WPM adjusted, and a different figure again as a net score. Compare a gross score from one site with a net score from another and you can manufacture a plateau — or a breakthrough — out of pure arithmetic.

So before changing anything, take a baseline you can repeat:

  1. Pick one test and stay with it. Note whether it reports gross, net, or accuracy-adjusted speed. Every future comparison uses this test, this setting, this scoring method.
  2. Use a longer test. Take a five-minute test rather than a one-minute sprint. Short tests reward a fast start; longer ones show the speed you can actually sustain, which is the speed your plateau is about.
  3. Take it three times in one sitting and write down all three scores plus your accuracy each time. Your baseline is the middle score — not the best, which flatters you, and not the worst, which punishes one bad passage.
  4. Record the spread. If your three scores sit within a few WPM of each other, week after week, the plateau is real. If they swing widely, you do not have a plateau yet — you have inconsistency, and the diagnosis below starts with the accuracy check.

For context, a large 2026 study of 10,613 U.S. adults found a median accuracy-adjusted speed of about 37.8 WPM on a computer, with the middle half of typists between roughly 24 and 56 WPM. Plateaus happen at every level in that range; the number itself is not the problem. The stall is.

The four-cause diagnosis

Run these four checks against your baseline sessions. Most learners find one dominant cause; fixing two at once muddies the retest, so pick the clearest one.

1. The accuracy ceiling. Look at the accuracy column, not the speed column. If accuracy sits below about 95%, errors are eating the speed you already have. The arithmetic is unforgiving: at 40 WPM you press roughly 200 keystrokes a minute, so 95% accuracy means about ten mistakes a minute to find and fix, while 98% means about four. Every correction also breaks rhythm in a way the score never shows. Experienced learners advising a typist stuck near 50 WPM with accuracy dipping below 95% give the same prescription: the routines being typed are sloppy at current speed, and pushing harder only rehearses them faster.

2. Problem keys and letter pairs. Some letter sequences simply take a given typist longer than others, every time they appear. If the same two or three keys or pairs — a top-row reach, a same-finger jump, an awkward pair like a letter followed by its neighbour — show up in your errors and hesitations across sessions, that is a local problem with a local fix, not a general speed limit.

3. A weaker hand. Most people type faster with their dominant hand, which means the other hand can quietly set the pace for both. The tell: words and pairs led by your non-dominant hand feel rushed or arrive late, and your errors cluster on that side of the keyboard.

4. A measurement artefact. If your "plateau" appeared when you changed tests, test lengths, or devices, suspect the ruler before the typist. Gross versus net scoring, a one-minute versus five-minute test, and laptop versus desktop keyboards all move the number without your skill changing at all. The controlled baseline above is the cure for this one.

Match the fix to the cause

Accuracy ceiling → slow down on purpose. For two weeks, practice at a pace where you can hold 98% accuracy, then let speed rebuild on top of clean movement. This feels like going backwards and is the single most prescribed fix in the sources for this lesson — deliberate, correct repetitions first, speed second.

Problem keys → custom drills, weekly. Build short drills out of only your problem keys and pairs, and run them as a focused block — typing instruction guidance recommends working problem keys in custom practice on a weekly basis to reinforce the muscle memory. Five minutes of only your worst pairs is worth more than thirty minutes of general text that contains them twice.

Weaker hand → quadrant practice. Spend a daily block typing patterns that live entirely under the weaker hand — its home-row letters, its reaches, words built from its half of the keyboard — until its pace stops trailing. The goal is not a stronger hand in the gym sense; it is removing the hesitation that makes the strong hand wait.

Measurement artefact → change nothing but the ruler. Re-baseline on one test and judge progress only there for the next month.

The two-week plateau protocol

  1. Day 1: take the controlled baseline (three five-minute tests, same site, note the scoring method) and run the four-cause diagnosis. Write down the one cause you are treating.
  2. Days 2–13: practice 10 to 15 minutes a day — the daily dose typing guidance consistently points to — structured as: five minutes on the matched fix (accuracy-paced text, problem-key drills, or weaker-hand patterns), then five to ten minutes of ordinary passages typed at your accuracy-controlled pace.
  3. Every session: log the date, the drill used, and one line on what went wrong most. You are collecting evidence for the retest decision, not just minutes.
  4. Day 14: retest exactly as on day 1 — same test, same length, same scoring, three attempts, middle score.

What success looks like: the day-14 middle score beats the baseline middle score while accuracy is the same or better, and the specific symptom you treated (error count, problem pairs, weak-side hesitations) is visibly reduced in your session notes. A higher score with worse accuracy is not a breakthrough; it is the accuracy ceiling being paid for in corrections the gross score hides.

Retest, read the result, and repeat

Three outcomes are possible, and each has a next step. Moved: keep the same structure for another two weeks; plateaus usually break in steps, not leaps. Accuracy up, speed flat: the ceiling is lifting — stay the course one more cycle, because speed typically follows accuracy with a lag. Nothing moved: your diagnosis was wrong or the cause is second on your list; treat the next cause for the next two weeks rather than drilling everything at once. And if your hands hurt, stop and rest — community advice and common sense agree that pain is a reason to pause and check your setup, not to push through. General information only: persistent pain, numbness, or tingling is a reason to talk to a qualified health professional, not a typing article.

One last temptation to refuse: buying your way out. A different keyboard does not repair sloppy routines — experienced typists make exactly this point to plateaued learners whose practice time is already adequate. Gear changes have their place, but a plateau with a named cause is cheaper to fix with two honest weeks than with a purchase.

FAQ

How long do plateaus last? As long as the cause goes untreated. The protocol above is built in two-week cycles because that is long enough for a matched fix to show up in a controlled retest — and short enough that a wrong diagnosis costs little.

Should practice be timed during the two weeks? Mostly no. Timed tests are the measuring instrument (days 1 and 14). Daily work is accuracy-paced drilling of one named cause; constant timing re-creates the rushing that built the accuracy ceiling.

What if two causes both look real? Treat the accuracy ceiling first if it is present — below roughly 95% accuracy, error noise hides every other signal, including which keys are genuinely your problem keys.
