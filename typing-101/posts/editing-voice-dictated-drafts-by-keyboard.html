Editing Voice Dictated Drafts by Keyboard: A Cleanup Workflow

Dictation · Published 2026-11-18 · Last updated 2026-11-18

A stubborn myth follows dictation around: that a dictated draft needs one cleanup pass, or one cleanup tool, and then it is finished. Read a raw dictated draft closely and the myth falls apart. The text in front of the keyboard is not one kind of mess. It is at least five different kinds of error stacked in the same paragraphs, each with a different cause, a different fix, and a different answer to the question of who can check it. A single pass treats them as one job and does each of them badly, usually in the wrong order, fixing sentences inside a paragraph that should have been deleted whole.

The keyboard is the right tool for the cleanup, for a reason the platforms themselves document. Google notes that a mistake made while voice typing in Docs can be corrected by moving the cursor to the mistake and fixing it without turning the microphone off. Apple notes that on a Mac with Apple silicon, the keyboard can be used while dictating, with no need to stop. Dictation produces the draft. Precise selection, deletion and replacement, the work cleanup actually is, belongs to the keys.

Five error classes, five different fixes

What dictation gets wrong, class by class
Error classWhat it looks likeWho or what can fix itThe keyboard move
Misheard wordsA real word in the wrong place, such as flour where flower was meant, the example Apple uses for ambiguous dictated textThe editor flags some of them; the writer decides all of themWork the flagged words first, blue underlined on a Mac, grey underlined in Google Docs, then read for the unflagged ones
Missing or wrong punctuationRun on sentences, or commas where a full stop was spoken and missedThe writer, reading for sense; settings can prevent part of it next timeA dedicated punctuation pass, sentence by sentence, after the words are final
Filler and false startsSpoken padding and abandoned sentence openings left in the textThe writer only; filler is usually grammatical, so tools leave itDelete by word and by phrase during the wording pass, never letter by letter
Missing structureOne long block where paragraphs, headings or list items were meant to beThe writer, before any sentence level workSplit, move and cut paragraphs first, while no effort has been spent polishing text that may be deleted
Names, numbers and termsA plausible looking name, figure or technical word that is simply not the one that was saidNobody but the writer, checking against the source of the factsA separate verification pass, with each name and number checked on its own, as the last step before the draft is used

The order in the table is deliberate. Structure comes before wording because polishing a paragraph and then deleting it wastes the polish. Verification comes last and stands alone because a name can be spelled perfectly, punctuated correctly and still be the wrong name. Mixing the classes into one read through guarantees that the eye, busy with commas, slides over the figure that matters.

Before the passes: make the next draft cheaper to clean

Part of cleanup is prevention, and two documented settings decide how much punctuation work a draft brings with it. Windows voice typing, opened with the Windows logo key plus H, carries an automatic punctuation setting in its gear menu, which lets voice typing insert punctuation based on what is being said. Mac Dictation, in supported languages, inserts commas, full stops and question marks automatically, and the feature can be turned off as Auto-punctuation in Keyboard settings by a writer who would rather speak every mark. Google Docs takes the opposite route: punctuation there is spoken, with commands including period, comma, exclamation point, question mark, new line and new paragraph.

None of those choices removes the keyboard passes. They change which class arrives biggest. Automatic punctuation shrinks the punctuation class and leaves the mishearings untouched. Spoken punctuation gives control at the cost of attention while drafting. Either way, one habit protects every later pass: finish dictating the whole draft before editing any of it. A draft that is still arriving cannot be structured, and corrections made mid stream are the ones most likely to be overwritten by the next sentence.

The keyboard cleanup workflow, pass by pass

  1. Pass one, structure only. Read the draft for shape and nothing else. Split the block where paragraphs were meant to be, move any paragraph that belongs elsewhere, and delete warm up text and abandoned openings whole. Select by paragraph where the editor allows it; in Word, Ctrl+Shift plus the up or down arrow key selects to the beginning or end of the current paragraph. Do not fix a single word in this pass.
  2. Pass two, the flagged words. Work through the words the editor itself marked as uncertain. On a Mac, ambiguous dictated text is underlined in blue; clicking the word offers alternatives, and the correct text can be typed instead. In Google Docs, right clicking a word underlined in grey shows suggested alternatives. Accept, replace or leave each one, and clear the whole set before moving on.
  3. Pass three, wording and filler. Read for misheard words the editor did not flag, homophones that read correctly and mean the wrong thing, and filler that adds nothing. Delete by word, not by letter: Ctrl+Backspace deletes one word to the left in Word and Ctrl+Delete deletes one word to the right, and Ctrl+Shift plus an arrow key selects a whole word at a time for replacement. Retype the replacement rather than editing the wrong word letter by letter.
  4. Pass four, punctuation and formatting. With the words final, read sentence by sentence for boundaries: split run on sentences, close questions with a question mark, and check that spoken punctuation commands which were typed out as words, a stray comma or new paragraph sitting in the text, are converted or removed. Apply headings and list formatting in this pass too, using the structure decided in pass one. In Google Docs, formatting can also be applied to a selection, and its documented commands include apply heading, bold and italicize for writers still working by voice at this stage.
  5. Pass five, recurring mishearings, fixed once. Note any word the dictation got wrong more than once, a name, a product term, a place. Fix every instance in one operation instead of hunting them one at a time. In Word, Ctrl+H displays the Replace dialog box, which finds and replaces text throughout the document; Ctrl+F opens the Navigation pane to count the instances first, so the replacement can be checked against the count afterwards.
  6. Pass six, verification. Read only the names, numbers, dates and technical terms, one by one, against wherever those facts came from. Ignore style completely in this pass. A figure that cannot be checked gets flagged in the text rather than smoothed over, because an unverified number in a clean looking draft is worse than an obvious gap.
  7. Final read, aloud if the room allows it. Read the finished draft once at speaking pace. Dictated text keeps the rhythm of speech, and the sentences that run too long or repeat a point are easiest to hear. Shorten what the ear catches, then stop.

What success looks like: a draft where every flagged word has been decided, every sentence ends where it should, recurring mishearings were replaced in one counted operation, and every name and number has been checked against its source, with anything unverifiable visibly flagged instead of hidden.

Why the passes must stay separate

It is tempting to merge the passes into one careful read and save the time. The saving is imaginary. Each pass changes what the eye is looking for, and an eye looking for everything finds the easy things: a missing comma is visible at a glance, while a wrong name is visible only to a reader who is checking names. That is also why automated cleanup, where a tool offers it, does not cancel this workflow. A tool can act on patterns in the text. It cannot know which name was meant, which figure was quoted, or whether the second paragraph repeats the first, because none of that information is in the draft itself. The classes a tool handles well, punctuation patterns and obvious filler, are the cheap classes. The expensive classes stay with the writer either way, and they are exactly the ones a merged pass misses.

One more documented feature helps when a correction goes wrong mid cleanup. Windows voice typing lists undo that among its English commands, and Word carries the same safety net on the keyboard as Ctrl+Z to undo and Ctrl+Y to redo. Cleanup done in passes, with undo close at hand, can afford to be decisive: cut the paragraph, replace the word everywhere, split the sentence. A hesitant edit is usually the one that has to be made twice.

When the draft is clean enough to stop

The stopping rule is a decision, not a feeling of doneness. Stop when the six checks in the success line above can each be answered yes, and not before. A short dictated note may clear all six in a few minutes. A long draft with names and figures in it earns the full sequence, and skipping the verification pass on a long draft is the single most expensive shortcut available, because its errors are the kind readers act on. If time forces a choice, cut the final read aloud and keep verification. Style can survive a rough edge. A wrong number, delivered in polished prose, cannot.

— Dex Carter, Tech Writer
