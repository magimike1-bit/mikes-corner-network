Free practice methods that actually work

Practice · 7 min read

You don't need to buy anything to learn touch typing. You need a keyboard, free tools, and a plan that favors accuracy over speed. Here's how to structure practice that sticks, using only free resources.

The anatomy of a good session

Aim for 15 to 20 minutes a day, five or six days a week. Longer sessions mostly produce tired hands and sloppy repetition, which teaches the wrong thing. Within each session, split your time roughly into three parts:

  • Warm-up (3 minutes): home-row drills and the keys you learned most recently. Slow, eyes on the screen, zero errors as the goal.
  • Focused work (10–12 minutes): one specific weakness — reaching the number row, common letter combinations, whatever is hardest right now. Not random typing: one target.
  • Free typing (3–5 minutes): type whatever you want — journal thoughts, an email draft, lyrics you know by heart. This teaches your hands to string letters into real words at a natural rhythm.

Method 1: key-by-key drills (weeks 1–3)

The first few weeks are about wiring. Work in small groups of letters: first the home row, then the top row, then the bottom row. A simple pattern that works well is to type short sequences like fff jjj fff jjj or ded ded fdf, building combinations that force the right finger to do the right reach. Many free typing trainer websites generate these drills for you; any trainer that lets you drill specific keys is fine — the method matters, not the brand.

Rule: if your error rate climbs, the drill is too hard. Shrink it. Accuracy above 95% should be your constant companion in this phase.

Method 2: real text at your own pace (weeks 2+)

Once the home row feels okay, start typing real sentences — news articles, Wikipedia pages, your own notes. Real text forces you to handle common letter pairs (th, er, in, ou) and punctuation, which isolated drills never teach. The trick is to keep it slow: cap your speed deliberately until accuracy is solid. Typing fast and wrong is just practicing mistakes.

A useful self-test: pick a paragraph, type it three times. The third pass should feel noticeably smoother. If it doesn't, the passage was too hard — pick something simpler.

Method 3: common-word lists

A large share of everything you type comes from a small pool of everyday words — the, and, you, that, with, and a few hundred friends. Practicing these as a set is efficient because it attacks the highest-frequency material directly. Type through a list of a few hundred common words, one at a time, until they feel automatic. Some free trainers build their core lessons exactly this way.

Method 4: timed tests as measurement, not training

One-minute timed tests are great thermometers and lousy workouts. Take one a week to track progress, but don't make them the bulk of your practice — chasing a high score encourages exactly the errors you're trying to eliminate. Log your results (speed and accuracy) in a notebook or spreadsheet so you can see the trend over months; progress feels slow day to day and obvious month to month.

A sample weekly schedule

DayFocus
MonWarm-up + new keys (top row reaches)
TueWarm-up + common words
WedWarm-up + weak-letter drills
ThuWarm-up + real-text typing
FriWarm-up + mix of week's hardest keys
SatTimed test (measure) + light free typing
SunRest

Total: about an hour and a half across the week. That's enough — consistency beats volume. Missing a day is fine; missing a week means re-warming up, so try to keep the streak to no more than one missed day in a row.

What to avoid

  • Marathon sessions. Typing for two hours teaches fatigue habits, not skill. Quit while your accuracy is still high.
  • Racing strangers too early. Competitive typing games are fun, but in the first two months they reward panic over precision. Save them for after your accuracy is stable.
  • Chasing new apps. Switching trainers every week resets your mental model. Pick one free tool and stay with it for at least a month.

Structure beats enthusiasm. Fifteen focused minutes, every day, will take you further than an hour of random typing once a week. Next, read about the bad habits to unlearn — the mistakes that silently cap everyone's progress.
