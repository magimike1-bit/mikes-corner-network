Home-row fundamentals: finger placement, posture, and your first drills

Getting started · 7 min read

Every fast typist started here: fingers resting on the middle row of the keyboard, eyes on the screen, building one correct movement at a time. If you get this page right, everything else — speed, accuracy, comfort — becomes much easier. If you skip it, you'll spend months undoing bad wiring later.

Where your fingers live

The home row is the middle letter row: A S D F on the left hand, J K L ; on the right. (On some international layouts the rightmost key differs, but the idea is the same: the middle row, centered under your hands.) Feel the small raised bumps on the F and J keys — those are your anchors. Without looking, slide your index fingers until you find them, then let the other fingers fall into place:

  • Left hand: pinky on A, ring on S, middle on D, index on F.
  • Right hand: index on J, middle on K, ring on L, pinky on the ; key.
  • Thumbs: both rest lightly on the spacebar.

This is the only correct starting position. Each finger is responsible for the keys in its column: it reaches up or down from home row, strikes the key, and returns. The left index finger, for example, handles F, R, V, T, G, and B; the right index handles J, U, M, Y, H, and N. You don't need to memorize that list — the drills below will teach your hands by repetition.

Posture and hand position

Good typing starts before your fingers touch anything:

  • Sit tall but relaxed. Feet flat on the floor, back supported, shoulders down and back rather than hunched forward.
  • Elbows at roughly 90 degrees, close to your body. Your forearms should float just above the desk — never rest your wrists on the desk edge or a hard surface while typing.
  • Wrists straight and level. The classic beginner mistake is bending the wrists upward ("tenting") to reach keys. Keep the wrist in a neutral, straight line with your forearm, hovering over the keys.
  • Screen at eye level, about an arm's length away, so you aren't craning your neck down.

If your setup makes any of this impossible — laptop on a couch, say — that's fine for a practice session, but don't try to build the skill in a bad setup full-time. (See ergonomics to avoid wrist pain for the fuller picture.)

Your first drills

Beginners do best with the tiniest possible units. Resist the urge to type words until the basics feel boring — boring means they're learned.

Drill 1 — finding home (2 minutes). Look at the screen, not the keyboard. Lift both hands, then place them back on home row using the F and J bumps as guides. Repeat ten times. This single skill — reseating your hands blind — matters more than you think.

Drill 2 — home-row letters (5 minutes). Type the home-row keys slowly: a s d f j k l ;, each with the correct finger. Keep it painfully slow and perfectly accurate. Speed is never the goal in early practice; clean wiring is.

Drill 3 — reaching (5 minutes). Practice one reach at a time. Left middle finger: D, E (up), C (down), back to D. Right middle finger: K, I, comma. Work through each finger's column one by one, returning to home row after every keystroke. If a reach feels awkward, slow down rather than letting a different finger cheat it.

Do drills 1–3 daily for the first week, ten to fifteen minutes a day. Two short sessions beat one long one — motor learning consolidates between sessions, not during them.

The rules that never expire

  • Eyes on the screen. The moment you look at your hands, your brain stops building the keyboard map. Cover the keys with a cloth if you have to.
  • One finger per key. Using a neighboring finger "just this once" teaches your hands the wrong pattern.
  • Accuracy over speed. Every error you make while learning is a mistake your muscles memorize. Slow down until errors vanish, then let speed come on its own.
  • Return to home row. After every keystroke, fingers come back. Home row is home — you should be able to feel, without looking, exactly where you are.

You will be slow at first — slower than your old hunt-and-peck, probably. That temporary dip is the price of the upgrade, and it passes within a few weeks of daily practice.
