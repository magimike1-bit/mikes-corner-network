Typing Practice for Kids: Age-Appropriate Sessions Without the Pressure

For parents · Published 2026-10-21 · Last updated 2026-10-21

Parents usually ask the wrong first question: “How fast should my child type?” Speed is the last thing that matters, and chasing it early teaches exactly the habits — looking down, two-finger reaching, guessing — that a child then has to unlearn. The useful sequence is placement first, accuracy second, and speed a distant third, because speed is what correct placement eventually produces on its own.

This guide gives a two-minute readiness check, session lengths by stage, and the published grade-band milestones framed the way they deserve: as a rough compass for pacing, never as a pass/fail verdict on a child.

Readiness is a check, not a birthday

Guides place the start of structured touch typing somewhere between ages 6 and 10, and the spread itself is the lesson: the deciding factors are physical and practical, not the calendar. Children can typically begin between ages 7 and 10, when hand size, finger dexterity, and reading comprehension are sufficient, while younger children of 5 or 6 do better with pre-typing exploration — keyboard games that build finger awareness and hand-eye coordination without formal drills. Other guidance puts the start at 6 or 7, once hands are big enough to reach the home row.

Run the check instead of counting birthdays. Hands: can the child rest fingers on A S D F and J K L ; without stretching or lifting the whole hand? Letters: can they name the letter you point to without hunting the alphabet from A? Sitting: can they stay with one short activity for ten minutes without distress? Three yeses mean structured lessons will land. A no on hands means wait and play pre-typing games; a no on sitting means shorten the session, not abandon it.

The three stages, in order

Stage 1 — placement (weeks, not days). Home row only, eyes up, accuracy unmeasured and speed unmentioned. The milestone for this stage in published homeschool guidance is telling: by the end of grade 2, all letter keys by touch, with speed explicitly not mattering yet. Stage 2 — accuracy. All letters, short words, then sentences, with a gentle accuracy target and praise attached to clean runs rather than fast ones. Stage 3 — speed, carefully. Only now do timed tests appear, and the published bands are modest: children under 13 typically type 15 to 30 WPM, and teenagers 35 to 55. A child inside those bands with clean technique is ahead of a faster child who looks at the keys.

A weekly routine a family can keep

  1. Pick a physical keyboard. Touch typing needs the raised bumps on F and J; a tablet touchscreen cannot teach placement. A laptop, desktop, or Chromebook keyboard is the right tool.
  2. Keep sessions short and daily. Ten to fifteen minutes a day builds muscle memory faster than one long weekly session, and 10 to 20 minutes is enough at any age in this range.
  3. Split the session in three. A workable 15-minute shape is 5 minutes of warm-up on known keys, 7 minutes of structured lesson, and 3 minutes of a game or speed drill as the reward at the end.
  4. Protect the eyes-up rule kindly. If peeking is constant, cover the keys with a light cloth for a minute at a time rather than scolding; the skill is feeling the F and J bumps, and it arrives with repetition.
  5. Celebrate accuracy milestones, not speed. A first fully clean sentence is a bigger event than a new WPM record, and children aim at whatever gets celebrated.
  6. Stop before it sours. End while it is still mildly fun. A session that ends in frustration teaches avoidance, which costs more than a missed drill.

What success looks like: after a month, the child sits down unprompted for short sessions, finds home row by touch without looking, and types familiar words with visibly less hunting. Speed may barely have moved — that is fine, and it is the expected order of events.

What to avoid

Avoid adult typing tests as report cards; their texts, lengths, and scoring assume adult reading levels. Avoid buying a program as the first move — several structured programs are free, and the routine matters more than the brand. And avoid comparing siblings or classmates out loud: the milestone bands span wide ranges precisely because normal development does. If a child shows persistent pain, extreme frustration, or cannot reach keys comfortably, treat that as a setup problem — chair height, keyboard size, session length — before treating it as an effort problem. When progress stalls, and what it usually means

Almost every child plateaus in the second or third week: the novelty fades, the easy letters are learned, and the new letters feel disproportionately hard. This is the normal shape of learning a motor skill, not evidence that the program is wrong or the child is behind. The useful responses are structural. Shorten the lesson portion and lengthen the game portion for a week. Drop back one level to material the child can type cleanly, and let success rebuild momentum. Check the physical setup with fresh eyes - a chair that leaves feet dangling and a keyboard pushed too far away will quietly tax every session, and children rarely complain about ergonomics; they just get tired and vague about why.

What not to do is double the dose. Doubling a stalled child's practice converts a plateau into a power struggle, and the skill being taught becomes 'typing is the thing adults make me do,' which outlasts any curriculum. If a child asks to type real things - a story, a letter to a grandparent, labels for a collection - drop the drill and let them. Real writing with a purpose is practice in its best disguise, and a page a child is proud of teaches the home row more durably than a week of exercises. The milestones in this guide exist to tell you the journey is on track over months; week to week, the only metric that matters is whether the child still sits down willingly.

FAQ

What age should a child start touch typing?

Somewhere between 6 and 10 for most children, decided by readiness — hands that reach the home row comfortably, letter recognition, and tolerance for a 10-minute activity — rather than by birthday alone. Younger children can start with pre-typing keyboard games.

How long should kids practise each day?

Ten to fifteen minutes daily is the commonly recommended dose, and short daily sessions build muscle memory faster than occasional long ones.

What is a good typing speed for a 10-year-old?

Published bands put under-13s at typically 15 to 30 WPM, and one milestone guide suggests about 20 WPM with 90 percent accuracy by the end of grade 4. Technique and accuracy matter more than the number at this age.

Does my child need a special keyboard?

No special purchase is needed, but a real physical keyboard is essential — the raised bumps on F and J are how fingers find home row by touch, and a tablet touchscreen has none.
