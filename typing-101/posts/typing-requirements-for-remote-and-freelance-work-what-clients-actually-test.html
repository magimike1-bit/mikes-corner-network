Typing Requirements for Remote and Freelance Work: What Clients Actually Test

Jobs & tests · Published 2026-10-30 · Last updated 2026-10-30

What actually screens you out of a remote typing job — the number on your certificate, or the errors underneath it? For most formal hiring, it is the errors. The posted words-per-minute figure is the visible requirement, but screening tools score a net figure that punishes every uncorrected mistake, and freelance clients often skip the formal test entirely and judge a grammar check or a trial task instead. Read the gate before you train for it.

The kill criterion: the score that screens you out before a human looks

Some assessments are automated pre-screens, run on platforms such as Upwork, FlexJobs, and Indeed before any recruiter contact. That ordering matters. A candidate can be rejected by arithmetic, with no interview in which to explain that the keyboard was unfamiliar or the morning was bad. If your practice scores sit below a posting's floor on net speed — not gross speed — more applications will not fix the problem. The training target has to move first.

There is a second kill criterion on the freelance side, and it runs the other way: walk away from the client. If money changes hands before work changes hands — a fee to unlock jobs, a deposit for training, payment for a starter kit — stop immediately. Legitimate screening costs you time. It does not cost you money up front.

Net WPM: how the screening tools actually score you

One documented formula for net WPM is (total correct keystrokes divided by 5) divided by the test duration in minutes. The detail that decides outcomes is the error rule: in tools such as Kenexa Prove It, Criteria, and TestGorilla, each uncorrected error deducts a full word from the score. An error is not a small percentage shaved off the top. It is a word you typed that no longer counts.

The worked example makes the trade concrete. A candidate scoring 52 net WPM at 99 percent accuracy beats a candidate scoring 70 gross WPM at 88 percent accuracy in recruiter scoring, because the second candidate's uncorrected errors are deducted from the headline speed. Seventy looks faster on a free practice site. Fifty-two is the score that survives the employer's arithmetic.

This is a specification worth verifying against your own practice results, and it changes how to practise:

  1. Measure net, not gross. Take a timed test on a tool that reports accuracy alongside speed, and record both numbers every time.
  2. Count uncorrected errors separately. Note how many mistakes were still in the text when the timer ended — those are the deductions, not the ones you fixed.
  3. Retest at the accuracy first. Slow down until accuracy reaches the level the role asks for, then rebuild speed at that accuracy.
  4. Compare like with like. A gross score from one site and a net score from another are different measurements. Keep one test, one format, one log.

What success looks like: your log shows net speed at or above the target for your role, at that role's accuracy level, on repeated tests — not one personal best surrounded by lower scores.

Requirements by role: the guide-consensus table

Published minimums disagree because the roles disagree. The table below reconciles one widely cited role breakdown; treat it as guide consensus, not as a promise about any single employer's posting.

Basic data entry35 WPM45+ WPM95%+

Read the accuracy column as the real ladder. Speed roughly doubles from basic data entry to medical and legal work, but accuracy climbs from 95 percent to 99 percent — and at 99 percent, only one character in a hundred can be wrong, including the ones nobody notices until a name or a dosage is misread downstream.

Why published minimums range from 30 to 80 WPM

Three variables explain most of the spread. The first is role, covered above. The second is market: one regional guide for data-entry postings in Nepal states 30+ WPM as a hard minimum, paired with accuracy above 90 percent — a real floor for that market, and well below the 60–80 WPM target another guide gives for most roles, with a professional accuracy standard of 98 percent or higher. Neither figure is wrong. They describe different labour markets pricing different work.

The third variable is scoring. A 60 WPM requirement measured gross is a softer gate than 50 WPM measured net with per-error deductions. When two postings seem to contradict each other, ask which number each one is actually counting before assuming one of them is inflated.

What freelance clients test instead

Freelance transcription platforms — Rev, TranscribeMe, GoTranscript, Scribie, and Speechpad are the names that recur — gatekeep differently from employers. Many transcription platforms run a strict grammar test as part of the application, because a transcript full of fast, wrong sentences is unsellable. Rev and 3Play Media are named as major captioning employers of freelancers, where the work adds timing and sound description to raw typing.

For direct clients, the test is often the first piece of work itself: a sample file, a short paid trial, a small job with a deadline. That makes your evidence of speed a profile problem as much as a skill problem. Stating an actual measured WPM on an application distinguishes a candidate from one who simply writes “typing” — a specific, recent, honestly measured number reads as credible precisely because it can be checked.

KPH and the numeric-entry variant

Not every assessment counts words. The same screening breakdown that documents net WPM also treats keystrokes per hour — KPH — as the metric for numeric entry, where the work is digits, codes, and form fields rather than sentences. A candidate who trains only on prose tests can meet a WPM target and still be unprepared for the numeric test the role actually uses. If a posting quotes KPH, practise digits.

The decision, in one pass

Match the preparation to the gate. Formal posting with a named test: train net WPM at the role's accuracy, using the table as your target range. Platform application: expect a grammar test and a sample, and prepare your measured numbers in advance. Direct freelance client: expect a trial task, state a real measured speed, and apply the scam filter before any money moves. The typing is the same skill in all three cases. The test is not.
