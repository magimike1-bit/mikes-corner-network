Cold-Weather Tire Pressure Check: Why the TPMS Light Comes On and How to Check Properly

Tires · 7 min read

The first cold snap of the season produces the same dashboard surprise in parking lots everywhere: the tire pressure warning light, glowing on a morning when all four tires look perfectly fine. Most of the time nothing is punctured and nothing is broken. The air inside the tires has simply contracted in the cold, the pressure has fallen, and the car is reporting that fact honestly. This guide explains how much pressure a temperature drop actually removes — with the arithmetic done in advance — what the warning light does and does not tell you, and the correct cold-tire checking procedure, step by step.

Why cold mornings turn the TPMS light on

Air takes up less room as it gets colder. Transport Canada puts a number on the effect: tires lose pressure when the air temperature gets colder, at about 7 kPa (1 psi) for every 5°C drop in temperature. Nothing has leaked. The same air is still in the tire; it is simply pressing outward less hard than it did on the warmer day when the pressure was last set.

Temperature is only half of the slow decline. Transport Canada also notes that tires lose a certain amount of pressure through the tire itself — its permeability — at about 14 kPa (2 psi) per month. A car whose tires were set correctly in early fall and never checked again arrives at the first hard frost down the permeability loss and the cold loss at the same time. That combination, not a nail, is the usual story behind the light.

None of this is visible. A tire can be well down on pressure and still look round and firm in the driveway, which is exactly why the checking procedure below uses a gauge rather than eyesight, and why the owner's-manual wording for these systems is blunt: the tire pressure monitoring system is not a substitute for proper tire maintenance, and keeping the pressure correct remains the driver's job even when the light has not yet come on.

The cold-snap math: how much pressure a temperature drop removes

Applying Transport Canada's rate — about 1 psi per 5°C — to the swings a Canadian fall actually delivers gives a forecast a driver can check a gauge against:

Temperature drop since the tires were setExpected pressure lossTypical moment
5°CAbout 1 psi (7 kPa)An ordinary cooler week in fall
10°CAbout 2 psi (14 kPa)A clear, frosty night after a mild day
20°CAbout 4 psi (28 kPa)Set on a mild afternoon, checked in a cold snap
30°CAbout 6 psi (42 kPa)Set in early fall, checked in mid-winter cold
40°CAbout 8 psi (56 kPa)Set at +20°C, checked at −20°C

Worked through with an example placard figure: suppose the door-jamb label calls for 32 psi and the tires were set to exactly that on a +20°C afternoon. On a −20°C morning, the table predicts about 24 psi — with no leak at all. That number matters because of where the warning threshold sits, which is the next section. Add a month or two of unchecked permeability loss on top of the cold loss and the same tires read lower still. The stakes of running low are not cosmetic, either: Transport Canada's tire safety checklist notes that operating a vehicle with just one tire underinflated by 56 kPa (8 psi) can reduce that tire's life by 15,000 km and increase the vehicle's fuel consumption by 4%.

What the light is actually telling you: steady vs flashing

The dashboard symbol — a horseshoe-shaped tire cross-section with an exclamation point — behaves in two distinct ways, and the two patterns mean different things.

  • Steady light: a pressure problem. On a direct TPMS (a sensor in each wheel measuring actual pressure), the warning is required to appear when a tire drops at least 25% below the recommended cold inflation pressure on the door-jamb placard. In the worked example above, 25% below a 32 psi placard is 24 psi — precisely where the +20°C to −20°C swing lands. In other words, the system is designed to stay silent through small losses and speak up only when the tire is significantly low, which is why a gauge check catches trouble weeks before the light does. Indirect systems, which infer pressure from wheel-speed differences through the ABS sensors, warn without measuring pressure directly — another reason the manual gauge check below is the deciding step, not the light.
  • Flashing, then steady: a system problem. The owner's-manual TPMS declaration describes the malfunction pattern: when the system detects a fault, the telltale flashes for approximately one minute and then remains continuously illuminated, and repeats that sequence at every startup while the fault exists. Adding air does not fix a flashing light — the system is reporting that it may be unable to detect or signal low pressure as intended, and that is a job for a shop with the right diagnostic tools.

Whichever pattern appears, the manual guidance is the same first move: when the low-pressure telltale illuminates, stop and check the tires as soon as it is safe to do so, and inflate them to the proper pressure. Driving on a significantly underinflated tire causes it to overheat and can lead to tire failure, and underinflation also reduces fuel efficiency and tread life and can affect handling and stopping.

Do not just drive it warm and call it fixed.

Driving warms the tires and can raise the pressure enough to switch a borderline light off. The pressure is still low for the conditions the car will be parked in overnight. Check with a gauge while the tires are cold, set the pressure to the placard figure, and let the light clear on its own over the next few kilometres of driving.

The cold-weather pressure check, step by step

  1. Start with genuinely cold tires. Transport Canada's definition: the vehicle has been stationary for at least three hours, or has not been driven more than 2 km. Continental Tire gives the same test in imperial terms — stationary for at least three hours, or driven under a mile at moderate speed. Driving raises tire temperature and pressure by several psi, enough to skew the reading, so first thing in the morning, before the car moves, is the honest moment to measure.
  2. Find the placard figure — on the car, not the tire. The tire information label is usually on the driver's door or inside the driver's door frame; Transport Canada's checklist adds the door post, the glove box, or the fuel door as other locations, with the owner's manual as the fallback. The number moulded into the tire's sidewall is the tire's maximum — its upper structural limit — not the pressure the vehicle is engineered to run on. Note that the placard can list different pressures for front and rear tires; check the label for both before starting.
  3. Use a good gauge. Transport Canada recommends a good-quality tire pressure gauge and notes that the pocket gauges sold by automotive supply stores are generally more accurate than the gauges on gas station air pumps. Keep one in the glove box so the monthly check does not depend on a service station visit.
  4. Measure the first tire. Unscrew the valve cap and set it somewhere it will not roll away. Press the gauge firmly onto the valve stem until the hissing stops — a proper seal is what makes the reading trustworthy. Read the pressure and write it down, or note it against the placard figure for that axle.
  5. Adjust in short bursts, rechecking as you go. If the tire is low, attach the compressor hose and add air in short bursts, checking the pressure at intervals rather than filling continuously — a strong compressor overshoots easily. If a tire is over the placard figure, press the small pin in the centre of the valve stem briefly to let a little air out, then measure again. Repeat until the gauge matches the label.
  6. Work around all four tires — front and rear pressures may differ. Repeat the measure-and-adjust sequence at each wheel, using the placard figure for that axle, not a single number carried around the car.
  7. Check the spare. Transport Canada's instruction is explicit: do not forget the spare. A spare that has sat unchecked for a season has been losing pressure to permeability the whole time, and a flat spare discovered beside a flat tire doubles the problem.
  8. Put every cap back on, and clear snow and debris from the valves. Transport Canada advises making sure the air pump hose valve and the tire valve are free of snow and other debris that could cause a leak at the valve, and replacing the cap when finished. Continental Tire notes the caps also maintain pressure integrity and keep debris out of the valve stem.
  9. Look at each tire while you are down there. Once a month, Transport Canada's checklist calls for inspecting for uneven tread wear, cuts or cracks, bulges, and foreign objects. A nail sitting in the tread, found during a calm morning check, is a much better discovery than the same nail at highway speed.

What success looks like: every tire, spare included, reads at its placard figure on a cold morning with your own gauge; the caps are back on; and on the next drive the warning light stays off — after correct inflation, the light should switch off on its own after a few miles of driving. Repeat the whole check at least once a month, and again after any sharp cold snap, since the table above shows what a single 20°C swing removes.

When it is not the weather: the one-tire test

Cold and permeability take pressure from all four tires at roughly the same rate. A leak does not. That difference is the simplest diagnostic on this page: set all four tires to the placard figure on a cold morning, then recheck them cold a few days later. Four tires that have drifted down together by a similar small amount are behaving normally. One tire that is down noticeably more than the other three has a problem that topping up will only postpone — a puncture, a leaking valve, or damage the monthly visual inspection may already have flagged. Two more patterns also point away from weather: a light that returns within days of a correct fill, and a light that stays on after the tires are correctly inflated and driven — the pattern that suggests either a leak or, if the light flashed first, a system fault.

Deciding: top up, recheck, or book a tire shop

  • Top up and carry on if all four tires were low by similar amounts, the temperature has recently dropped, and the light clears after correct cold inflation and a short drive. Put the monthly check on the calendar — that is the whole fix.
  • Top up and recheck in a few days if one tire was lower than the rest but nothing is visibly wrong. The recheck is the leak test; a tire that holds its pressure was probably set low at the last fill.
  • Book a tire shop if one tire keeps losing pressure faster than the others, if the inspection step turned up a cut, crack, bulge, or embedded object, or if the tire looks flat or the car pulls or thumps — do not drive at speed on a significantly underinflated tire.
  • Book a shop for the TPMS itself if the light flashes for about a minute at startup and then stays on. That pattern is the system reporting a malfunction, not a pressure reading, and no amount of air changes it.

FAQ

The light came on in the morning but went off after driving. Is anything wrong?
Probably a borderline tire plus a cold night: driving warmed the tires and lifted the pressure just over the threshold. The tire is still low for cold conditions. Check it cold with a gauge and set it to the placard figure rather than treating the cleared light as a pass.

Should winter tires be filled to a different pressure?
The number to use stays the one on the vehicle's placard. What changes in winter is how often the pressure needs checking, because cold removes about 1 psi per 5°C of temperature drop on top of the roughly 2 psi a month lost through the tire itself.

Why not just fill to the number on the tire?
That number is the tire's maximum pressure, not the recommended pressure for the vehicle. Transport Canada is explicit about the distinction, and an overinflated tire rides on the centre of its tread, shrinking the contact patch and reducing grip — the opposite of what winter driving needs.

The bottom line

A TPMS light on the first cold morning is usually arithmetic, not damage: about 1 psi gone per 5°C of temperature drop, plus about 2 psi a month through the tire itself. Check cold — three hours parked, or under 2 km driven — with a good gauge, fill to the door-jamb placard rather than the sidewall, include the spare, and recheck monthly. Save the worry for the patterns that actually signal trouble: one tire falling faster than the others, a light that will not clear after a correct fill, or a light that flashes before it stays on.
