Driving in Snow: The Basics for Calmer Trips

Driving · 5 min read

This isn't a driving course, and it won't make you a winter rally driver. It's the handful of habits that keep ordinary drivers out of ordinary trouble on snowy roads: slow everything down, leave more room, and know what to do if the car starts to slide. Most winter collisions come down to the same few preventable things.

The one rule: everything gentler, everything slower

Snow and ice cut your tires' grip dramatically, and grip is what lets you accelerate, brake, and steer. The less grip available, the more gently you have to use it. That means:

  • Accelerate softly. Press the gas gradually from a stop. If the wheels spin, you're asking for more grip than the road has — ease off and try again more gently.
  • Brake early and lightly. Start braking much sooner than you think you need to. ABS (the system that pulses the brakes to prevent lockup) helps, but it can't invent grip — it only manages the grip you have.
  • Steer smoothly. Big, sudden steering movements break traction. Plan your turns early and make them gradual.

Leave earlier than you need to. Rushing is the enemy of gentle inputs, and almost every "I'm fine, it's just a little snow" story involves someone running late.

Following distance: give yourself a real buffer

In dry conditions, a couple of seconds of following distance is the norm. On snow or ice, that isn't enough — stopping distances stretch enormously. The practical guidance: double or triple your normal following distance, and more than that on ice.

Pick a fixed object (a sign, a bridge) and count the seconds between the car ahead passing it and you passing it. On a snowy road, you want a long count — six seconds or more. It will feel like a lot. That's the point: the buffer is what lets you brake gently instead of slamming, and gentle is what keeps you in control.

What to do if you slide

It happens to careful drivers too — a patch of black ice, a slushy rut, a corner taken a touch too fast. General guidance if the car starts to slide:

  1. Stay calm and don't slam the brakes. Hard braking usually makes a slide worse. Ease off the gas instead.
  2. Steer gently in the direction you want to go. If the rear of the car is swinging out to the right, steer right — look where you want the car to go, not at what you're afraid of hitting.
  3. Keep your inputs small. Overcorrecting — cranking the wheel the other way — is how a small slide becomes a spin. Small steering, small throttle, patience.

If you end up off the road, don't panic-rev your way out. Rock the car gently (a little forward, a little back), put your traction aid under the drive wheels, and if it won't move, accept the tow. A calm call for help is always cheaper than a damaged car.

Know your car's systems — roughly.

Traction control and stability control help in slides, but they work within the available grip; they can't override physics. All-wheel drive helps you go, not stop — it does nothing for braking on ice. And if your car behaves strangely in winter (warning lights, odd noises), have a qualified mechanic check it rather than hoping it holds.

The things people forget

  • Don't use cruise control on slippery roads. You want your foot controlling speed at all times.
  • Watch for black ice — it looks like wet pavement. Bridges, overpasses, and shaded spots freeze first and thaw last.
  • Clear the whole car before driving: windshield, windows, mirrors, lights, and the roof. Driving with a porthole scraped in the frost is dangerous and, in many places, illegal.
  • Check conditions before you go. Road reports and weather forecasts exist for this. If the forecast says the storm peaks at 5pm, don't be on the highway at 5pm.
  • Keep the tank half full or more — you never want to be the car that ran out of fuel in a snowstorm traffic jam.

The bottom line

Winter driving is just regular driving with the margins widened: gentler everything, much more following distance, no cruise control, and a calm plan for the slide you hope never happens. These are general habits for everyday driving — if you want real winter-driving skill, a defensive driving course in your area is money well spent.
