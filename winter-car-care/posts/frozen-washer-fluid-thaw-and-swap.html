Frozen Windshield Washer Fluid: Thawing the Lines and Switching to Winter Fluid

Fluids & Wipers · 6 min read

Published October 30, 2026 · Last updated October 30, 2026

The usual first move — pour something warm on it and keep pulling the washer stalk — treats the wrong problem, because “the washer fluid” is not one place. A washer system freezes in three separate locations, each with its own fix, and the location that froze most often is not the big plastic reservoir under the hood. It is the smallest one: the nozzles. Heat applied to the reservoir does nothing for a nozzle plugged with ice, and a stalk held down against a frozen line only works the pump harder. This guide runs the job in the order that works: diagnose the location, thaw it gently, then swap the fluid properly so the system is not frozen again by the next cold night.

The spec that decides everything: what is in the reservoir now

Washer fluid freezes at the temperature its mixture dictates. Summer fluids are water-based, with a freezing point near that of plain water — approximately 0°C. Winter fluids carry a much higher concentration of alcohol, typically methanol or ethanol, and are commonly rated from about −29°C to −40°C. Transport Canada’s winter-preparation guidance lands at the protective end of that range: fill up with winter washer fluid in the −40°C temperature range, and carry an extra jug in the vehicle.

The second half of the spec is the trap. Dilution raises a mixture’s freezing point, so a reservoir that was half summer fluid and got topped up with −40°C winter fluid is not a −40°C system — it is an unlabelled mixture, and it may still freeze in moderate winter temperatures. That is why the swap at the end of this guide is a drain-and-refill, not a top-up.

Diagnose first: reservoir, lines, or nozzles

NozzlesPump runs, little or nothing reaches the glass; or fluid dribbles weakly from one nozzle while the other stays blockedThe jets sit exposed to cold air, and their narrow openings block first
Lines / hosesPump runs, no spray at all, reservoir confirmed liquidThin tubes run through unheated areas of the car; a frozen line anywhere along the path stops flow completely
ReservoirFluid surface is solid or slushy when the cap comes off; little or no pump sound reaching fluidThe largest volume of the wrong mixture — summer fluid, or winter fluid diluted with water

Start with the cap-off look at the reservoir, because it takes ten seconds and rules the biggest volume in or out. If the reservoir is liquid and the pump runs, work outward: nozzles next, then the lines. One caution governs every test in this section — run the pump in short bursts only. Frozen fluid blocks the lines and nozzles, and a pump kept running against that blockage can be overworked or burned out.

The thaw procedure, in order

  1. Move the car somewhere warm, if that option exists. Parking in a heated garage or underground parking for several hours is the most straightforward thaw there is: ambient warmth melts the frozen fluid in the reservoir and the lines slowly, with no thermal shock to any plastic part.
  2. If the car stays outside, warm the reservoir gently. A household hair dryer aimed carefully at the reservoir accelerates the thaw — apply the heat gently and keep it moving, to avoid damaging the plastic reservoir or nearby components. Warm, not hot, is the rule everywhere in this procedure.
  3. Lower the mixture’s freezing point from inside. Once some liquid exists in the reservoir, adding concentrated winter washer fluid — or isopropyl alcohol — depresses the freezing point of what is already in there, helping the remaining ice melt and become pumpable.
  4. Clear the nozzles directly. Nozzles often stay blocked after the reservoir is liquid. Warm (not boiling) water poured over them, a hair dryer on a low setting, or a warm damp cloth pressed against them thaws the plug; de-icer or rubbing alcohol applied to the nozzle melts localised ice. A sewing needle or safety pin can be inserted gently into the nozzle opening to dislodge ice or debris.
  5. Free the lines by hand where they are reachable. Visible hose sections, particularly along the underside of the hood, can retain ice after everything else has thawed. Gently squeezing those sections by hand can break up the internal ice without damaging the hose.
  6. Test in short pulses. Try the washer switch intermittently, in short bursts. When the fluid is liquid, spray in short pulses to move it through the lines and clear the nozzles.

What success looks like: both nozzles deliver a full, even spray pattern onto the glass, the reservoir holds liquid winter fluid, and the system still sprays normally the next cold morning — not just in the warm garage where it thawed.

The one hard don’t: no boiling water.

Boiling water on frozen washer parts risks exactly the damage the thaw is meant to avoid — thermal shock can crack plastic and damage seals. Lukewarm water and low heat take longer and leave every part intact. A cracked reservoir turns a free thaw into a parts-and-labour repair.

The swap: drain, refill to the −40 class, purge the lines

A thaw that ends with the same diluted mixture still in the system is a postponement, not a repair. Once the system sprays:

  1. Drain the reservoir. Run the old fluid out through the washers or draw it out — the point is that the summer or diluted mixture leaves, rather than being diluted further by good fluid.
  2. Refill with winter fluid in the −40°C range, per the Transport Canada guidance above. Read the rating on the jug, not the colour of the fluid.
  3. Purge the lines. Run the washers until the spray has clearly cycled the new fluid through the lines and nozzles — the old mixture sitting in the tubing is the part that froze last time, and it freezes again if it stays.
  4. Carry the extra jug. Transport Canada’s guidance includes keeping a spare container of winter fluid in the vehicle, which turns a roadside empty-reservoir problem into a two-minute stop.

When it is a shop job

Book the car in if the pump makes no sound at all with a liquid reservoir (an electrical or pump fault, not a freeze), if a hose or the reservoir is visibly cracked and leaking after a freeze, or if the system freezes repeatedly with verified −40°C fluid in it — that pattern has a cause beyond the fluid choice, and it is faster to find in a shop than in a driveway. Everything short of that list is the procedure above: locate the freeze, warm it gently, and finish by changing what the system is full of.
