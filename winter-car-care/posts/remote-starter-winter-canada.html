Remote Starters in Winter in Canada: Battery Drain, Idling Rules, and Safe Use

Engine & Electrical · 7 min read

Published October 30, 2026 · Last updated October 30, 2026

0.25 litres. That is what Natural Resources Canada (NRCan) says every 10 minutes of idling costs the average vehicle with a 3-litre engine — over a quarter of a litre of fuel burned going nowhere, before the drive begins. A remote starter is, mechanically, a machine for spending that fuel from indoors. Used with its runtime set deliberately, it buys clear glass and a cabin that does not numb your hands. Used on its factory runtime out of habit, it can burn more fuel than the trip, flatten a marginal battery over a parked week, and — in some Canadian cities — run an unoccupied car well past the local idling limit. All three problems are settings and measurement problems, which means all three are fixable.

The winter math: what a season of remote starts actually burns

NRCan’s warm-up guidance sets the baseline: with today’s computer-controlled engines, even on cold winter days, no more than two to three minutes of idling is usually enough warm-up before driving, and the best way to warm a vehicle up is to drive it — the transmission, bearings, steering, and tires only warm in motion anyway. Against that baseline, run the arithmetic on a common habit:

The same NRCan page adds the restart comparison: more than ten seconds of idling uses more fuel than restarting the engine, and the break-even point for starter and battery wear is under 60 seconds. It also addresses remote starters directly — its advice is to minimize their use, because they encourage starting the vehicle before the driver is ready to leave. None of that makes the device useless. It makes the runtime setting the whole decision: the difference between a 3-minute and a 15-minute setting, compounded over a winter, is roughly 36 litres burned in a driveway.

Where the goal is an easier cold start rather than a warm cabin, NRCan’s alternative is a block heater on a timer — two hours plugged in is more than enough, and at −20°C a block heater can improve overall fuel economy by as much as 10 percent.

The rules are municipal — and they do not agree with each other

There is no single Canadian idling rule. Idling is regulated city by city, and the three sourced examples below span a five-fold range — which is why any article (or neighbour) quoting one number as “the” limit should be checked against your own city’s by-law page.

The practical reading: a remote start is, by definition, an unoccupied vehicle idling. In Ottawa that combination carries the strictest limit in the table. In Vaughan, a cold-morning warm-up may fall inside the heating exemption. The device is the same; the legality is local. These are general summaries, not legal advice — read your municipality’s current by-law before treating any runtime as compliant.

Battery drain, measured instead of guessed

A remote starter can flatten a battery two different ways, and only one of them is the starter’s fault.

The standing draw. The module has to listen for the fob signal while the car is off, so it draws a small, continuous quiescent current — parasitic draw, in the trade’s vocabulary. In a properly installed system that draw should be only a few milliamps. The benchmark for the vehicle as a whole: total parasitic drain on most modern vehicles is generally considered acceptable below 50 mA. A remote starter that itself draws 20 to 30 mA is already signalling a problem with the unit or its wiring, and a malfunctioning module drawing 100 mA or more can deplete a battery in a matter of days. An older battery with reduced capacity reaches that point faster — which is why a drain problem so often “appears” in the first cold month.

The usage pattern. Short winter trips after a long remote start spend charge on cranking and cabin heat and repay only part of it before the car is parked again. The starter did not cause that arithmetic, but it lengthens the spending side of it. The installer guidance is blunt on a related point: a remote starter will not fix a weak battery — it will only schedule its failure for a colder morning.

The diagnostic order, when a battery keeps going flat:

  1. Check resting voltage first. A fully charged 12-volt battery measures approximately 12.6 volts or higher with the engine off. Well under that, charge and load-test the battery before blaming any accessory.
  2. Measure the draw, do not guess it. A parasitic-draw test — a multimeter in series with the battery, read after the car’s modules have gone to sleep — gives a number to compare against the below-50 mA total benchmark.
  3. Isolate by fuse. If the total is high, pulling fuses one at a time while watching the meter identifies the circuit; if the reading drops when the remote starter’s fuse comes out, the module or its installation is the fault.

What success looks like: resting voltage at about 12.6 V or above, a total sleep draw under the 50 mA benchmark, and a car that still starts after a parked week in January.

Safety rules that outrank convenience.

Municipal guidance is explicit on the human side: never leave children, elderly passengers, or pets unattended in an idling vehicle — carbon monoxide risk is the stated reason — and do not sit in an idling vehicle yourself. A remote-started car is an unoccupied running car; it belongs outdoors, never in a closed garage, and the drive should begin when the runtime ends, not a quarter-hour later.

Set it up once, then use it on a short leash

  • Set the shortest runtime that clears the glass. NRCan’s two-to-three-minute warm-up guidance is the reference point; anything beyond it is cabin comfort, and should be a conscious purchase measured against your city’s limit, not a default.
  • Prefer two-way confirmation. A one-way system only sends the start command; a two-way system confirms the vehicle actually started — the difference between assuming and knowing on a −25°C morning.
  • Treat installation as electrical work. Poor installation is a documented source of no-start conditions, warning lights, battery drain, and intermittent operation. On a newer vehicle, professional installation is the safer route.
  • Start it when you are actually leaving. Boots-on, bag-by-the-door timing keeps the runtime honest — the pattern NRCan flags, starting early “just in case,” is where the fuel table above comes from.
  • Decide in fall, not at −20°C. If the battery is old, trips are short, and the car sits for days, the framework says fix the battery and shorten the runtime first — the starter is the last variable, not the first.
