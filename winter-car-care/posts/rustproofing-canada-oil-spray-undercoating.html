Rustproofing in Canada: Oil Spray, Undercoating, and What Actually Stops Salt Damage

Rustproofing · 6 min read

Published October 27, 2026 · Last updated October 27, 2026

$150 to $170 per year. That is the retrieved Canadian price for an oil-spray treatment on a car or small SUV, with trucks and vans at $180 to $200 per year. Those figures come from one Canadian pricing source checked on October 25, 2026; the manufacturer and applicator sources retrieved alongside it published no prices at all. Treat the numbers as a planning range from a single source, not as a market survey — and treat the annual part as the real cost driver, because oil spray is a repeat purchase, not a one-time treatment.

The five- and ten-year columns are simple arithmetic on the annual range, and they are the honest way to compare an annual oil spray against any product sold as a single application: the comparison only works at the same time horizon. One retrieved applicator comparison makes a related point from the other direction — the product matters less than consistent annual application. A cheaper treatment applied every year and an expensive one applied once are not the same purchase, whatever the first invoice says.

Read the table by mechanism. Creeping products are sold on reaching the seams and spot welds where a surface coating cannot go. Barrier products are sold on coverage of exposed surfaces. Those are different claims about different places on the car, which is why brand-versus-brand comparisons that never name a mechanism settle so little.

Oil spray: the creep argument, the drip, the drilled holes

The manufacturer describes its petroleum-based product as creeping into seams, crevices, and spot welds, and as self-healing where it is disturbed. It is sprayed inside doors, rocker panels, and wheel wells, into hood and trunk seams, and across the underbody. Because the claim is penetration, access matters: the pricing source notes that access holes are drilled in door jambs and rockers and fitted with plugs. A buyer who objects to drilled holes on principle should settle that question before booking, not at the counter.

Two practical costs come with the treatment. Application takes about 30 to 60 minutes. The treatment then drips for roughly 48 hours afterwards. Neither is a defect; both belong in the plan for the week, including where the car parks for those two days.

Plan the parking spot before the appointment.

Barrier coatings and wax: where the evidence thins

The case against hard undercoating in the retrieved sources is specific: it cannot penetrate seams, and it can crack, trapping moisture against the metal. That case comes from the oil-spray manufacturer, an interested source, and no retrieved independent source tested it. The fair reading is narrow — a barrier that does not reach a seam does not protect that seam, and any coating, whatever its type, should be inspected where it has cracked. Wax-based systems sit in a thinner file still: they appear among Canadian applicator options, but the retrieved sources offer no mechanism, price, or performance description to rank them on, so this guide does not rank them.

Electronic modules: an open evidence question

Electronic rust modules are sold in Canada, and the retrieved rustproofing sources — manufacturer, pricing, and applicator comparison alike — do not assess them at all. That absence is the finding. A module purchase currently rests on the seller's claim alone, and a reader weighing one can ask a simple question before paying: what test, run by someone other than the seller, shows protected and unprotected panels side by side after salt exposure? Until a source answers it, the module belongs in a different budget category from the treatments above — a claim awaiting evidence, not a mechanism with a track record in the sources at hand.

Timing, and the decision by ownership horizon

This is a buying-decision guide, not a do-it-yourself procedure: application is the applicator's job, so there are no home steps to follow. The owner-level decisions are which mechanism, whether the drilled access holes are acceptable, and whether the annual appointment will actually happen — scheduled ahead of the season's first salt, and repeated, since the applicator position retrieved for this guide is that consistent annual application matters more than the product chosen.

  • Keeping the car a long time: the ten-year column is the real price — $1,500 to $2,000 at the retrieved range. Judge the creep claim on that horizon: seams and spot welds are exactly the places a surface-only treatment is argued not to reach.
  • Selling within a few years: five years of applications cost $750 to $1,000. The decision turns on whether repeated annual appointments fit the calendar at all; a system that depends on annual renewal and gets renewed once delivers once.
  • Unwilling to accept drilled holes or two days of dripping: say so at the start. Those two facts, both from the pricing source, rule the standard oil-spray application in or out faster than any brand comparison.
  • Offered a module at the sales desk: apply the evidence question above before adding it to the bill. Nothing in the retrieved research either confirms or refutes it — and a purchase that size deserves better than silence.
