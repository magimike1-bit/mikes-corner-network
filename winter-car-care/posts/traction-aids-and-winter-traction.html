Traction Aids in Winter: Sand, the Cat Litter Myth, Traction Boards, and When Each One Helps

Driving · 7 min read

Every winter, the same trunk-kit advice gets repeated: keep sand in the car, or cat litter, or buy a set of traction boards. What the advice rarely explains is that these aids are not interchangeable. Each one works on a different surface, fails in a different way, and helps only if it goes under the right tires. This guide compares the main options honestly — including the cat litter myth — and ends with a simple framework for deciding when an aid is worth trying and when the smarter move is to stop and call for help.

First, the part no aid replaces

Before anything goes under a tire, three free steps do most of the work. Clear the snow away from in front of and behind the drive wheels, making a short, clean runway — even a foot or two of cleared path changes the odds. Clear snow that is higher than the car's ground clearance from around the vehicle as well, because a car resting on packed snow under its floor cannot move no matter how much grip the tires find. Then use gentle inputs: light throttle, and a slow rocking motion between drive and reverse if there is room. Wheelspin is the enemy — a spinning tire polishes ice, melts snow into a slicker layer, and digs the car deeper. Turning traction control off can help in deep snow because it allows some controlled slip, but the throttle stays gentle either way. Only when the runway is clear and the car almost moves does a traction aid earn its moment.

Two points in that table deserve emphasis. First, none of the granular aids melts anything. Sand and litter work — when they work — by giving the tread particles to grip, the same way gravel helps on a slick path. Melting is salt's job, and salt is a road treatment, not a get-unstuck tool for a car that is already stuck. Second, placement beats quantity: aids go under the drive tires — the front pair on a front-wheel-drive car, the rear pair on a rear-wheel-drive car, and any pair on an all-wheel-drive car, though even then the tires still need something to grip. A generous pile under a tire that does not drive the car accomplishes nothing.

The cat litter myth, sorted out

Cat litter is the most repeated tip and the most misunderstood. The claim travels in two forms: that litter absorbs moisture and so creates traction, and that any bag will do. The problem is what most litter is made of — clay. Forum discussions from drivers who tried it are blunt: once clay litter gets wet it turns into a gluey layer that can be as slippery as the surface it was meant to fix, and clumping or scoopable litter — engineered to turn wet on purpose — is the worst version of the idea. The non-clumping, cheapest clay type is the least bad, holding its granular shape a little longer, but it is still a pinch-hitter, not a first choice. Coarse traction sand does the same job, keeps working when wet, and is sold for exactly this purpose. The practical verdict: if litter is what happens to be in the trunk, use the non-clumping kind, use it on a thin slick layer rather than in deep snow, and do not expect a second attempt from the same patch — once it is wet and flattened, it is done. If buying something to carry all winter, buy sand.

Where traction boards earn their trunk space

Traction boards — including the lighter mats sold for cars — work on a different principle from grit. Instead of adding particles, they give the tire a solid, studded surface to climb onto, spread the vehicle's weight over a larger area so the tire stops digging, and bridge over the soft spot that swallowed the tire in the first place. That makes them the strongest option in snow and slush, and they double as a makeshift shovel for clearing the runway. Their limits are real, though. On smooth, hard ice a board has little to key into and can skate. A heavy right foot can spit a board out behind the tire, which is both a failure and a hazard to anyone standing there — nobody should ever stand in line with a drive wheel during an attempt. And boards sized for off-road trucks are bulky; the compact mats made for cars are the realistic trunk item, best in light snow and slush when the car is stuck but not deeply buried.

Using any aid: the short procedure

  1. Stop spinning the wheels. Every extra second of spin makes the hole deeper and the surface slicker.
  2. Clear the runway. Shovel snow from in front of and behind the drive tires and from under the car's floor.
  3. Place the aid tight against the drive tires, in the direction you intend to travel, wedged slightly under the tread.
  4. Straighten the front wheels unless a slight angle is clearly needed to reach firmer ground.
  5. Ease onto the throttle — the gentlest pressure that moves the car — and keep going, gently, until the car reaches firm ground. Stopping halfway up an icy patch usually means starting over.
  6. Retrieve the aid if it is safe to do so, and do not stop on the next slick patch to celebrate.

What success looks like: the car rolls out under light throttle without wheelspin, reaches a surface where it can keep moving, and the driver keeps that momentum — slowly — to properly cleared road. Success is not a dramatic roar out of the hole; drama is how boards get launched and tires get dug back in.

The decision framework: try, retry, or call

  • Try an aid when the car is nearly free — it rocks a little, the runway can be cleared, and the stuck spot is short: a drifted driveway exit, an unplowed parking space, a slick patch at a stop.
  • Retry once, differently, if the first attempt almost worked: more clearing, the aid repositioned, or grit added at the point where the tire first bites.
  • Stop and call for help when the car is resting on snow packed under its floor, when wheels are dug to the hubs, when the car is in a live traffic lane, or when two honest attempts have made things worse. A tow is cheaper than a damaged transmission, an overheated clutch, or an injury.
  • Skip the air-out-of-the-tires trick. It is sometimes suggested as a way to widen the tire's contact patch for a short distance, but the air must be replaced immediately afterward, and driving on a deliberately soft tire risks the tire and the wheel. For an everyday driver without a way to reinflate on the spot, it is a bad trade.

FAQ

Does sand melt ice?
No. Sand and litter provide grip only. Anything that melts ice is a de-icer, and de-icers work on a timescale of minutes to hours on a surface you are about to drive over — not the seconds a stuck car needs.

Which tires get the aid on an all-wheel-drive car?
Any driven pair can work, but start with the end that has the clearest, shortest path to firm ground, and clear that path first.

What belongs in the trunk, then?
A small shovel first, a bag of coarse sand second, and compact traction mats if space allows. That order is deliberate: clearing beats grit, and grit beats gadgets.

The bottom line

Traction aids help at the margins, after shoveling, with a gentle right foot. Sand is the honest granular choice, cat litter is a myth with a small kernel of truth, boards are the strongest tool in soft snow, and knowing when to stop is an aid in itself.
