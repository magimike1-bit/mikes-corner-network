A Windshield Chip Before Winter: Why the First Cold Morning Can Run It, and the Repair Decision to Make Now

Visibility & glass · 7 min read

Published November 4, 2026 · Last updated November 4, 2026

The chip has been there since fall, small and quiet, the kind of damage a driver means to deal with eventually. Then the first hard frost arrives. The car starts, the defroster comes up to clear the glass, and sometime during that ordinary warm-up a line walks out of the chip toward the edge of the windshield. Nothing hit the car that morning. The cold did not break sound glass either. What happened is narrower and more mechanical: the temperature difference across the glass found the weak point that was already there, and the weak point let go.

Two mechanisms do the damage

The temperature gradient. A windshield is two sheets of glass bonded to a plastic layer in the middle, and the two faces do not move together when their temperatures differ. Safelite's explanation, quoted in a Manitoba dealer guide, puts it this way: in cold weather the outer surface of the windshield cools and contracts while the inner surface, warmed by the car's heater, stays relatively warm, and that difference creates tension across the glass. Add the defroster on high and the gradient sharpens. In the same quoted guidance, pointing high heat directly at a cold windshield creates an intense temperature gradient, and if damage exists, that stress often causes it to spread immediately. Undamaged glass absorbs the tension. A chip is a break in the outer sheet with small fractures running off it, and each one is a place where the tension can concentrate.

Water, frozen in place. A chip that sat open through fall rain takes on moisture. Safelite notes that if moisture gets into a chip or crack and freezes, the expansion of the ice forces the glass apart from the inside. Fall in most of Canada is a nightly rehearsal of exactly that: above freezing in the afternoon, below it overnight. Safelite's repair guidance adds the practical half of the same fact, that water in the damaged area can complicate the repair itself, and in cold weather can freeze and expand the damage overnight.

Repair or replace: the decision table

Two sets of numbers circulate, and they do not match, because they are different kinds of document. Safelite's are a repairer's assessment rules. Manitoba Public Insurance publishes an actual criterion for what it will repair. Your province and your insurer may set their own; the table shows both so the difference is visible rather than smoothed over.

One cost of landing in the replacement column rarely gets mentioned in chip advice. MPI's glass page notes that a full replacement may require recalibration of the vehicle's driver assistance systems, the camera behind the mirror. A repair, by contrast, is a resin fill that Safelite says takes most shops 30 minutes or less. That gap, a short visit now versus new glass plus a calibration later, is the whole financial argument for acting while the damage is still small.

The tape disagreement, stated plainly

Advice on the simplest interim step contradicts itself across the top results. A Manitoba dealer guide advises covering the chip with clear tape to keep water and grit out until it is filled, quoting a broker's version of the same advice: dry the area off and cover the chip with tape to keep it dry and clean. Safelite's own rock chip FAQ says the opposite, in so many words: do not cover it with tape. Neither source is careless; they are optimising for different risks, grit and moisture in the break on one side, and whatever tape leaves behind or traps on the other. The goal both sides agree on is in Safelite's own list: keep the damage dry. So the usable rule is this. Keep water out of the chip by the means your repairer prefers, and if you have already booked the repair, ask the shop which side of the tape question they want you on. Do not let the disagreement delay the booking.

The cold morning protection procedure

  1. Photograph the damage today. Safelite advises photos from multiple angles before anything else, for the insurance record and for your own check on whether it has grown.
  2. Keep the chip dry. Moisture in the break is the freeze mechanism, and it complicates the resin repair as well.
  3. Start the defroster gentle. Fan low to medium, temperature warm rather than hot, and let the glass come up with the cabin. Never aim a high heat blast at the damaged area.
  4. Never pour hot water on the glass. The dealer guide cites AAA's warning that hot water can crack glass through thermal shock. On chipped glass it is the fastest route to a replacement.
  5. Scrape around the chip, not across it. Safelite warns that ice scrapers can make chips and cracks worse. Work the scraper away from the damage and let the defroster lift the ice over it.
  6. Skip the car wash until it is repaired. Safelite advises against washing a car with a cracked windshield at all: pressure jets push water into the crack, and hot wash water followed by a cold rinse repeats the thermal stress. If the car must be cleaned, a gentle hand wash at ambient temperature, with no pressure aimed at the crack, is the safest option in its guidance.
  7. Close doors gently if the damage sits near the edge. The dealer guide's threshold is 8 inches: damage within that distance of the edge gets door slams transmitted through the body and the glass.
  8. Park indoors where you can. A garage keeps the glass out of the deepest overnight cold and softens the morning swing.
  9. Book the repair now, while the table still says repair. Rock chip damage falls under comprehensive insurance in most cases, per Safelite, and MPI states that Manitoba repair claims do not affect premiums.

What success looks like: morning after morning, the chip in your photograph is the same size as the chip in the windshield, with no new lines running from it, and the car reaches its repair appointment with the damage still in the repair column of the table. Success is not the chip healing. It is the chip not growing.

How long can you wait: the honest answer

There is no safe number of days, and the sources refuse to invent one. A chip can sit for months in the fall and hold, the Manitoba guide says, and it can also run on the first cold morning with the defroster on high. Safelite's version is the same answer in different units: damage can spread within hours or days depending on temperature, road vibration, and moisture exposure, and a chip that is clearly repairable today can become a full crack requiring replacement within a week. Its summary of the waiting period is worth quoting whole: cold weather will continue to stress the crack every day it goes unaddressed. November is when the odds turn. Every protection step above buys time, but time is all it buys.
