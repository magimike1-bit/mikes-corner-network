Winter Headlights and Visibility: The Clean, Check, and Aim Procedure

Visibility & safety · 7 min read

Winter visibility fails quietly. Tires announce their problems; a headlight just gets dimmer, dirtier, and slightly more mis-aimed, week by week, while the days get shorter around it. The official guidance treats lighting as maintenance with a standard, not as a switch: the summary of Transport Canada's advice runs to three words — vehicle lights should be kept clean, in good working condition, and properly aimed. This guide turns those three words into a fall procedure, then adds the beam discipline for the days when clean and aimed still is not enough.

General information only, not professional automotive advice. Aim and electrical faults belong to a qualified shop.

The standard: clean, working, aimed

Each word in the standard catches a different failure. Clean, because dirt, road film, and early slush cut a lamp's output even when the bulb is perfect — the light is made and then filtered by grime. Working, because a failed tail lamp or headlamp is invisible from the driver's seat; the only way to know is to look, which is why the guidance recommends a walk-around check in the driveway. Aimed, because aim decides where the light goes: a lamp aimed too low shortens the driver's useful seeing distance, and one aimed too high adds glare for other drivers without putting light where it is needed. One caution from the same guidance: some newer vehicles with automatic aiming may need professional inspection rather than a driveway adjustment, so the check below ends at detection — correction of an aim fault on such a vehicle is a shop visit, not a screwdriver session.

The fall visibility check

  1. Wash every lens. Headlights, tail lights, indicators, and the license plate light get cleaned, not just wiped with a mitten — road film needs water and a cloth.
  2. Walk-around lamp test. With a helper, or against a wall or garage door at dusk: headlights low and high, tail lights, brake lights, indicators both sides, reverse light, hazards. Any dark corner is a bulb or a fuse, and it gets fixed before the season, not during a storm.
  3. Aim sanity check. On a flat surface facing a wall, both beams should sit at the same height with a clean, level cutoff. One beam climbing or diving is an aim fault — on an automatic-aiming vehicle, that is the professional-inspection trigger.
  4. Clear-the-car habit. Build the rule now, before snow: clearing the car includes lights and license plates, every time. CAA Magazine's winter guidance is blunt about why — it ensures the driver can see and other drivers can see the car, and in Ontario it is the law, with traffic fines for failing to clean a vehicle properly.
  5. Interior fog drill. Fogged windows from inside are a visibility fault too. The documented fix: the air conditioner acts as a dehumidifier, so run it with the heat to clear the glass — a control many drivers never touch in winter.

What success looks like: every lamp on the car lights when it should, both headlight beams sit level and matched on the wall, the lenses are clear enough to read the reflector through them, and the driver can state the defog setting without looking it up. Anything less is a list, and fall is when lists are cheap.

Beam discipline when the weather arrives

The Manitoba Driver's Handbook states the timing law for its province, and it is a good rule anywhere: headlights are required from a half hour before sunset until a half hour after sunrise, and whenever visibility is reduced to 60 metres. Inside that weather, the handbook's reduced-visibility practice is a short, exact sequence. Sharply reduce normal speed. Run low beam, because high beam reflects back as glare in dense fog or snow; fog lamps are better still where fitted. If the car is travelling at greatly reduced speed, turn on the hazard flashers so following drivers read the situation early. And if visibility becomes so poor the driver can barely see, the instruction is to pull cautiously off the pavement and stop until conditions improve — a rule that reframes stopping not as failure but as the correct manoeuvre. CAA's winter guidance adds the standing habit underneath all of it: always use headlights to increase visibility, for the driver's own seeing and for everyone else's.

The two failure stories this check prevents

Ask how winter visibility accidents get described afterward and two stories recur. In the first, the driver never saw the other car in time: a lamp had been out for weeks, invisible from the driver's seat, or the lenses had filmed over so gradually that the reduced output felt like darkness rather than equipment. In the second, the other driver never saw them: tail lamps dark, the rear of the car snow-caked from a clearing job that stopped at the windshield, plates and lights left under the one layer of snow that mattered most. Both stories are maintenance stories wearing accident costumes. The walk-around check exists because the driver's seat is the one place in the car from which none of this can be seen — every lamp on the vehicle faces away from the person responsible for it. Fifteen minutes with a cloth, a wall, and a helper is the entire cost of retiring both stories for the season, which is why the guidance states the standard as a standing condition — clean, working, aimed — rather than as a repair to schedule after something goes wrong.

Daytime is not exempt

A final note on the hours outside the headlight law's window. Winter daylight in Canada is short, grey, and frequently wet, and visibility inside it fails by degrees: spray on a highway, a snow squall between towns, dusk arriving an hour earlier than the driver's habits expect. The standing advice from CAA's winter guidance — always use headlights to increase visibility, for the driver's own seeing and for everyone else's — costs nothing and covers the whole grey middle of the day that the sunset rule does not reach. The habit to build in fall is simple: lights on with the engine, in any weather that makes the road shine, spray, or disappear. By the time the season's first storm makes the decision feel urgent, the decision should already be a reflex.

FAQ

Do daytime running lights count? They mark the front of the car; they are not the headlight system the guidance means. When visibility drops — the 60-metre rule is a useful personal trigger — switch the full lights on so the tail lamps light too.

High beams in falling snow: yes or no? No. The handbook's reasoning is optical: high beam reflects off dense fog and snow back into the driver's eyes as glare. Low beam, or fog lamps, put the light under the weather instead of into it.

How often should the walk-around happen? Once properly in fall, then as a habit after any bulb warning, any front-end nudge, or any storm that cakes the car — the failures the check catches are exactly the ones the driver's seat hides.
