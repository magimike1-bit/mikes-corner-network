Bleeding Radiators and Boiler Pressure: Fix Cold Tops and Gurgling

System care · Published 2026-10-23 · Last updated 2026-10-23

General information only. Work on a heating system only within the manufacturer's instructions for your boiler. If pressure behaves unusually, water is discoloured or leaking, or you are unsure at any step, stop and call a qualified heating professional — and never open a hot, pressurised system.

A radiator that is hot at the bottom and cold at the top is not mysterious, and it is not dying. Air, which rises, has collected where the hot water wants to be. Bleeding the radiator releases that air. But bleeding is only half the job, and the half-finished version — bleed every radiator, never check the boiler — is how a ten-minute fix becomes a house with no heat: every bleed lets a little water out of a sealed system, the pressure falls, and below a threshold the boiler simply declines to run. This guide treats bleeding and pressure as the single job they actually are.

First, confirm air is really the fault

Three symptoms point three different ways. Cold at the top, warm at the bottom, sometimes with gurgling is trapped air — this article's job. Cold at the bottom, hot at the top points the other way: sludge settling in the radiator, which bleeding cannot fix and which usually needs a professional clean. One radiator cold everywhere while others are hot suggests a closed or stuck valve, or a balancing problem, before it suggests air. Bleeding a system whose fault is sludge wastes an afternoon and drops the pressure for nothing, so the two-minute diagnosis comes first. Run the heating, map which radiators are cold and where on each one, and only then reach for the bleed key.

The procedure: bleed, then re-pressurise, then verify

  1. Turn the heating off and let radiators cool — twenty to thirty minutes. This protects you from hot water and lets air finish rising to the top of each radiator.
  2. Note the boiler pressure before you start, cold. Many residential hydronic systems sit around 12 to 20 psi; sealed systems commonly read about 1.0 to 1.5 bar cold. Your starting number is the target you will restore at the end — and if it is already low before bleeding, pressure, not air, may be the real fault.
  3. Start with the highest radiators. Air collects at the top of the system, so top-floor radiators and any panel that habitually runs cool come first, working downward. (Guides that say "farthest from the boiler first" are solving circulation order for stubborn systems; height-first is the defensible default because that is where the air physically is. If a low radiator still misbehaves afterwards, bleed it on the second pass.)
  4. At each radiator: container and cloth under the bleed valve — usually a small fitting at the top corner — then open slowly, a quarter to a half turn at most, with the bleed key or a flat screwdriver. A hiss is air leaving. The moment a steady stream of water replaces sputtering, close the valve snugly. Do not overtighten; the small tapered seat damages easily.
  5. Check pressure after every few radiators, not just at the end. Bleeding removes volume, and the gauge will fall as you work.
  6. Restore pressure per your boiler's instructions — typically via the filling loop or fill valve — bringing a sealed system back to roughly 1.2 bar cold (within the usual 1.0-1.5 bar cold range), added slowly in small amounts rather than one surge. Overfilling invites the relief valve to discharge and can hide a real problem.
  7. Turn the heating on and verify radiator by radiator. The bled radiators should now heat evenly, top to bottom, without gurgling. Pressure will rise as the system warms — up to around 2.0 bar is typical on a sealed system.

What success looks like: every radiator hot across its whole surface, a quiet system, cold pressure back at its starting value, and warm pressure steady — no slow fall over the following days.

The warnings that change the job

Bleeding should be an occasional task — at the start of the heating season, or when a cold top appears. Patterns outside that rhythm are diagnostics, not chores. Needing to top up every few days means water is leaving the system somewhere; repeated topping-up also dilutes any corrosion inhibitor, and guidance is explicit that frequent re-pressurising points to a deeper issue. One radiator needing bleeding again and again means air is entering or being generated in the system — a leak, a failing component, or corrosion producing gas — not that the radiator is forgetful. Black or sludgy water at the bleed valve upgrades the job from bleeding to system health: note it, and plan a professional assessment rather than repeated bleeds. And pressure that climbs too high when hot, or a relief valve that drips, ends the DIY scope on the spot. In each case the correct move is the same: restore safe pressure if the manual tells you how, stop, and book the heating professional — with your notes on which radiator, how often, and what the gauge did, which is exactly the information that makes their visit short.

Keeping air out between seasons

Air enters hydronic systems slowly — through microscopic leaks, after any work that opens the loop, and as water chemistry generates gas over years. The habits that minimise it are unglamorous: bleed once at the start of the season rather than repeatedly through it, fix weeping valve glands instead of wiping them, keep the pressure where the manual puts it rather than habitually high, and have the system serviced on the manufacturer's rhythm so inhibitor levels and the expansion vessel get professional eyes. Done that way, bleeding stays what it should be — a five-minute seasonal ritual with a pressure check attached — instead of a recurring symptom you learn to live with while the underlying fault compounds.

Baseboards, convectors, and panels: the same air, different fittings

Everything above applies beyond the classic column radiator. Hydronic baseboard runs trap air at their high points and usually carry a small bleed fitting at one end of the element, opened the same quarter-turn way with the same hiss-then-water signal; a baseboard that gurgles along its length is announcing the same fault in a longer shape. Panel radiators hide their bleed valve on the rear top corner, which is why owners sometimes conclude theirs has none — it is worth one careful look with a torch before assuming a call-out is needed. Convector cabinets need their covers lifted to reach the coil's bleeder, and the dust that comes out with the cover is itself worth removing, since a choked convector mimics an air problem by heating weakly. In every case the closing steps do not change: pressure checked as you go, restored cold to its starting value, heat back on, and each unit verified hot across its whole surface. The fitting differs; the physics, and the half-finished-job trap, are identical.

Questions homeowners ask about bleeding radiators

How often should radiators be bled? At the start of the heating season, and whenever a specific radiator develops a cold top. More often than that, treat the frequency itself as the symptom.

Should the heating be on or off while bleeding? Off, and cooled. Bleeding a hot, running system risks scalding water and gives misleading pressure readings; every procedure source agrees on this step.

My pressure reads in psi, not bar — what is normal? Many North American residential systems run about 12 to 20 psi depending on the building's height and design. Your boiler's manual and its gauge markings outrank any general range; restore the value the system had when it was working properly.
