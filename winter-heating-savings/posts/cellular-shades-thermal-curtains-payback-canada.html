Cellular Shades and Thermal Curtains: What the Payback Actually Looks Like in Canada

Save daily · Published 2026-11-02 · Last updated 2026-11-02

General information only, not financial advice. Prices were checked on the date shown and change with size, options and sales. Worked examples label every assumption so you can rerun them on your own bill.

40 percent is the number that sells cellular shades, and it is a real number, with a catch. Tightly installed cellular shades can reduce heat loss through windows by 40 percent in winter, according to figures from the US Department of Energy (s2). That is 40 percent of the loss through the glass, not 40 percent off your heating bill. Confusing those two is how a sensible purchase turns into a disappointing one, so the two stay separate all the way to the payback.

The two numbers that get mixed up

Start with the ceiling on what any window covering can do. Natural Resources Canada puts windows and doors together at up to 25 percent of total house heat loss (s1). The US Department of Energy figure quoted in the packet sources is higher for windows alone, about 30 percent of a home heating energy lost through windows (s2). Either way, windows are one slice of the bill, not the bill itself.

Now apply a covering to that slice. A utility guide that rates coverings side by side, from Chelan County PUD, credits tight fitting cellular shades with saving about 10 percent on heating bills, and well fitting double layer drapes with reducing heat loss by up to 10 percent in winter (s3). Read those two lines carefully, because they are not measuring the same thing. The cellular figure is framed as a bill saving. The drape figure is framed as a heat loss reduction. A covering that trims a quarter of your heat loss by a tenth has moved your bill by a few percent, not by a tenth.

That gap is the whole story of this purchase. Cellular shades earn their price through the window slice. Drapes earn theirs mostly through comfort, the cold downdraft off the glass feeling smaller, at a much lower cost.

Why cellular shades sit at the top is structural. The honeycomb pockets trap air, and that trapped air acts as insulation between the glass and the room (s2). A drape works differently, by slowing air movement and cutting radiant loss at the window (s1), which is real but smaller. Neither result is automatic. The same utility guide is blunt that even the best window coverings will not deliver comfort or bill savings if they are not adjusted based on the conditions (s3). A shade that stays open all winter is decoration.

What a window costs in Canada right now

Prices checked 2026-11-02 at Select Blinds Canada. Treat these as starting points, because the final price moves with the exact window size and the options picked:

  • Value Cordless Light Filtering Honeycomb Shade: listed sale price $31.99 (s4).
  • Designer Single Cell Light Filtering Honeycomb Shade: listed sale price $95.99, with a 3/4 inch single cell construction (s5).

Six windows at those listed prices come to about $192 at the value end and about $576 at the designer end, before any size upgrades. Custom sizes, blackout fabrics and motorization push a real order above these figures, which is exactly why the payback below is run as a range instead of a promise.

The payback, run three ways

One labelled assumption drives everything here: a heating bill of $2,000 for the season. That is an example bill, not an average and not a quote. Substitute your own total and the arithmetic still works the same way.

Three readings follow from that table. First, the value tier can pay back in one to three heating seasons even on a modest result. Second, the designer tier needs the strong result, or a larger bill, or a colder house with a lot of glass, to pay back in under three years. Third, a home with a $1,000 heating bill halves every saving in the table and doubles every payback, which is where this purchase quietly stops making sense on money alone.

The conditions the headline assumes

  1. Fit tight. The 40 percent window figure belongs to tightly installed shades (s2). Measure for the closest fit the frame allows and ask about edge gaps before ordering, because air moving freely behind the shade spends the insulation you paid for.
  2. Close them on a schedule. Closed during the coldest hours, open when winter sun can do useful work through south facing glass. The covering only insulates while it covers (s3).
  3. Check condensation in the first cold snap. NRCan treats interior window condensation and frosting as a common winter complaint and discusses lowering indoor humidity as one response (s1). A tight covering changes the air and temperature at the glass, so look behind the shades on the coldest mornings of the first month. Persistent heavy frost is a humidity problem to solve, not a reason to ignore the window.
  4. Do the worst windows first. Large, old or north facing windows in rooms you actually heat are where the window slice is biggest. Bedrooms used all night come before a spare room that already sits cool.

What success looks like: the room beside the window feels less cold at the same thermostat setting, the shades are closed every cold night without a daily argument about it, and the seasonal bill, compared like for like on weather, lands inside one of the scenario rows above rather than beside the 40 percent headline.

When to skip it

Skip the premium tier if your windows are already modern and small, if your heating bill is low, or if the household will not close shades nightly. In those cases the honest buy is the value tier on the two or three worst windows, or double layer drapes where the goal is comfort in one cold sitting room rather than a whole house saving. And if the window frames themselves leak air, seal that first. No covering priced in this guide outperforms a draft that runs underneath it.
