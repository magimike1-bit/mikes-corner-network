Chimney and Wood Stove Winter Prep: Inspect Every Year, Sweep at 1/8 Inch, Burn Dry Wood

Winter prep · Published 2026-10-25 · Last updated 2026-10-25

Safety information only, not a substitute for a certified chimney professional. If you see a chimney fire, call 911. Do not use a chimney or stove you suspect is damaged until a qualified service person has inspected it.

Wood heat fails in a predictable season: the first cold weeks of fall, when a system that sat idle all summer — accumulating creosote residue, moisture, and possibly a nest — is suddenly asked to burn daily. The seasonal advice usually arrives as one blended chore, have your chimney cleaned once a year, and that blending hides how the job actually works. The standards separate three different jobs with three different triggers: an inspection that happens every year regardless of use, a sweep that happens when buildup reaches a stated thickness, and a fuel check that happens every time wood goes into the stove. Late October is when all three come due at once, which is why this is the moment to work through them in order rather than lighting the first fire and hoping the summer was kind.

Three different jobs, three different triggers

The inspection rule comes from NFPA 211, quoted by the Chimney Safety Institute of America: chimneys, fireplaces, and vents shall be inspected at least once a year for soundness, freedom from deposits, and correct clearances, and cleaning, maintenance, and repairs shall be done if necessary. Note the logic — inspection is annual and unconditional, while cleaning is conditional on what the inspection finds. The CSIA's reasoning, quoted by the Massachusetts Chimney Sweep Guild, is that even a little-used chimney can become unsafe over a year: animals may build nests in the flue, or deterioration may develop that makes the chimney unsafe to use. The sweeping trigger, by contrast, is a measurement. CSIA Level 1 inspection guidance instructs technicians to sweep, or recommend sweeping, any chimney with 1/8th inch or greater buildup, and the guild guidance adds that masonry fireplaces should be swept at 1/8 inch of sooty buildup and sooner if any glaze — hardened creosote — is present, because fire-safety experts consider that enough buildup to sustain a chimney fire capable of damaging the chimney or spreading to the home.

The pre-season inspection and sweep procedure

  1. Book the annual inspection before the first fire. Cranbrook Fire & Emergency Services directs residents to clean and inspect chimneys for creosote buildup and any damage prior to their first fire of the season, and to keep checking throughout the burning season. The EPA likewise advises having a certified professional service the wood heater or fireplace annually — and not just relying on a carbon-monoxide alarm.
  2. Understand what a Level 1 inspection covers. Under CSIA guidance, a Level 1 inspection is included with each sweep job. The technician examines the readily accessible portions of the chimney exterior and the accessible portions of the appliance and chimney connection, and must determine that the system is not blocked or significantly restricted, is capable of continued use, and establish the overall cleanliness of the venting system.
  3. Let the measurement decide the sweep. If the inspection finds 1/8 inch or more of buildup, or any glaze, the chimney gets swept — this season, before regular burning, not at some average interval. If it finds less, the inspection record is still the point: it proves the flue was checked for blockage, deposits, and damage while there was time to act.
  4. Act on damage before burning, not after. Creosote buildup can cause chimney fires which damage chimneys and allow smoke, carbon monoxide, and fire to extend beyond the chimney and into living spaces. A cracked liner or a blocked flue found in October is a repair; the same defect found by smoke in December is an emergency.

What success looks like: a completed annual inspection with the flue confirmed free of obstruction and combustible deposits, a sweep performed if the 1/8-inch threshold was met, and no known damage outstanding — all before the first regular fire of the season.

The fuel check: moisture is a number, not a guess

The cheapest efficiency upgrade in wood heating is not a new stove; it is refusing to burn wet wood in the stove already installed. The EPA states that wood burns best when its moisture content is less than 20 percent, and Connecticut DEEP puts the rule as a test: buy an inexpensive moisture meter, test the wood, and only burn wood if the moisture content is 20 percent or less. Wet wood creates excessive smoke and burns inefficiently — in DEEP's phrase, the heat literally goes up in smoke — and the smoke is also what condenses as creosote, so wet fuel quietly manufactures next year's chimney-fire risk while delivering less heat this year. Seasoning is a calendar, not a hope: the EPA advises splitting and seasoning softwood outdoors for at least six months and hardwood for twelve months before burning, noting that burning seasoned wood generates more heat and can result in significant cost savings over the winter. Build hot fires with sufficient air and never let a fire smolder — DEEP is blunt that a smoldering fire is neither safe nor efficient.

The stove itself sets the ceiling on what dry wood can deliver. DEEP, citing EPA figures, notes that newer EPA-certified wood stoves and fireplace inserts, used properly, reduce air pollution by 70 percent compared with older models, are up to 50 percent more energy efficient, use one-third less wood for the same heat, and help lessen the risk of fires by reducing creosote build-up in chimneys. Those are comparison figures between stove generations, not a promise about any single installation — but they explain why an old, smoky stove burning wet wood is the worst cell in the table: it pays the most for fuel, delivers the least heat, and deposits the most creosote while doing it. Some fuel is not a judgement call at all. Never burn painted or pressure-treated wood, ocean driftwood, wood that contains glue such as plywood, household garbage, trash, cardboard, plastics, or foam — the EPA states these emit toxic fumes when burned.

Ashes, chimney fires, and the stop rules

Two rules in this section are not optimisation tips; they are the lines where a heating article becomes a safety article. First, ashes: regularly remove them into a covered metal container, and store that container outdoors on a nonflammable surface. Keep a fire extinguisher handy. Second, the chimney-fire rule from Cranbrook Fire & Emergency Services: if you see a chimney fire, call 911 — and after a chimney fire, have the chimney inspected by a qualified service person prior to its use. No exceptions for it seemed small, and none for the stove still drawing well afterwards; the damage a chimney fire does to a liner is exactly the kind an inspection exists to find. A Canadian note for readers north of the border: fire services there give the same pre-season direction — inspect and clean before the first fires, burn dry seasoned wood, and treat creosote as the fire risk it is — so none of this procedure changes at the border, even though the inspecting professional's certification body may.

DIY or certified sweep: a decision framework

Split the work by consequence. The fuel check is a homeowner job every load: a moisture meter reading takes moments and decides what burns tonight. Ash handling, keeping flammables away from the appliance, and keeping the doors of the appliance closed except when loading or stoking are homeowner jobs every fire. The annual inspection and any sweeping at or past the 1/8-inch threshold belong to a certified professional — the EPA advises certified professional service annually, CSIA guidance frames the Level 1 inspection as a technician's determination about soundness and continued use, and a flue's upper reaches, liner condition, and glaze removal are not assessable from the firebox with a flashlight. The decision rule is therefore simple: measure your wood yourself, and pay a professional to measure your chimney. Both measurements are cheap compared with the failures they prevent, and October is the last month when booking either one is still ahead of the season instead of behind it.

Questions homeowners ask about chimney prep

The chimney was barely used last year. Does it still need inspection? Yes. NFPA 211's annual rule, as quoted by CSIA, applies regardless of use, precisely because animals may nest in an unused flue and deterioration continues whether or not fires are lit.

How much creosote is too much? At 1/8 inch of buildup, CSIA guidance says sweep — and sooner if any glaze is present. That thickness is considered enough to sustain a chimney fire capable of damaging the chimney or spreading to the home.

How can one load of wood be checked quickly? With a moisture meter, the tool both the EPA and DEEP point to. At or below 20 percent moisture, burn it. Above that, it belongs back on the seasoning stack — softwood needs at least six months of seasoning and hardwood at least twelve, per the EPA.

A small chimney fire happened and everything looks fine. Can burning resume? No. Cranbrook Fire & Emergency Services directs that after a chimney fire the chimney must be inspected by a qualified service person before it is used — and during any chimney fire, the instruction is to call 911.
