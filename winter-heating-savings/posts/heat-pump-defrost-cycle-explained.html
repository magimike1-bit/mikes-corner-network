Heat Pump Defrost: Steam, Whooshes and Cooler Air — What Is Normal and What Is Not

Diagnose · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-21 · Last updated 2026-10-21

The first cold, damp morning of the year, thousands of heat pump owners step outside, see their outdoor unit pouring what looks like smoke, hear a whoosh, feel the vents blow cooler — and call for emergency service on a healthy machine. What they are watching is the defrost cycle: the heat pump briefly reversing itself to melt frost off its outdoor coil. Frost is inevitable in this job. The outdoor coil runs colder than the winter air it extracts heat from, so moisture in that air freezes onto it, and the frost layer insulates the coil and chokes airflow until it is removed. Defrost is not a malfunction mode. It is scheduled maintenance the machine performs on itself, and the skill worth learning is telling its normal performance from the handful of signs that genuinely mean trouble.

What defrost looks and sounds like when it is healthy

The sequence is distinctive once you know it. The reversing valve switches the heat pump into cooling mode for a few minutes to warm the outdoor coil (s3). The outdoor fan stops — spinning it would just blow the heat away — and steam or fog rises as the melted frost evaporates into cold air (s3). Lennox adds the soundtrack: a whooshing sound as the system shifts modes is a normal sign of the process at work (s1). Indoors, warm air production pauses briefly, the vents may blow noticeably cooler for a few minutes, and many systems switch on auxiliary heat automatically to prevent temperature swings while the defrost runs (s1). Your thermostat may even show an AUX indicator during those minutes. All of that is the machine behaving as designed.

Frequency follows weather, not the clock. Frost builds fastest in damp air near freezing, so a foggy 1°C evening can produce a defrost every half hour or so, while a dry, deep-cold night may produce almost none — there is little moisture in very cold air to freeze out. Control type matters too: some systems run on timers that check at fixed 30-, 60- or 90-minute intervals, while demand-defrost systems sense frost and cycle only when it is present (s3). A timer system occasionally defrosting a nearly clean coil is wasting a little heat by design; a demand system skipping defrost on a dry night is doing exactly its job. Know which behaviour your unit shows before judging it.

The one-evening observation procedure

  1. Pick the right evening. Choose a damp evening between about -5°C and 3°C, when frost forms fastest. A dry -20°C night will teach you little.
  2. Start a simple log. Note the time each defrost begins (you will hear the whoosh and the fan stop) and the time heating clearly resumes.
  3. Check the coil before and after one cycle. Before: light, even frost is fine. After: the coil should be essentially clear, with meltwater dripping. Photographing both states settles arguments with your own memory later.
  4. Note the indoor side. Record whether auxiliary heat engaged (thermostat indicator) and how long any cooler air lasted.
  5. Do the safe outdoor checks. Clear leaves, snow and debris from around — not inside — the unit, and confirm meltwater can drain away instead of refreezing under the pad. Lennox lists clearing debris among the checks a homeowner can do (s1). Never open the cabinet or touch refrigerant components.
  6. Compare your log with the table above. Cycles inside the normal bands with a clear coil afterward mean a healthy machine. Anything in the right-hand column, repeated, is your service-call evidence — and the log itself is what the technician will ask for.

What success looks like: three or four logged cycles in an evening, each roughly 5–15 minutes, each ending with a clear coil and normal heating — and an owner who no longer flinches at steam. If instead your log shows constant cycling, marathon cycles or ice that survives defrost, you have converted a worry into documentation.

When it is actually a problem

Lennox names the usual suspects behind excessive defrosting: dirty or blocked coils that make frost form faster, faulty sensors or controls that misread conditions, and low refrigerant charge (s1). Add a defrost that never happens at all while ice builds — a unit can fail by silence as well as by excess (s2). These are not homeowner repairs. Refrigerant, sensors and control boards belong to a licensed technician; your contribution is the observation log, the cleared surroundings, and resisting two temptations: do not chip ice off the coil with tools, which bends fins and can puncture refrigerant lines, and do not cover the running unit to 'help' it, which starves the airflow it needs. If the house is cold and the unit is encased, switching the thermostat to emergency or auxiliary heat keeps you warm while you wait for service — at a higher running cost, so treat it as a bridge, not a setting.

Our take: Steam is water, the whoosh is a valve, and the cool few minutes are the price of a clear coil. Learn the numbers, log one damp evening, and you will never again pay an emergency call-out to be told your heat pump was defrosting.

Defrost and your heating bill

Owners sometimes worry that frequent defrost is quietly inflating their electricity bill, and the worry deserves a proportionate answer. A defrost cycle spends a few minutes running the system in reverse and often brings on auxiliary heat, which is the expensive heat source in most systems, so a defrost-heavy week does cost more than a mild one. But the alternative is worse: a coil left frosted loses capacity steadily, runs longer to deliver less heat, and leans on auxiliary heat far more than a unit that clears itself on schedule. Defrost is the cheaper of the two states. The bill signal worth watching is not defrost frequency in damp near-freezing weather — that is weather, billed fairly — but a change in pattern at the same weather: cycles suddenly doubling week over week, auxiliary heat showing on the thermostat at mild temperatures, or a unit that seems to defrost and still cannot keep up. Those are the patterns that connect this guide's observation log to money, and they are exactly the evidence a technician needs to find a dirty coil, a failing sensor or a charge problem before a season of inflated bills explains it the hard way.

FAQ

Why does it steam more some days than others? Steam volume follows frost volume, and frost follows humidity. Damp near-freezing weather is peak defrost season; dry cold produces little frost and little spectacle.

Should the outdoor fan really stop? Yes. The fan stopping while the compressor runs is part of the sequence — moving air would cool the coil the cycle is trying to warm (s3).

Is frost on the unit in the morning a bad sign? A light layer is described by manufacturer guidance as completely normal in cold, humid weather, generally under a quarter inch (s3). Judge it after a defrost cycle: frost that clears is weather; ice that stays is a symptom.
