Heating Oil Tank Winter Care in Canada: The Homeowner Checks and the Jobs That Are Not Yours

System care · Published 2026-11-20 · Last updated 2026-11-20

An oil tank needs very little from its owner in winter, and what it needs is specific: watch for water, watch for leaks, keep the fuel level up, and keep the fill and vent clear. Everything past that line, including cleaning the tank and servicing the equipment attached to it, belongs to certified hands in Ontario, by regulation rather than by preference. Most winter oil failures are not sudden. Water and sludge build quietly at the bottom of the tank for seasons, then announce themselves as a clogged filter and a cold house on the worst weekend of January. The checks below are how a homeowner catches that story early, plus the exact point where the job stops being theirs.

What winter actually does to an oil tank

Water arrives from the air inside the tank. Fluctuations in temperature cause condensation inside the tank, and condensation is more likely when the fuel level is low, because a low tank holds more air to condense. The water sinks below the oil, sits against the steel, and corrodes the tank from the inside until it leaks. Keeping the tank fuller through winter is therefore a corrosion measure, not just a supply habit: a fuller tank leaves less room for the moist air that feeds condensation.

Sludge builds at the bottom. Sludge is a mixture of oil, dirt, water, and other contaminants that accumulates under the fuel. Disturbed sludge, or enough of it, clogs filters and fuel lines and starves the burner. It is also the record of how much water the tank has seen, which is why the filter tells the story early. The oil filter canister acts as an early warning for water damage: rust or sludge showing on the cartridge indicates water in the fuel, long before the tank itself shows damage outside.

Cold works on the outside too. The vent pipe must stay clear of snow, ice, and debris so the tank can breathe during fills and firing. A blocked vent, a buried fill cap, or a delivery path left under a metre of snow are winter problems with no mechanical cause at all.

The homeowner checks, in order

None of these steps opens the tank, disconnects a line, or touches the burner. That is deliberate. This is an inspection routine, done with eyes, a flashlight, and a notebook.

  1. Read the gauge and write it down. A working gauge is the starting point of winter care, and a stuck gauge is itself a defect to report. Record the level monthly so a sudden drop, which can mean a leak or a failing line, stands out against your own normal use.
  2. Walk the tank and look at the bottom and the legs. Check for rust weeping, damp or dark staining on the tank underside, oil smell near the tank, and legs or supports that have shifted or sunk. Look at the ground or floor beneath for fresh staining.
  3. Check the filter canister area for rust and sludge signs. Where the canister and cartridge are visible, rust or sludge on them is the early warning described above. In Ontario this observation is where your part ends: servicing fuel oil equipment is certified work, covered in the next section, so a dirty filter is a service call to book, not a part to swap on a Saturday.
  4. Clear the vent and the fill. Confirm the vent pipe is open and its cap is intact, clear snow and ice away from both vent and fill, and keep a shovelled path so a delivery driver can reach the fill pipe safely.
  5. Top up before deep cold, not during it. Order fuel while the gauge still shows a comfortable reserve. A fuller tank condenses less, and a delivery arranged early is not competing with every other oil house in the county on the coldest day of the year.
  6. Note anything that changed. A new smell, a new stain, a gauge that stopped moving, or a filter that needed attention twice in one winter each go in the notebook with a date. That record is what a technician, a distributor inspector, or an insurer will ask about later.

What success looks like: a steady, explainable gauge record, a dry tank bottom with stable supports, a clear vent, no oil smell, and no repeat filter warnings across the season.

Smell, stain, or a second clogged filter: stop and call

Three findings end the homeowner routine immediately. A fuel oil smell that was not there before, fresh staining under the tank, or a filter warning that returns after service each point at water, sludge, or a leak that inspection from outside cannot size. Do not open fittings to investigate, do not attempt to drain water from the tank bottom, and do not keep resetting a burner that keeps locking out. Those responses turn a service visit into a spill or a damaged burner. Book the certified technician, and if oil has reached soil or a drain, treat it as a spill first and a heating problem second.

The jobs that belong to a certified technician in Ontario

Ontario regulates fuel oil under O. Reg. 213/01, the Fuel Oil regulation made under the Technical Standards and Safety Act, and the TSSA governs the system. Two duties from that framework shape what winter care legally looks like.

First, the work itself is reserved. Under section 4 of the regulation, no person shall install, alter, purge, activate, repair, service, or remove any appliance or equipment used in handling fuel oil unless that person holds a certificate for that purpose. Tank cleaning sits squarely on the certified side of that line, which matches the practical advice in winterization guidance that cleaning is best left to the experts, on a cycle of about once every three years to remove accumulated sludge. Burner service, line work, tank replacement, and removal are likewise certified jobs. Removing an underground tank is narrower still: only a TSSA registered petroleum contractor is qualified to do it, and an underground tank that is 25 years old or of unknown age must be removed.

Second, your distributor carries an inspection duty. Under section 7, a distributor must not supply fuel oil unless satisfied the installation complies with the regulation, and the distributor must have inspected the appliance or work at least once within the previous ten years, or under a quality assurance inspection program, with a report prepared and kept until the next inspection replaces it. That ten year inspection is a condition of supply, separate from your own monthly checks and separate from annual burner service. A home inspection, by contrast, comments on visible condition and does not certify the tank under the regulation. If you cannot name the date of your last distributor inspection, ask your supplier. It is their record to hold and your right to know.

Pin the schedule inside the furnace room door, with the notebook beside it. Winter care for an oil tank is mostly attention, paid on time, plus the discipline to hand the wrench work to the people the regulation names.
