Line-Voltage Thermostats for Baseboard Heat: What the Upgrade Actually Pays Back

Save daily · Published 2026-10-31 · Last updated 2026-10-31

General information only, not financial advice. Prices were checked on the date shown and change without notice. Line-voltage wiring is 120–240 volts: if you are not completely familiar with house wiring, hire a licensed electrician.

$129. That is the manufacturer's own Canadian price, checked October 31, 2026, for a smart thermostat built for electric baseboard heat — and it frames the only question that matters about this upgrade. The thermostat does not make a baseboard heater more efficient; resistance heat converts electricity to heat the same way regardless of what controls it. Every dollar of saving comes from heating rooms less when nobody is in them. So the payback question is really a scheduling question, and it can be answered with one official rule of thumb and your own heating bill.

One exclusion belongs right in the table's shadow: the familiar low-voltage smart thermostats sold for furnaces cannot switch a baseboard circuit at all. Baseboard heat runs at line voltage, 120–240 volts, through the thermostat itself (s4, s5). Any buying guide that prices a furnace thermostat for this job is pricing the wrong product.

Where the saving comes from: the 2-percent rule

Natural Resources Canada's Keeping the Heat In puts the arithmetic plainly: a drop of 1°C over an eight-hour period can save about 2 percent on heating energy (s1). Baseboard heat is unusually well suited to that rule for two reasons that follow from how it works. Each room is its own zone, so a setback can be deep where nobody sleeps and shallow where somebody does. And resistance heat recovers at the same efficiency it runs at — there is no second, more expensive stage waiting to claw the saving back during the morning warm-up.

Against that rule, treat the headline claims carefully. The maker of the smart units above advertises savings of up to 26 percent (s2). "Up to" is doing heavy work in that sentence: a number like that assumes a home that currently heats empty rooms at full temperature around the clock and then schedules aggressively. A household that already turns dials down by hand has much less left for any thermostat to capture.

One worked example, assumptions on the label

Take a home that heats entirely with baseboards and spends an assumed $1,400 per heating season on that heat — an assumption, stated so the math can be rerun with a real bill. Apply the NRCan rule (s1) to a realistic schedule:

Now the payback divides itself. At $84–$168 a season, the $129 Gen3 (s3) pays back in roughly one to two heating seasons; the $159 V2 (s2) takes a little longer. A basic programmable unit captures the identical schedule saving — the setback does not care whether it was set on a touchscreen or a button panel — so its payback is shorter by exactly the difference in purchase price. What the Wi-Fi premium actually buys is not more saving; it is remote control, per-room energy reports, and a schedule that is easier to keep honest. Households should price those features as features, not as extra percent.

Check these three things before buying anything

  1. Count the wires. Kill the breaker and look inside the thermostat box. Smart line-voltage units need at least four wires, including a neutral or a second live wire, and do not work on two-wire installations (s4); the current model needs the same 3–4 wire setup with a neutral or second live wire (s3). A two-wire box points to a 2-wire programmable like the Honeywell class (s5) — or to an electrician.
  2. Add up the watts on the circuit. Sum the nameplate wattage of every heater the thermostat controls and compare it with the cap: 4,000 W for the Gen3 (s3), 3,800 W at 240 V for the V2 (s4), 3,500 W maximum for the Honeywell — which also refuses loads under 200 W at 240 V (s5). An overloaded thermostat is a fire concern, not a bargain.
  3. Confirm the load type. These controls are for resistive electric heat only: baseboards, convectors, radiant ceilings and fan-forced heaters (s2, s5). The Honeywell manual states plainly that its unit cannot drive a system run by a contactor or relay, and cannot run a central heating system (s5).

What success looks like: the schedule holds for a full week without manual overrides becoming the routine, setback rooms recover to comfort temperature before they are occupied again, and the first full billing period after the change shows the heating load down against a comparable-weather period — not merely down against a warmer month.

When it is not worth it

Skip the upgrade where rooms are occupied around the clock, where the household already works the dials faithfully, or where the wiring check above fails and an electrician's visit would swallow several seasons of saving. Skip it, too, as a whole-home project done in one weekend: the rooms with the longest empty hours — spare bedrooms, a basement, a home office on a work-away schedule — carry the payback, and the always-occupied living room carries almost none. One or two thermostats in the right rooms beat five in the wrong ones.

The BC exception: when the units are free

Eligible BC Hydro residential customers with electric baseboard or fan-forced heat can currently claim up to five Mysa baseboard thermostats at no cost through the Peak Saver offer, with a $50 bill credit for each winter of continued enrolment in which they take part in at least half the peak events (s6). The trade is stated openly in the offer: from November through March, BC Hydro may lower the setpoint by up to 3°C for no more than four hours at a time during peak events (s6). At a $0 purchase price the payback question disappears, and the only real decision left is whether those occasional setpoint events are acceptable in that household.
