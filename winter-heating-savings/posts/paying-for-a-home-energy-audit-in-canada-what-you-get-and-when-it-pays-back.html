Paying for a Home Energy Audit in Canada: What You Get and When It Pays Back

Rebates & planning · Published 2026-10-29 · Last updated 2026-10-29

General information only, not financial advice. Audit prices, rebates, and program terms differ by province and change over time. Confirm current prices and program rules with a registered energy advisor or the program itself before booking.

The most expensive energy audit is the one booked to unlock a rebate that no longer exists. Plenty of advice still frames an audit as the doorway to the Canada Greener Homes Grant — but that grant is closed, with the last day to submit documents on December 31, 2025, and the companion Greener Homes Loan closed after final loan agreement requests on March 31, 2026 (s1). An audit can still be money well spent in 2026. It is just a different purchase now, and it has to pass a different test.

Two products share one name

The first product is the rebate-gateway audit: a pair of evaluations, one before retrofit work begins and one after, performed by an NRCan-registered energy advisor because rebate programs require exactly that (s2) (s3). Ontario's Home Renovation Savings Program is the live example — it requires an assessment before work begins and reimburses up to $600 toward the assessments (s1) (s3). Without the first visit, the work that follows may not qualify at all.

The second product is the planning audit: a single pre-retrofit evaluation bought for its report, with no program attached. Its job is to rank the house's weaknesses before money is committed to any one fix. The two products overlap in the visit itself and diverge completely in how they pay back — which is why quoting one price for "an audit" misleads everyone.

What the evaluation includes, and what the report is

A pre-retrofit evaluation is the full assessment visit; a post-retrofit evaluation is the shorter follow-up that confirms what changed after the work (s1) (s2). The deliverable that outlives both visits is the EnerGuide report, which one national guide prices on its own at $200 to $400 (s2). Only NRCan-certified — registered — energy advisors can produce evaluations that rebate programs accept (s2) (s3), so the first booking question is not price. It is whether the advisor is registered, because an unregistered assessment cannot open a program door no matter how thorough it feels.

Read across the rows and the apparent conflict dissolves. The ranges overlap because they are pricing different things: a first visit against a follow-up, Ontario against a smaller provincial program, a report against a full evaluation pair. Home size, location, and the advisor move the Ontario figure across a $300 spread on their own (s3). A quote is only comparable when the scope matches — pre or post, which province, and whether a second visit is bundled or billed later.

The rebate side of the ledger

Where a program is live, it changes the arithmetic directly. Ontario's program reimburses up to $600 total for assessments when the program completes (s3). Manitoba offers a $400 home energy evaluation rebate, and Nova Scotia assessments are listed at $199 (s1). Two cautions belong beside those numbers. The Ontario reimbursement arrives when the program completes — the household carries the full fee first and recovers up to $600 later (s3). And the closed federal programs are a standing warning: a gateway audit bought for a program that closes, or for work that never happens, recovers nothing.

The payback test: three household scenarios

Scenario 1 — Ontario, program route, work already planned. A pre-retrofit evaluation at $400–$700 plus a post-retrofit evaluation at $300–$400 totals $700–$1,100 (s3). Against that, the program reimburses up to $600 when it completes (s3), leaving a net assessment cost of roughly $100–$500. If the retrofit work was going ahead anyway, that net figure buys both program eligibility and the report. This is the strongest case for booking.

Scenario 2 — No program applies, planning only. One pre-retrofit evaluation at the most-provinces range of $300–$600 (s1) has to earn its fee from better decisions alone. The test is concrete: the audit pays back if the work it re-ranks, resizes, or rules out saves at least the fee. A labelled worked example — assumptions, not quotes: a $450 evaluation (inside the sourced range) that steers a household away from a $450-or-larger mis-sized purchase, or toward a fix that trims $150 a year from heating costs, breaks even in about three heating seasons on the second path and immediately on the first. A household with no retrofit budget this year fails this test by definition: a plan for work that will not be funded returns nothing yet.

Scenario 3 — Manitoba or Nova Scotia pricing. With a $400 evaluation rebate in Manitoba (s1), a mid-range pre-retrofit fee can land close to fully offset — subject to that program's own terms, which must be confirmed before booking. At Nova Scotia's listed $199 (s1), the planning test in Scenario 2 gets much easier to pass: the report only has to redirect $199 of spending to justify itself.

The pattern across all three: the audit is rarely the risky spend. The risky spend is booking the gateway pair for a program that is closed, not funded, or mismatched to the work — or skipping the one evaluation a live program requires before the first contractor touches the house.
