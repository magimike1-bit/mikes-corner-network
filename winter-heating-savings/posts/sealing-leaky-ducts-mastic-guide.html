Sealing Leaky Heating Ducts: Find the Leaks, Use Mastic, Verify the Fix

System care · Published 2026-10-25 · Last updated 2026-10-25

General information only, not a quote or a savings guarantee. The percentages below are published typical-house and test-standard figures from the U.S. Department of Energy, ENERGY STAR material, and Natural Resources Canada — your home's leaks, and your savings, will differ. Work near gas appliances, electrical components, or in confined crawl spaces belongs to a licensed professional.

Every forced-air home pays for heat twice when its ducts leak: once to make the warm air, and again in the extra runtime needed because part of that air never reached a room. Utility guidance puts the scale plainly: in a typical house, about 20 to 30 percent of the air that moves through the duct system is lost due to leaks, holes, and poorly connected ducts. That lost air usually escapes exactly where nobody looks — into attics, crawl spaces, garages, and unfinished basements — while the rooms at the end of the longest runs stay stubbornly cool. October is the right month to fix it, because the system is about to run daily, the spaces are still tolerable to work in, and a sealed duct pays back on every single heating cycle all winter rather than on a few summer afternoons.

Sealing is not cleaning, and the difference matters

Two services get sold under similar names and solve different problems. Duct cleaning removes dust and debris from inside the ductwork. Duct sealing repairs the physical leaks in joints, seams, plenums, and connections so conditioned air stays inside the metal until it exits at a register. Natural Resources Canada makes the distinction bluntly in its housing guide: duct cleaning alone will not resolve balancing issues and generally has little effect on the distribution problems that leave rooms uncomfortable. A spotless duct that leaks at every joint still heats the crawl space. Conversely, a sealed duct does not need to be pristine inside to deliver its air. If a contractor's quote mixes the two, ask which line item closes leaks, which standard the sealing follows, and how the result will be tested — the answers separate a sealing job from a cleaning job with a new name.

Find the leaks first: a fan-on inspection procedure

  1. Set the system to fan. Turn the heating system on at the thermostat so air is moving through the ducts, following the equipment manufacturer's instructions. Moving air is what makes leaks findable; a still duct hides them.
  2. Start at the air handler and the plenum. Work outward from the furnace or air handler along every accessible run in the basement, attic, crawl space, and garage. The connections at the plenum and at the filter access panel are prime suspects — the DOE fact sheet notes that panel should be fitted with an airtight gasket.
  3. Feel and look at every joint. Run a hand slowly along seams, elbows, takeoffs, and register boots. Air escaping at a joint is a leak. Dust streaks radiating from a seam are the fossil record of one: air has been carrying dust out of that gap for a long time.
  4. Check the boots and the disconnected runs. Seal duct boots to the finished surfaces they pass through, and look for runs that have separated entirely — a disconnected duct is not a leak to seal but a joint to reconnect and secure first, then seal.
  5. Mark everything before opening any sealant. Note each leak, its location, and roughly how wide the gap is. The width decides the material in the next step, and a marked map keeps the sealing pass systematic instead of hopeful.

What success looks like at this stage: a written list of leaks by location and width, covering the plenum, every accessible joint, and every boot — not a vague sense that the ducts are probably fine.

Seal to a specification, not a slogan

Most advice stops at the word mastic. The published specifications go further, and the extra detail is what makes a seal last. The U.S. Department of Energy's Building Science guidance states that all duct leaks, connections, and plenums shall be sealed with UL-approved mastic, UL 181 tape, or an equivalent. For seams wider than 1/8 inch, the same guidance adds a step that casual advice omits: cover the gap with fiberglass mesh tape first, then apply the mastic over it, so the sealant bridges the opening instead of sagging into it. And the guidance is explicit about the household staple: regular cloth-backed duct tape should not be used, because it can dry out and fail quickly. NRCan's Canadian guidance agrees in substance — seal all plenums and supply and return ducts with aluminum foil duct tape, approved flexible plastic tape, or water-based mastic — and adds its own prohibition: do not use vinyl type duct tape on duct insulation.

That table is the specification check for the whole job: if the material in hand is not in the first three rows, it does not go on the duct. The DOE guidance also frames the payoff in bill terms rather than air terms: a properly sealed heating and cooling duct system can reduce utility bills by up to 20 percent, increase comfort throughout the home, and improve indoor air quality by reducing the moisture, dust, and pollen that leaky returns pull in from unconditioned spaces. Treat the up-to figure honestly — it is a ceiling for a leaky system, not a promise for an average one.

Insulate ducts in unheated spaces — to a number

Sealing stops air escaping; insulation stops the air that remains from surrendering its heat through the duct wall on its way through a cold attic or crawl space. Here the two national guides give different-looking numbers that fit together. The DOE/PNNL fact sheet requires all accessible ducts in unconditioned areas — attics, crawlspaces, basements, and garages — to be insulated to at least R-8 for supply ducts and R-6 for return ducts. NRCan's guidance for ducts running through unheated or cool basements and crawl spaces calls for RSI 2.1 (R-12) or more of batt or blanket insulation cut to size, or a specially designed foil-faced fibreglass blanket duct insulation, secured with string, wire, or approved tape. One Canadian safety line deserves its own sentence: do not wrap ducts within 1.8 metres (6 feet) of a wood-fired furnace unless the insulation is a special, approved noncombustible type. Seal first, then insulate — insulation over an open joint hides the leak and guarantees the warm air escapes into the insulation layer instead of the room.

DIY or professional: a decision framework

The honest dividing line is access, not skill. Leaks you can reach, see, and press sealant onto — basement runs, exposed attic runs, the plenum, the boots — are a homeowner job with the materials above. Leaks inside walls, ceilings, and floors are not reachable by hand at all, and that is where professional testing earns its cost. The DOE/PNNL procedure begins with leakage testing performed to the RESNET or ACCA standard, with a targeted maximum level of duct leakage set before work begins — preferably 10 percent or less of total airflow. That target converts a professional job from trust me into a number the finished work can be measured against. Choose the professional route when rooms stay cold after the accessible sealing is done, when most ductwork is concealed, when a run may be disconnected inside a cavity, or when the work sits near gas or electrical equipment. Choose the DIY route for the accessible map from the inspection step, then re-test your own work the simple way below.

Verify the fix at the registers

  1. Repeat the fan-on pass. With the system running, revisit every marked joint. A sealed joint should show no air movement against a hand, and no new dust streaking should form at its edges over the following weeks.
  2. Compare the far rooms. The registers at the ends of the longest runs are the honest witnesses. Rooms that lagged before sealing should now track the rest of the house more closely during a normal heating cycle.
  3. Watch one full bill cycle. Compare heating use against the same period's weather rather than against last month's bill — a colder month can hide a real improvement, and a milder one can fake one.

What success looks like: no detectable air movement at the sealed joints with the fan running, far rooms heating with the rest of the house, and a duct system whose remaining leakage — if professionally tested — sits at or under the 10-percent-of-airflow target the DOE/PNNL procedure sets. Anything less is a list of leaks still to find, which is useful information, not failure.

Questions homeowners ask about duct sealing

Is duct tape really wrong for ducts? Yes, on the published guidance. The DOE states regular cloth-backed duct tape should not be used because it can dry out and fail quickly, and NRCan separately prohibits vinyl type duct tape on duct insulation. Use mastic or UL 181 foil tape.

Will sealing fix a room that is always cold? It fixes the share of the problem caused by air lost before it reaches that room — often the largest share when the room sits at the end of a long, leaky run through an unheated space. If the room stays cold after sealing and verification, the remaining causes are sizing, balancing, or the heating equipment itself, which is a contractor conversation.

Should ducts be cleaned at the same time? Cleaning is a separate service solving a different problem. NRCan notes cleaning alone does not resolve distribution or balancing problems. Seal first; decide about cleaning on its own merits, not as a substitute.

How much can sealing save? DOE guidance says a properly sealed duct system can reduce utility bills by up to 20 percent, against a typical-house loss of 20 to 30 percent of duct air. Homes with tight, accessible, mostly-conditioned-space ducts will land far below that ceiling — which is why the inspection comes first and the promise never should.
