A Thermostat Strategy That Actually Saves

Save daily · 5 min read

Your thermostat is the cheapest heating upgrade you own — it just needs a plan. The core idea is simple: your home doesn't need to be at full comfort temperature when nobody's awake to enjoy it. Here's how to turn that idea into a schedule.

What "setback" means

Setback is lowering the thermostat for the hours you're asleep or away, then bringing it back up before you wake or return. While the house sits cooler, the heating system runs less — and the bigger the gap between indoor and outdoor temperature, the faster heat escapes, so those cooler hours save disproportionately. The recovery warm-up does use extra energy, but in a reasonably insulated home the net effect is still less total heating over the day.

A common myth: that reheating the house "uses more than you saved." For typical forced-air furnaces and boilers in insulated homes, that's not how the physics works. The longer your house spends at a lower temperature, the less heat it loses overall.

A schedule that fits real life

Most households do well with four periods. Adjust the times to your actual routine:

  • Wake: comfortable temperature, starting 30–45 minutes before the alarm. Nobody wants to get out of bed into a cold house — that's how setback plans die.
  • Day (empty house): set back a few degrees while everyone's at work or school.
  • Evening: comfortable temperature when the family is home.
  • Sleep: set back a few degrees overnight. Most people sleep fine — often better — in a slightly cooler bedroom with warm bedding.

Start with a modest setback of a few degrees. You can always deepen it once you're used to the rhythm. Comfort you can stick with beats an aggressive plan you abandon in February.

If you have a manual thermostat

You can still do setback — it just takes discipline. Set a phone reminder for the evening setback and the morning warm-up, or better: a basic programmable thermostat is inexpensive, installs on the same two wires in most homes, and pays for itself by running the plan automatically. (Turn off the breaker to the heating system before touching any wiring, and if the wiring looks unfamiliar, call a professional.)

Smart-thermostat habits worth using

  • Schedules over learning: "learning" thermostats are fine, but a schedule you set deliberately usually matches your life better than one the device guesses.
  • Geofencing: if your thermostat supports it, let your phone's location trigger the away setback — it handles irregular days the schedule misses.
  • Room sensors: if bedrooms run cold and the living room runs warm, a sensor in the bedroom can keep the thermostat honest about where comfort actually matters.
  • Vacation hold: going away for days? Set a hold at a safe low temperature (see frozen-pipe note below) instead of letting the schedule run.
Don't let the house get too cold. When you're away in winter, keep the thermostat no lower than about 13°C (55°F). Below that, pipes in exterior walls can freeze — and a burst pipe costs far more than any heating savings. This matters most in homes with plumbing in outside walls or unheated crawlspaces.

When to be careful

Heat pumps: deep setbacks can backfire. Many heat pumps switch to energy-hungry backup (auxiliary) heat to recover from a big temperature drop, wiping out the savings. With a heat pump, keep setbacks small — a degree or two — or use the thermostat's "smart recovery" feature if it has one.

Steam and hot-water radiators: these systems heat up and cool down slowly. Big setbacks mean long, uncomfortable recovery times. Small setbacks work fine; aggressive ones just make mornings miserable.

Homes with humidity problems: very cool indoor temperatures can let condensation form on cold surfaces. If you see window condensation already, keep setbacks modest and address the moisture.

Make it stick

The best thermostat strategy is the one that runs without you thinking about it. Program the schedule once, check it after the first cold snap, and tweak the timing — not the temperatures — if something feels off. After a week, you won't notice the setback hours at all. Your heating bill will.
