Window Insulation Film: A Renter-Safe Afternoon That Cuts Drafts

Quick wins · 6 min read

Published 2026-10-21 · Last updated 2026-10-21

A shrink-film window kit is the least glamorous product in the winter aisle: a sheet of clear plastic, a roll of double-sided tape and your own hair dryer. It is also, on the right windows, one of the highest-value afternoons in home heating. The mechanism is simple and honest. Applied to the inside of the frame, the film creates an airtight barrier over the whole window, and the stagnant air trapped between the film and the glass acts as insulation, reducing heat transfer through the window (s1). One product, two jobs: it gaskets shut the leaks around a sash that rattles, and it adds a still-air pocket over glass that conducts heat freely. Everything useful about film — where it shines, where it is pointless, and how to install it so it lasts the season — follows from those two jobs.

Choose the windows before you buy the kit

Film's gains concentrate where its two jobs are actually needed. On drafty single-pane windows — older homes, basements, cottages, rental units with tired sash — both the leak sealing and the air pocket matter, and the comfort change near the window is usually obvious the first cold evening. On double-pane windows that are already fairly tight, the gains are smaller, because the sealed unit already traps air and the frame already seals (s3). Spend your film where physics pays you: single-pane first, then any window where you can feel air movement at the frame with the back of your hand, then windows in rooms you actually heat and sit in. Skip, or leave for last, windows you open daily for ventilation and any window that serves as an emergency exit route in a bedroom — film over an egress window is a barrier in the worst possible moment. Kits commonly cover up to five standard windows (s2), so one kit, well aimed, covers the worst offenders in most homes.

What you need

The kit itself, plus the supporting cast the utility guide from Avista recommends: scissors or a utility knife, a tape measure, a hair dryer, a microfiber cloth, cleaning spray or rubbing alcohol — and an extra roll of double-sided tape, because the strips included in kits are frequently inadequate for the job (s2). The frame needs a flat surface at least about half an inch wide for the tape to grip (s3), and the tape has to hold that grip for several months, which is why surface preparation, below, is the step that decides whether your film is still sealed in February.

The installation procedure

  1. Clean and dry the frame thoroughly. Dust, grease and moisture are the three reasons tape lets go mid-season. Wipe the full tape path with your cloth and cleaner, then let it dry completely (s2).
  2. Apply tape on all four sides, with no gaps. A single break in the tape line is a leak in the air barrier. Corners deserve a second press with a warm fingertip (s2).
  3. Cut the film oversize. Measure the taped area and cut the sheet with a few centimetres of slack on every side; you will trim after shrinking, and film cut exactly to size leaves no margin for a crooked start.
  4. Stretch the film across and press it to the tape. Work from the top down, keeping gentle, even tension so the sheet seals flat rather than bellied (s2).
  5. Shrink it with the hair dryer on its highest heat setting. Move steadily a few centimetres off the surface; wrinkles pull out and the film goes drum-tight and nearly invisible. Lingering in one spot can melt a hole — keep moving (s2).
  6. Trim the excess. Run scissors or the knife along the outside of the tape line for a clean edge (s2).
  7. Press-test every edge the next morning. A gentle push near each border should meet resistance, not a sigh of escaping air. Re-press any lifted section while the room is warm; tape adhesion improves with pressure and warmth.

What success looks like: the film is clear and taut, a tissue held at the frame hangs still instead of fluttering, and the cold stripe you used to feel on the floor beneath the window is gone or greatly reduced. Condensation patterns change too — film reduces condensation build-up on the glass, one of the benefits utility and how-to sources both list (s1, s2).

Renters, removal, and honest limits

Film is a seasonal, temporary measure, not a replacement for sound windows (s1) — and that temporariness is its renter-friendly virtue. It installs with tape, not screws, and comes down in spring. Two cautions keep it deposit-safe. First, test your tape on an inconspicuous patch of the frame finish first; tape that must adhere for months can lift fragile paint or varnish on removal, and warming the tape line with the hair dryer as you peel, slowly, is gentler than a cold yank. Second, photograph the frames before you start if your lease makes you nervous — documentation settles deposit questions better than memory. Know the limits, too: film does nothing for a frame that is rotting, a sash that will not close, or a wall that leaks around the window unit itself. Those are landlord-and-repair problems, and no plastic sheet should be asked to hide them.

Our take: Buy one kit, aim it at your worst single-pane or draftiest windows, and install it on a mild afternoon with obsessive frame-cleaning. Judge it that evening from your usual chair. Film earns a house-wide rollout one window at a time, on evidence, not on the box's promises.

Pair film with the other cheap seals

Film performs best as one layer in a small, deliberate system rather than as a solo act. The windows you film are usually in rooms with other honest leaks: a door to an unheated entry with daylight under it, outlet boxes on exterior walls that puff cold air on windy days, and a fireplace damper left open to a chimney that draws all winter. None of those fixes costs more than the film kit, and together they change how a room feels far more than any one of them alone, because comfort is governed by the worst leak in the room, not the average. Work the room as a unit on the same afternoon: film the window, sweep or gasket the door, plug the outlets with child-safe foam gaskets behind the covers, and close and latch the damper with a note on the mantel so nobody lights a fire against it. Then sit in the room that evening with the same thermostat setting as the night before. The comparison is the audit. If the room still feels wrong, the remaining culprit is bigger than weatherization — a duct problem, a heating shortfall — and you have at least cleared the cheap suspects honestly before paying anyone to find it.

FAQ

Can the film be reused next winter? Plan on no. Film is generally a one-season product: it stretches, tears at tape lines and rarely survives removal intact. The tape-and-film cost per window is low precisely because it is consumable.

Which side of the window does it go on? Interior, per the standard kits and the utility procedure — tape on the inside frame, film across the room side (s2). Interior mounting also keeps weather off the tape.

Will it work on sliding or casement windows? Most window types suit shrink film provided the flat tape path exists around the frame (s3). The real disqualifier is use, not style: a window you open weekly will destroy the seal and your patience together.
