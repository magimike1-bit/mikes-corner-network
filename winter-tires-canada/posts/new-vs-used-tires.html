New vs. Used: How to Inspect a Used Set

A used set of winter tires can be a genuine bargain — or four liabilities with tread. Here's the full walkthrough for telling which one you're looking at.

← All guides

Winter tires with plenty of life left change hands every spring — someone sells the car, moves south, or switches sizes. Buying used can save real money. But unlike a used bookshelf, a bad tire fails at highway speed. Here is the inspection routine that separates the bargains from the hazards.

1. Tread depth: measure, don't eyeball

Winter tires do their work with deep tread and sipes; a tire worn near the legal minimum is a winter tire in name only. Every tire has wear bars — small raised bars molded into the bottom of the main grooves. When the surrounding tread is flush with those bars, the tire is done. You want to see clear daylight between the tread surface and the wear bars, evenly across the tire.

The classic coin test works as a quick field check: insert a coin into a groove — if the tread doesn't reach well up the coin's design, there's not much tire left. But a proper tread-depth gauge costs very little and removes all guesswork; measure the outer, middle, and inner grooves of each tire and write the numbers down.

2. Wear patterns: read the story

Uneven wear tells you what the tire lived through — and what the car it came from was doing wrong:

  • Even wear across the tread: the good case. The tire was properly inflated and aligned.
  • Both edges worn, centre fine: chronic under-inflation. The tire flexed too much; its internal structure may be compromised.
  • Centre worn, edges fine: chronic over-inflation. Less structural concern, but the contact patch was wrong for the tire's whole life.
  • One edge worn: alignment or camber problem on the donor car. The tire itself may be fine, but verify the remaining tread is still deep and even enough to use.
  • Cupping or scalloped dips: worn suspension components bouncing the tire. Cupped tires are noisy and often structurally tired — treat as a red flag.

Compare all four tires against each other. A matched set should show similar wear; one tire that looks completely different from its siblings suggests a replacement was mixed in, and mismatched tires are a handling compromise.

3. Sidewalls: the no-repair zone

Run your hands and eyes over every sidewall, both sides. You are looking for:

  • Bulges or blisters — a bulge means the internal cords have failed. This is an instant reject; the tire can blow out without further warning.
  • Cuts or deep scuffs — anything that exposes cords or goes more than superficial is a reject.
  • Cracking or crazing — fine cracks in the rubber, especially around the sidewall lettering, signal age and ozone damage. Light surface checking on an older tire warrants a professional opinion; heavy cracking is a reject.
  • Plug or patch repairs near the sidewall — repairs belong in the tread area. A repair at the shoulder or sidewall is not trustworthy.

4. Age: read the DOT code on every tire

Find the DOT code on each sidewall — the last four digits are the production week and year (see our buying guide for how to read it). Check all four: a set is only as trustworthy as its oldest tire, and mixed ages are common in used sets. If the tires are getting on in years, factor in that rubber hardens with age — an old tire with deep tread can still grip poorly in the cold, which defeats the purpose of buying winter rubber.

Our take

Our rule of thumb: a used set should have clearly deep, even tread, no sidewall damage, and a production date you'd be comfortable explaining to a passenger. If you'd feel sheepish telling a friend "yeah, these are fine" — they aren't. That's our opinion, and it has saved more than one buyer from a bad deal.

5. The practical checks

  • All four match: same size, same model, similar wear. A set of four identical tires behaves predictably; a mixed set doesn't.
  • Wheels included? If the tires come on wheels, confirm the bolt pattern, centre bore, and offset fit your vehicle — "came off a similar car" is not confirmation. If they don't fit, price the set as tires only.
  • Ask why they're selling: "sold the car" and "moved to a condo downtown" are good answers. Evasive answers are information too.
  • Budget for mounting and balancing: a used set still needs professional mounting, balancing, and new valve stems. Factor that into the real price before comparing against new.
Safety note

When in doubt, have a tire shop inspect a used set before you pay — most will do a quick assessment for a small fee, and it's the cheapest insurance in this transaction. This guide is general information, not professional advice. Never buy a tire with a sidewall bulge, exposed cords, or a repair outside the tread area.

The bottom line

Used winter tires are worth buying when they pass every check above: deep even tread, clean sidewalls, sensible age, matched set. Fail any one of those and the "deal" is the most expensive tires you'll ever buy — the ones that don't work when you need them.
