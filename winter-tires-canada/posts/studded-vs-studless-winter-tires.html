Studded vs Studless Winter Tires: Legality, Ice Grip, and the Costs Nobody Lists

Studs bite, compounds grip — and in Ontario your address decides before physics gets a vote. The provincial windows, the test that complicates the simple story, and a framework for choosing.

Published October 22, 2026 · Last updated October 22, 2026

← All guides

Studded and studless winter tires solve the same problem with opposite tools. A studded tire carries small metal pins in the tread that bite mechanically into ice. A studless winter tire — the category tyre makers also call friction tires — relies on a soft, heavily siped compound that stays flexible in the cold and grips with thousands of tiny edges. Canada adds a third factor most comparisons skip: whether studs are legal at all where the car is registered, and for how much of the year. This guide takes the three questions in order — legality, performance, cost — and ends with a decision framework keyed to the surfaces a driver actually crosses each week. It is a buying-decision guide with one verification procedure (checking legality and fitment before purchase), rather than a hands-on how-to.

Is it legal where you live? Province by province

In most of Canada, studded tires are legal during the winter months, with the exact rules set by each province. The exception that catches people is Ontario, which runs the most restrictive regime in the country: studs are a Northern Ontario permission, not a provincial one.

Studless winter tires face none of this: they are legal in every province, in every month winter tires themselves are permitted, with no window to miss and no address test to pass. For a driver who regularly crosses from Southern Ontario into a stud-permitting province — or the reverse — that difference alone can settle the question, because the tire has to be legal at home first.

Spec verification, Nova Scotia style. One province shows how detailed stud rules can get. Nova Scotia permits studs from October 15 to May 31 only if the studs protrude no more than 1/8 inch, are no larger than 1/2 inch in diameter including the casing, number no more than 130 per tire on vehicles under 10,000 lbs, and are equal in number on the left and right sides of the vehicle. Ontario, for its part, permits Scandinavian-standard lightweight studs in the north, which reduce pavement wear by about 75 percent compared with older, heavier studs. The practical step before buying any studded set is the same in every province: confirm the window, confirm any stud specification, and confirm the set being sold meets it.

Ice, snow, and bare pavement: what the testing shows

The folk version of this comparison — studs win on ice, full stop — survives because it is half true, and the controlled testing shows exactly which half. In Tire Rack's ice test of studdable winter tires, adding studs to the same tire clearly helped that tire: the studded version stopped from 12 mph in 35.4 feet, against 40.0 feet for the identical tire run unstudded, and it accelerated over 60 feet in 4.88 seconds against 4.94. Studs, on the tire designed to take them, are a real improvement on ice.

But the same test included a dedicated studless ice-and-snow tire, and it beat both: 32.7 feet to stop from 12 mph, and 4.28 seconds over the 60-foot acceleration run. Tire Rack's conclusion was that none of the studdable tires — not even the studded one — could match the dedicated studless tire's ice traction in that test. The lesson is not that studs do nothing. It is that the comparison that matters is not studded versus unstudded versions of a mid-tier studdable tire; it is a factory-studded tire against the best studless designs, and there the studless compound and siping can hold or take the lead even on ice.

Where studs keep a genuine edge is a narrower, specific surface: wet ice near freezing. A Manitoba guide aimed at prairie drivers puts it precisely — studs win on ice, particularly the wet ice near freezing, the surface where a rubber compound has the least to work with, and a polished, thawed-and-refrozen side street is exactly that surface. Deep and packed snow is a different contest, decided by tread pattern and compound rather than pins; and bare cold pavement — which that same guide notes is most of the Trans-Canada Highway for most of a Manitoba winter — favours the studless tire outright. Slush and treated, salted urban roads sit on the studless side as well: there is little for a pin to bite, and the full rubber contact patch does the work.

The costs studs carry: noise, wear, and bare-road grip

The Manitoba guide summarises the studded bill in three items, and each deserves a sentence of its own. Noise: metal pins on pavement produce the clicking and hum that make studded tires the louder choice on every cleared road, all winter — the reason they are restricted in the first place is the combination of that noise and the road damage they cause. Bare-pavement grip: on dry or cleared roads the pins hold part of the tread off the surface, which is why studless tires beat studs on bare cold pavement in ordinary winter driving. Wear: studs themselves wear down with road contact, and a stud that has worn down stops helping long before the tread around it is finished — the tire can look serviceable while its one advantage has quietly expired. Add the legal windows above and the full cost picture is: a narrower legal season, a louder car, weaker grip on the surface most highway drivers see most often, and an advantage with its own expiry date.

None of that makes studs the wrong choice. It makes them a specialised one. A driver whose winter is unplowed concession roads, a long icy rural commute, and intersections that polish to wet ice for months is buying exactly the surface studs exist for. A driver whose winter is a salted city grid and a cleared highway is paying the three costs to insure against a surface rarely met — and the controlled testing suggests a top studless tire may protect that driver as well or better even when the ice does appear.

The decision framework

  1. Legality gate. Is the vehicle's home address in a stud-permitting region, and can the set be removed by the window's end? Southern Ontario residents stop here: the answer is studless. Everyone else continues.
  2. Name the worst weekly surface, not the worst imaginable one. If the honest answer is wet, polished ice on untreated roads, week after week, studs earn their place. If it is packed snow, slush, or bare pavement with occasional ice, the studless side of the table applies.
  3. Count the bare-pavement share. Highway commuters on maintained routes spend most winter kilometres on the surface where studs are weaker, noisier and wearing out. Rural drivers on untreated roads spend far fewer.
  4. Compare the right pair. Price a factory-studded set against a top-tier studless ice-and-snow set — not against the unstudded version of a studdable tire. The test numbers above show those are different contests.
  5. Plan the spring removal. Windows end between April 30 and May 31 depending on province, winter compounds wear quickly in warm weather in any case, and a studded set kept on past its window is both illegal and self-defeating.

What success looks like: the chosen set is legal at the home address for the whole season, its strength matches the surface named in step 2, and the removal date is in the calendar before the tires are on the car.

Our take

For most Canadian drivers the default has quietly flipped: a top studless ice-and-snow tire is the safer all-round buy, and studs are the specialist's tool for untreated ice country — bought deliberately, where they are legal, for a surface the driver can actually name.

FAQ

Are studded tires legal in Ontario? Only in Northern Ontario, for vehicles with a northern ownership address, from October 1 to April 30 (visiting out-of-province vehicles under a month also qualify). Southern Ontario residents are not permitted to use them.

Do studs really stop shorter on ice? Against the same tire unstudded, yes — 35.4 feet versus 40.0 feet from 12 mph in Tire Rack's test. Against a dedicated studless ice-and-snow tire, not in that test: the studless tire stopped in 32.7 feet. The comparison pair decides the answer.

Can studs be added to any winter tire? No. Only tires designed as studdable can take studs, and studded fitments must still meet provincial rules — Nova Scotia, for example, specifies maximum protrusion, diameter and stud count. Factory-studded tires arrive with the studs fitted to the maker's specification.

Why do provinces restrict studs at all? Road damage and noise. Studs wear paved surfaces — Southern Ontario's softer limestone pavement is specifically cited — which is why windows, regions and lightweight-stud standards exist even where studs are welcome.
