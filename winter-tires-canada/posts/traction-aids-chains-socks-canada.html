Tire Chains vs. Textile Socks vs. Nothing: Traction Aids in Canada

The right traction aid depends first on where the trip goes — in one province chains are a signed legal requirement, in another they are reserved for snowplows — and only then on the snow itself.

Published October 24, 2026 · Last updated October 24, 2026

← All guides

For most Canadian winter driving, the traction question is already answered by the tires: a proper winter set, in good condition, handles plowed and treated roads across the country. Traction aids — steel chains, cable chains, textile socks — exist for the remainder: unplowed grades, mountain passes under chain-up signs, a cottage road after a storm, a driveway that defeats even good rubber. Choosing among them starts with an uncomfortable fact: Canada has no single rule. The device that is mandatory equipment on a signed British Columbia highway is, for an ordinary passenger car in Quebec, not authorized at all.

The short answer by trip type

On plowed urban and highway routes in most provinces, on legal winter tires with healthy tread: nothing extra, and that is a considered answer, not a gap in the kit. On signed B.C. mountain routes in storm conditions: chains, or an accepted traction device carried and ready, because the signs carry the force of law. As a get-unstuck aid for short, slow stretches — an unplowed side road, a steep driveway — where the province accepts them: textile socks, fitted over winter tires. As a season-long substitute for winter tires: nothing on this page qualifies, anywhere. British Columbia states it directly: chains on summer tires are not an acceptable substitute for legal winter tires on signed highways.

Two rows deserve emphasis. The textile row is a short-distance tool: the same provincial guidance that credits socks with chain-like traction on snow and ice warns that they degrade quickly on asphalt, so a sock that gets a car to the plowed main road has done its whole job — driving home on it across town destroys it. And the “nothing” row is only as strong as the tires under it. British Columbia’s legal winter tire needs at least 3.5 mm of tread depth and a three-peak mountain snowflake or M+S marking, and the province recommends the mountain-snowflake tire and carrying chains for extreme conditions; Quebec’s SAAQ advises at least 4.8 mm of tread depth across the full width when winter tires are installed, for better traction. A worn winter tire plus no traction aid is the worst combination on the page.

What the law actually says, province by province

British Columbia is the requirement province for passenger vehicles. Sections of highway requiring winter tires or chains on board are marked with roadside signs; drivers must obey winter tire and chain signs from October 1 to April 30, with the requirement ending March 31 on select highways not passing through mountain passes or high snowfall areas. Going beyond the signs without the proper equipment can mean being turned around and fined — $121 for a passenger vehicle not equipped with winter tires when and where required. Passenger vehicles may use alternative traction devices with their winter tires, provided the devices have been tested for performance in winter conditions. AutoSock, the best-known textile brand, states its device may be used in all Canadian provinces and is accepted in B.C. as equivalent to chains when chain lights are on for vehicles up to 11,794 kg gross vehicle weight rating; vehicles over that weight must use chains when the lights are illuminated.

Quebec is the restriction province, and the fact most often gotten wrong. The SAAQ authorizes studded tires and chains between October 15 and May 1 inclusive — but the two devices go to different vehicles. Studded tires are authorized for passenger vehicles, taxis and comparable vehicles, and commercial vehicles up to 3,000 kg. Chains are authorized only on the tires of emergency vehicles, farm tractors, and road vehicles used for snow removal and winter maintenance. An ordinary Quebec driver’s traction strategy is therefore winter tires — mandatory in the province in season — plus studs if chosen, not chains in the trunk.

The rest of the country mostly leaves the choice to the driver, with road-damage liability as the boundary. Alberta has no regulation prohibiting tire chains, but a driver who damages the road with them could be charged for that damage. Manitoba has no law prohibiting tire chains; they are permitted when needed. In New Brunswick, chains may be carried for use in ice and snow conditions. None of these provinces turns a chain into a requirement for passenger cars, and none of them accepts any device as a replacement for winter tires in good condition. Municipal bylaws can add local restrictions, which is why the framework below ends with a check of the specific route, not just the province.

Clearance and fit: the check before any device goes in the trunk

A traction device that does not fit is worse than none, because the failure happens at the roadside, in the storm. Before buying chains or socks, confirm three things from the vehicle’s owner’s manual and the device maker’s fitment list: the vehicle permits traction devices at all (some low-clearance models do not), the device size matches the exact tire size on the drive axle, and there is clearance between the tire and the suspension, brake lines and bodywork through a full steering turn. Devices go on the primary drive axle — the B.C. guidance for both steel and textile devices is written around the drive wheels — and a practice fitting in a dry driveway, in daylight, is the difference between a five-minute stop and a miserable hour. Fit them, drive a short distance slowly, re-check tension on chain types, and learn where the fasteners sit before the first real use.

Our take

Buy the device for the worst legal requirement on the routes actually driven, not the worst imaginable storm. For a B.C. mountain-pass driver that means chains or an accepted device, carried all season. For most other Canadian drivers it means good winter tires, honest tread depth, and — where accepted — a set of socks as insurance against one bad driveway, not as a driving mode.

Decision framework: what to carry, where, and when

Step 1 — the law of the route. Signed B.C. routes in season: carry chains or an accepted traction device and be ready to install them; enforcement can turn unequipped vehicles around. Quebec passenger driving: chains are not an authorized option; the legal kit is winter tires, with studded tires permitted in season. Elsewhere: devices are generally permitted when conditions warrant, with liability for road damage. Step 2 — the surface. Deep snow and ice on grades point to steel link chains where legal. A short, slow, unplowed stretch points to textile socks. Plowed and treated roads point to no device at all. Step 3 — the vehicle. If the manual forbids chains or clearance is tight, cable chains or socks are the remaining device options — or the trip waits. Step 4 — the honest default. British Columbia’s own winter guidance gives the final rule: the best way to stay safe in extreme conditions is to avoid driving until the weather improves. No device in the table outranks that sentence.

FAQ

Are tire socks legal in Canada? The leading textile maker states its device may be used in all Canadian provinces, and British Columbia accepts it as equivalent to chains under its chain-light rules for vehicles up to 11,794 kg. Provincial and municipal rules govern; check the route’s current requirements before relying on any device.

Can chains replace winter tires? No. In British Columbia, chains on summer tires are explicitly not an acceptable substitute for legal winter tires on signed highways, and no province treats a traction device as a winter-tire replacement.

Why are chains restricted in Quebec? Quebec law authorizes chains in season only for emergency vehicles, farm tractors and snow-removal or winter-maintenance vehicles. Passenger vehicles use winter tires, which are mandatory in season, and may use studded tires within the authorized window.

Chains or socks for a vehicle with very little wheel clearance? Neither should be forced. Cable chains sit lower-profile than link chains, and textile socks add the least bulk, but if the owner’s manual prohibits traction devices, the correct decision is not to fit one — improve the tires and choose the route and timing instead.
