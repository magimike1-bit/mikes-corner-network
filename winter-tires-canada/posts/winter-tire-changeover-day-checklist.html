Winter tire changeover day checklist: before, during and after the appointment

Seasonal · 8 min read

The installation appointment is where winter preparation quietly succeeds or fails. The tires themselves get all the attention, but the appointment bundles decisions — what is included, what is billed separately, what gets inspected, what you bring, and what you check before you drive away — that determine both the final invoice and whether the car is actually ready. Shops are slammed from late October onward, which makes preparation worth more, not less: a prepared customer gets the inspection items caught, the fees agreed in advance, and the follow-up task (the re-torque) actually scheduled. This checklist runs the whole appointment, before, during and after.

Before you book: confirm the scope, in writing

Installation pricing varies by provider, service scope and region, and the line that separates a good quote from a surprise invoice is what the quote includes. Before committing, confirm:

  • Mounting and balancing — included for all four, or per tire? Published Canadian benchmark ranges put standard mounting and balancing around $20–35 per tire at large chains and full four-tire packages around $100–160 at others; treat these as orientation ranges for spotting an outlier quote, not as a price promise.
  • On-rim swap vs. mount-and-balance. If your winters live on their own rims, the job is a swap — faster and cheaper. If both sets share one set of rims, every changeover is a full mount and balance, twice a year, forever; that recurring cost is the real argument for a second rim set.
  • TPMS service — sensor relearn or reprogramming included? A second wheel set may need its own sensors, and the relearn is a distinct step.
  • Alignment — checked, included, or billed separately? Installation and alignment are different services; a checklist-grade quote says which one you are buying.
  • Storage — does the shop store the off-season set, and at what price? Seasonal swap and storage bundles exist precisely because most households would rather not haul and store tires.
  • Warranty and inspection terms — what does the shop inspect while the wheels are off, and what warranty applies to the work?

Bring and verify: the pre-appointment checks

A short self-check in the driveway prevents the two classic appointment failures — the wrong tires and the missing key:

  1. Confirm the set. Exact size, compatibility with your wheels, overall condition, and tread depth on all four — a set with one tired tire is discovered cheapest at home.
  2. Find the wheel-lock key and put it in the car. A missing key turns a one-hour appointment into a removal job.
  3. Clean the off-season set if it is going into storage; debris and brake dust damage wheels over a season in a bag.
  4. Note your TPMS situation — which set has sensors, and whether the dash light is currently on.
  5. Clear the trunk. The shop needs the space you are asking them to put four tires into.

During the appointment: what a proper job includes

You will not stand in the bay, but knowing the sequence tells you what to ask about at the counter. A complete changeover runs: vehicle secured on a lift; wheels removed; wheels and tires inspected for damage, corrosion and bends; TPMS sensors inspected; new tires mounted; each wheel balanced on a machine with weights added so it spins true (unbalanced wheels vibrate and wear unevenly — this step is crucial, not cosmetic); sensors reset or reprogrammed so pressure readings stay accurate; wheels installed; and lug nuts tightened with a torque wrench to the manufacturer's specified torque — the step that matters most for safety. The inspection dividend is real: a safety inspection during installation catches uneven tread wear, damaged rims and suspension problems while the car is already apart, and catching a small issue then is cheaper than meeting it in January.

Before you drive away: the five checks

  1. Pressures. Ask for the final pressure readings and confirm they match the door-jamb placard — not the sidewall maximum — set cold.
  2. Torque and the re-check. Confirm lug nuts were torqued to spec with a calibrated tool, and get the re-check instruction in writing: careful shops tell you to recheck torque after a short drive (commonly the first 50–100 km — follow your shop's and manual's number) because wheels can settle.
  3. Direction. If your winters are directional, confirm the rotation arrows point forward on all four corners; a reversed directional tire is a five-minute fix now and a traction problem later.
  4. The old set. Confirm where the summer set went — your trunk, bagged and marked by position, or the shop's storage — and that position marks were made for next season's rotation.
  5. The invoice. Read it against the quote: mounting, balancing, TPMS, alignment and storage each appear only if agreed. Surprises are cheapest at the counter.
What success looks like: no dash lights, no vibration at highway speed in the first week, pressures at placard spec, a re-torque date on your calendar, and an invoice that matches the quote line for line.

After: the first two weeks

Three habits close the loop. Recheck torque at the interval you were given. Check pressures again after the first real cold snap — pressure falls roughly in step with temperature (about a pound per ten degrees of cooling, in the common rule of thumb), so a set filled on a mild October afternoon reads low on the first cold morning without anything being wrong. And build the between-appointment routine the good shops recommend: a monthly pressure check and before long trips, rotation roughly every 8,000–13,000 km or per the owner's manual, and a glance at tread and sidewalls when you check. The appointment gets the tires on; the routine is what makes them last the seasons you paid for.

The storage decision, made once

The appointment forces one more decision that deserves a sentence of its own: where the off-season set lives until spring. Home storage done well is free but demands the discipline from the storage guide — clean, marked by position, bagged, cool and dark, away from ozone sources. Shop storage costs money — bundled swap-and-storage pricing exists precisely because the service sells — but returns the set inspected, keeps heavy lifting off your household, and means the tires are already where the spring appointment happens. Neither answer is wrong; the wrong answer is the improvised one, where four tires spend the winter leaning against a damp garage wall, unmarked, and come back in spring with flat spots, mixed positions and a slow leak nobody can date. Decide at the counter, while the set is in the shop's hands, and write the decision on the invoice copy you keep — future you, booking the spring changeover, will ask exactly this question.

FAQ

Is an alignment part of a tire installation? Not automatically. Balancing is part of mounting; alignment is a separate service that may be checked or billed separately. Confirm which you are buying before the appointment, especially if the old set wore unevenly — that wear pattern is the argument for adding it.

Why did my TPMS light come on after the swap? Usually a relearn issue with the second set's sensors, or pressures set warm and read low cold. Both are counter conversations, not emergencies — but do not ignore the light for the season; it is the only low-pressure warning you have.

When should I book? Before the rush. Shops fill from the first cold forecast onward, and Quebec's mandatory winter-tire deadline compresses demand further east. A booked October appointment gets a fresher crew and a shorter wait than a desperate November walk-in.
