Winter Tire Insurance Discounts in Canada: What Each Province Actually Offers

A discount you have to ask for, a legal duty with no discount attached, and a government loan that is not a discount at all — three different things hide behind one phrase. Sorted province by province, with the math.

Published October 18, 2026 · Last updated October 18, 2026

← All guides

“Winter tires save you money on insurance” is the kind of sentence that is true in one province, optional in most, and quietly misleading in one — because the Manitoba program people describe as help with winter tires is a loan you repay, not a reduction in what you pay. With changeover season open, this guide separates the three mechanisms, names the insurer windows that decide whether a discount actually applies, and does the arithmetic honestly: the discount is real, it is modest, and the safety case was always the larger number.

Three different things get called a winter tire discount

A mandated premium discount. Only one province requires insurers to offer one: Ontario, where the discount has been mandatory for all insurers since January 1, 2016. The province does not set the size of it. Ratehub puts the usual range at 3 to 5 percent, set by each insurer, and Ontario's own announcement of the discount tells drivers to contact their insurance company first to determine the amount offered and the documentation required to verify winter-tire use. A mandated offer is not an automatic credit — an insurer cannot apply a discount it does not know you qualify for.

A legal requirement with no discount attached. Quebec requires winter tires province-wide, as this site's Quebec guide covers in detail, and British Columbia requires them on designated highways. Neither duty comes with a mandated premium reduction. Rates.ca puts the fines for driving without winter tires where required at $121 in BC and up to $300 in Quebec. In BC the compliance question is about the route, not the premium: on designated highways a passenger vehicle needs tires carrying the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol or an M+S marking, in good condition with at least 3.5 mm of tread, and a driver without them can be turned back as well as fined $121.

Financing that is not a discount. Manitoba Public Insurance runs a Winter Tire Program that lends eligible customers up to $2,000 per vehicle toward winter tires and related costs, repayable over one to four years through participating retailers. That is genuinely useful in October, when the tire bill and the installation bill land together. But it is debt at low interest, not a reduction in premium or price, and it should be compared against paying cash — not counted as savings.

Province by province: what actually exists

Ontario is the discount province, and its details reward precision. The mandate covers the offer, not the amount, the dates or the proof — all three are the insurer's. Ontario sets no fixed installation dates in law; the insurer sets them. Alberta sits in the voluntary column with one named example: Rates.ca reports that Allstate offers a winter tire discount to drivers in Ontario and Alberta exclusively, which is a reminder that in voluntary provinces the answer depends entirely on which company holds the policy. Quebec drivers should not assume the mandate earns a reduction — availability there depends on the provider, and the discount, where it exists, still has to be requested. British Columbia drivers on designated routes are meeting a legal standard, not earning a rebate; whether an individual policy rewards winter tires is a separate question for the broker. In the remaining provinces, Ratehub's summary is the honest one: insurers decide whether to offer a discount and how much it is, which makes it a question to ask at renewal rather than a fact to assume.

Manitoba deserves a second paragraph this year, because the program changed on October 1, 2026. MPI announced that from that date, only manufacturer-designated winter tires displaying the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol remain eligible for Winter Tire Program financing. The practical consequence is at the tire counter: an all-weather tire that carries the snowflake symbol — and many do — no longer qualifies if it is not designated and marketed as a winter tire. MPI also reports the program's scale and its safety rationale: more than 287,000 loans worth over $387 million since the program began in 2014, over 670 participating retailers who can confirm eligibility and start the loan paperwork, and MPI's finding that winter tires reduce collisions by 6.3 percent.

The insurer windows that decide whether you qualify

The most common way to lose a discount you have earned is a calendar, not a tire. Each insurer sets its own winter window, and the tires must be on the car for that window — which is set by the policy, not by the weather or by a provincial deadline. Two published examples show how much the windows differ. Rates.ca reports that TD Insurance's winter tire dates in Ontario run from December 1 to March 30, while the CAA insurance discount applies when tires are installed by November 20 and kept on until April 15. A driver who books a late-November changeover to satisfy one insurer could miss the other's install-by date entirely. This is the late-October task that matters: before booking the changeover, get your own insurer's two dates — install by, and keep on until — and book inside them.

Proof is the second trap. Surex's Ontario explainer notes that some insurers may request proof such as tire purchase receipts, photos or installer certification during claims or renewals, while in other cases a declaration is enough — and advises keeping the documentation in case of an audit or a claim. The safe habit is to keep both the tire invoice and the installation invoice every season, photographed, whether or not the insurer asked. A discount challenged at claim time, with no paperwork, is a discount that can disappear at the worst moment.

What the discount is worth: worked math

Run the numbers on an illustrative Ontario premium of $1,800 a year — an example figure for the arithmetic, not a quote. At 3 percent, the discount is $54 a year. At 5 percent, it is $90 a year. Held across five winters on the same terms, that is $270 to $450, which brackets Ratehub's published estimate that installing winter tires could save Ontario drivers upwards of $600 over five years on higher premiums. Set against the cost of a winter set, the discount alone does not pay for the tires, and no honest guide should pretend otherwise. What it does is shave the net cost of a purchase the safety case already justifies: the Ontario government cites winter tires in good condition shortening braking distances by as much as 25 percent, and MPI's collision figure — 6.3 percent fewer collisions — is the scale of benefit a premium percentage cannot express. Claim the discount because it is there. Buy the tires because of the stopping distance.

How to claim it: the procedure

  1. Call before the changeover, not after. Ask your insurer or broker three things: do you offer a winter tire discount, what percentage is it on this policy, and what are your exact install-by and keep-on dates?
  2. Confirm what counts as a winter tire on your policy. Ask whether the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol is required, and whether an all-weather tire qualifies. Do not assume — Manitoba's program just changed its answer to that question.
  3. Book the installation inside the window. Use the booking-timing guidance in this site's installation guide, then check the appointment date against the insurer's install-by date, not just the weather.
  4. Keep both invoices. Tire purchase and installation, with dates and the tire model on them. Photograph them the day you get them.
  5. Declare it and confirm it landed. Tell the insurer the tires are on, give the installation date, and ask for the discount to be confirmed on the policy — then check the next statement or renewal document for the reduced premium.
  6. Repeat the question at every renewal and every insurer switch. The discount does not reliably follow you. A new insurer starts from no.

What success looks like: the discount appears as a visible reduction on your policy documents, the insurer's window dates are in your calendar alongside the changeover booking, and both invoices are filed where you could produce them during a claim.

Our take

The discount's real value is as a prompt: it forces one phone call in October in which you also confirm your insurer's dates and proof rules. Drivers who make that call stop losing money to calendars. Drivers who assume the discount is automatic are the ones who never receive it.

FAQ

Is the winter tire discount automatic in Ontario? No. Insurers must offer it, but the driver has to tell the insurer the tires are installed and meet that insurer's conditions. Ontario's own guidance is to contact the insurer to learn the amount and the documentation required.

Do all-weather tires qualify? It depends on the insurer and the program, and the ground is shifting: from October 1, 2026, MPI financing in Manitoba covers only designated winter tires with the mountain-snowflake symbol. Ask your own insurer before counting an all-weather set.

Does Quebec's mandate come with a discount? No mandated discount accompanies the Quebec requirement. Some insurers may offer one voluntarily; it has to be requested.

Is the Manitoba program a discount? No. It is low-interest financing of up to $2,000 per vehicle over one to four years. It spreads the cost; it does not reduce the premium.
