Winter Tire Pressure, Cold Drops and TPMS Warnings

The first cold snap lights up dashboards across Canada every fall. Most of those warnings are simple physics, a few are real trouble — and the difference is a five-minute cold check done properly.

Published October 15, 2026 · Last updated October 15, 2026

← All guides

Every fall the pattern repeats: a mild October, a sudden cold night, and a dashboard full of tire-pressure warnings the next morning. Nothing has punctured anything. The air inside the tires has simply contracted as the temperature fell, and the pressure fell with it. Understanding that drop — how big it gets, when to top up, and when a warning means something else entirely — turns a worrying light into a short, routine job.

Why pressure falls when the temperature falls

Transport Canada puts a number on the physics: tire pressure drops by about 1 psi (7 kPa) for every 5°C the air temperature falls. That sounds small until a Canadian cold swing does the arithmetic. A 20°C drop — an ordinary January cold snap in much of the country — costs about 4 psi. A 40°C swing, from a mild fall afternoon to a deep-freeze night, costs about 8 psi. No leak is required. The air is still in the tire; it is just taking up less space.

Temperature is not the only quiet drain. Transport Canada also notes that tires lose roughly 2 psi (14 kPa) per month through permeability — air slowly passing through the tire itself. The two effects stack. A set of tires filled correctly in September, never checked, and then parked through the first serious cold snap can easily be 6 to 10 psi low by December, with no puncture, no damage, and no visible flatness. That is why a visual glance is not a check: a tire can look normal while carrying far less air than it should.

The consequences of running low are not cosmetic. Transport Canada estimates that driving with just one tire underinflated by 8 psi can shorten that tire's life by about 15,000 km and raise fuel consumption by about 4%. Underinflated tires also flex more at the sidewall, build heat, steer sluggishly and stop longer — and in extreme cold, Yukon RCMP warn, a badly underinflated tire can even lose its seal against the rim, which risks the tire coming off the rim and a loss of control.

The worked example: heated garage to cold overnight park

Here is the trap that catches garage-parked cars, verified step by step from the Transport Canada figures above. Suppose the tires are set exactly to the door-placard pressure inside a heated garage at +20°C. The car then parks outside overnight at −20°C. That is a 40°C fall, at about 1 psi per 5°C: roughly 8 psi gone by morning, before any permeability loss is counted. Transport Canada makes the same point in plain terms: a tire at good pressure in a warm garage will be underinflated once it is below zero outside.

Use the table as a sanity check, not a substitute for a gauge. If the measured drop roughly matches the temperature swing, cold contraction explains it: top up cold and move on. If one tire is far lower than the other three, or the same tire keeps dropping week after week, temperature is not the explanation — that pattern points to a slow leak, a valve problem, or bead corrosion, and it needs a shop, not another top-up.

Solid light, flashing light, no light: reading TPMS properly

The tire pressure monitoring system is a threshold alarm, not a pressure gauge, and its limits matter. Vehicle documentation is explicit on two points. First, the low-pressure warning illuminates when one or more tires is significantly underinflated — by the time the light appears, the tire is not slightly low, it is substantially low, and the correct response is to stop, check, and inflate to the proper pressure as soon as practical. Second, TPMS is not a substitute for maintenance: a driver is expected to keep pressure correct even when underinflation has not yet reached the level that triggers the light. A dark dashboard is not proof of correct pressure.

The light's behaviour separates the two very different problems it can report:

Winter adds its own sensor wear. Canadian tire retailers note that TPMS faults in winter commonly trace to corrosion, a low sensor battery, or a damaged valve stem — while stressing that most winter warnings are still simple cold-related pressure drops. Salt and slush are hard on valve hardware, which is one more reason the valve caps matter: Transport Canada advises making sure the pump hose and tire valve are free of snow and debris before inflating, and putting the cap back on when finished.

The cold-check procedure

This is the routine that settles every TPMS argument. It takes about five minutes, once a month, and after any sharp temperature drop — retailers who service Canadian winters suggest checking every two weeks, or whenever the temperature drops suddenly.

  1. Start cold. Measure before driving, or after the vehicle has sat for at least three hours. Transport Canada's definition of cold is precise: stationary for at least three hours, or driven no more than 2 km. A reading taken after a highway drive is a warm reading and will look better than the truth.
  2. Find the right target number. Read the recommended pressure from the tire information label — usually on the driver's door edge or door post, sometimes in the glove box or fuel door — or from the owner's manual. Front and rear pressures can differ. Never use the maximum pressure molded on the tire sidewall; that figure is a limit, not the vehicle's recommendation.
  3. Use a real gauge. Transport Canada notes that pocket gauges sold at auto-supply stores are generally more accurate than the gauges on gas-station air pumps. Michelin gives the same procedure: press the gauge firmly onto the valve stem, read, and compare against the door sticker figure.
  4. Check all four, plus the spare. A spare that has lost pressure in the trunk is no help at the roadside in February. Note which tire reads what; a single outlier is diagnostic information, not a nuisance.
  5. Adjust to the placard figure, exactly. Add air if a tire is below the figure; release air if it is above. Clear snow and debris from the valve first, and replace the cap afterwards.
  6. Recheck after the next cold night. One follow-up cold reading confirms the top-up held and the warning stays off at the temperature that triggered it.

What success looks like: all four road tires read at the placard pressure on a cold morning, the spare reads at its specified pressure, and the TPMS light stays off through the next cold start — including the coldest morning of the week, not just the mild afternoon the air was added.

Our take

The advice sometimes given to wait, because pressure rises again once driving warms the tires, is the one piece of winter pressure advice to ignore. A warm reading that barely clears the threshold means the cold tire — the one that does the first, coldest kilometres of every trip — is underinflated. Check cold, fill to the placard, and let the warm reading take care of itself.

Overfilling to get ahead of the cold: why it backfires

The tempting workaround — adding a few extra psi in fall to pre-pay for winter's drop — trades one problem for another. An overinflated tire rides on just the centre portion of its tread, Transport Canada warns: the contact patch shrinks, grip falls, the ride turns harsh, and the centre of the tread wears early. On ice, where the whole case for winter tires is a flexible, full contact patch, deliberately shrinking that patch is the wrong direction. Pressure also has to be corrected when the weather warms again, and a tire set high for January is overinflated by March. The placard figure, checked monthly and after cold snaps, already accounts for the season. There is no seasonal bonus figure, and retailers who publish winter pressure guides say the same thing plainly: use the vehicle's recommended pressure — not higher, not lower.

When a top-up does not fix it: leaks, valves and sensors

Three patterns mean the problem is hardware, not weather. First, one tire repeatedly lower than the rest: temperature acts on all four tires roughly equally, so a lone low tire has a leak until proven otherwise — a nail in the tread, a corroded bead seat, or a leaking valve. Second, a warning that returns within days of a correct cold top-up at stable temperatures. Third, the flashing-then-solid light, which vehicle documentation defines as a system malfunction regardless of pressure. Add a fourth, northern pattern from the Yukon RCMP advisory: in extreme cold, a tire losing air or starting to come off its rim is a do-not-drive problem — seek roadside assistance rather than limping to a pump. TPMS sensor batteries also simply age out, and a sensor that has died will not revive in spring. All four patterns earn a shop visit; none of them is solved by more air.

FAQ

Should winter tires run at a different pressure than summer tires? No separate winter figure exists on the placard. Use the vehicle manufacturer's recommended pressure year-round, measured cold, and expect to add air as the temperature falls.

The light went off after driving. Is everything fine? Not necessarily. Driving warmed the tires and lifted pressure past the warning threshold. Do a proper cold check the next morning; that reading decides.

How often should pressure be checked in winter? At least once a month by Transport Canada's guidance, before long trips, and after sharp temperature drops. Every two weeks in the coldest months is a reasonable, published Canadian practice.

One tire keeps triggering the light. Why? Temperature affects the whole set. A single repeat offender points to a slow leak, valve or bead problem, or a failing sensor — have it inspected instead of repeatedly topping it up.
