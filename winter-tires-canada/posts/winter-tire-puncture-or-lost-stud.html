Winter Tire Puncture or Lost Stud Mid-Season: Repair, Plug, or Replace?

Two mid-season problems, two different answers. A tread puncture may be repairable under a strict set of limits. A missing stud, according to the tire maker, is not a part to slot back in.

Published October 27, 2026 · Last updated October 27, 2026

← All guides

Can a punctured winter tire be repaired, and can a missing stud simply be replaced? The first answer is sometimes, under conditions a manufacturer’s repair manual states in plain numbers. The second answer, from Nokian Tyres, is no — and that answer matters because other websites say the opposite. Start by separating the symptoms, because a slow leak, a flat and a missing stud are not the same job and do not share the same decision.

First, name the symptom correctly

A tire losing air points to a puncture or another leak, and the repair rules below decide whether that tire can return to service. A studded tire with a stud gone has not necessarily lost air at all; its problem is the missing pin and what that absence says about the rest of the set. Treating the two as one problem produces the two classic errors: plugging a tire that should have been retired, and trying to restud a tire the maker says should be left alone.

Either way, the first move is inspection, not driving on hope. A puncture repair done properly starts with the tire off the wheel, which places the work in a shop’s hands rather than at the side of the road.

Punctures: the repair limits, in numbers

The Bridgestone/Firestone maintenance manual sets the boundary as a list of injuries that must never be repaired. Read it as a gate. If any one item applies, the repair conversation is over and replacement is the remaining option.

  • The puncture is larger than 1/4 inch (6 mm).
  • The injury sits outside the tread area.
  • Remaining tread depth is below 2/32 inch.
  • The injury was previously repaired improperly.

Those four lines are the spec verification for this whole topic. They do not mention brand, age in seasons or how inconvenient a replacement would be in February. A puncture inside the tread, at or under 1/4 inch (6 mm), on a tire with tread remaining above 2/32 inch and no earlier bad repair, passes the gate and can be considered for a proper repair. Anything else does not.

What a proper repair actually involves

A plug pushed in from outside, on its own, is not the repair the manual describes. The full procedure, as the manual specifies it, runs in this order:

  1. Demount the tire from the wheel so the inside can be reached.
  2. Inspect the interior before any repair material is used, to confirm the injury matches what was seen from outside.
  3. Fill the injury with a plug or stem through the puncture itself.
  4. Seal the inside with a patch over the injury, so the repair has both parts: the fill and the interior seal.
  5. Confirm the repair restores air retention and seals the injury, which the manual ties to preventing rust and ply separation.

What success looks like: the repaired tire holds air because both halves of the repair are present — the injury is filled and the interior is sealed — and none of the four never-repair conditions applied. A repair missing the interior inspection or the patch is not a cheaper version of this procedure. It is a different, lesser job.

Lost studs: the claim online, and the maker’s answer

Here the sources disagree openly, so both deserve to be quoted fairly. A general tire site, vehiclestire.com, claims studs on winter tires can be replaced when they loosen or fall out, and suggests professional tools and size matching for any stud replacement. Nokian Tyres, which makes studded winter tires, states the opposite position: its studs are installed at its factories to secure balance and security, and once the studs are worn or fall out, Nokian does not recommend replacing the studs.

The manufacturer’s word carries this argument. Nokian also states that removing studs from a studded tire does not make it equivalent to an all-season or all-weather tire, which closes the other shortcut drivers sometimes consider: pulling the remaining studs out and treating the set as something it was never built to be.

One honest gap remains. No manufacturer source in this research publishes a number for how many missing studs retire a studded tire. Anyone quoting a precise count is inventing it. The practical call — a single missing pin on a sound set, against studs shedding across the tread — is a judgement for a tire professional inspecting that specific tire, not a specification to be read off a chart.

Repair, replace one, or replace more: the decision order

  1. Is air being lost? If not, and the issue is a missing stud, skip the repair gate entirely and use the stud guidance above: no restudding, professional inspection, judgement on the set.
  2. Does the puncture pass all four limits? Size at or under 1/4 inch (6 mm), inside the tread area, tread above 2/32 inch, no previous improper repair. Any failure ends the repair option.
  3. Can the shop perform both halves of the proper repair? Demounting, interior inspection, plug or stem fill, and interior patch seal. If only an external plug is on offer, that is not the manual’s repair.
  4. If repair is out, the tire is out. Replacement then becomes a question about the remaining set’s condition, to be settled with the shop using the tires in front of them — not a reason to return a failed-gate tire to service.
Our take

Mid-season pressure is exactly when bad repairs get sold. The manual’s four limits and Nokian’s plain “do not replace studs” are short enough to hold onto at a service counter. If a quote contradicts either one, ask which document it is based on.
