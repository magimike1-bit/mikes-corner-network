Recalls & Tire Age

Checking Winter Tire Recalls and DOT Dates Before Installation

The answer is on the sidewall, in one embossed string of characters. Read in full, that string does two jobs before a stored winter set goes back on: it is the key to the recall search, and its last four digits are the tire’s birth date.

← All guides

Do both checks before the installation appointment, not after it. A winter set spends half the year in storage, off the vehicle, with both sidewalls reachable and no pressure to drive anywhere on it. That is the one moment in the set’s life when reading every character moulded into it costs nothing. Once the tires are mounted and the vehicle is back in service, the same check means another trip, another lift, and a set of tires that may have to come straight back off. The check itself is two questions about the same number: has this tire been recalled, and how old is it?

Reading the full DOT number, not just the date

Every tire carries a DOT code embossed on its sidewall — a series of letters and numbers that identifies the manufacturer and the plant, carries size and type information, and ends with the manufacture date. Most guides stop at the last four digits. For a recall check, the whole string matters, because a recall does not describe “tires made around then”; it names specific tires. So record the complete code for each tire, exactly as moulded, before searching anything.

Two reading traps are documented and worth knowing in advance. First, the full DOT code may appear on only one side of the tire — if the outward-facing sidewall shows a partial code without the ending digits, the complete code is on the inner sidewall, which on a stored set is simply the other side of a tire that can be turned over on the garage floor. Second, the date format changed with the century: tires made before 2000 used a three-digit date code — week plus a single year digit — instead of four digits. A stored tire carrying a three-digit code is not a puzzle to solve; it is a tire from before 2000, and its age settles the installation question on its own.

The recall check: database, maker, dealer

Transport Canada maintains the Road Safety Recalls Database, and its advice is to check it regularly for new recalls that may apply to a vehicle. Work the check in three layers, because each layer answers a slightly different question. The database search shows which recall campaigns exist for vehicles and tires. The tire maker’s own recall information connects a campaign to the tires it actually covers — the DOT numbers recorded from the sidewalls are what get compared here. And confirmation for a specific vehicle runs through the channels Transport Canada names: the manufacturer’s VIN-based recall lookup tool, where one is available, or an authorized dealer, who can say by VIN whether recalls exist for that vehicle or have already been repaired.

That a real Canadian tire recall works this way is not theory. In 2021, Cooper Tire’s recall 181 — Transport Canada recall 2021-535 — covered certain Hercules Roadtour 455 Sport tires in size 225/50R17, identified by a specific DOT serial number, U9 40 H3T 0721. The notice identified its subject tires by product name, size and DOT identification number, with the four-digit date code closing the sequence. Owners whose inspection verified an affected tire were to have it replaced, mounted and balanced at no charge when presented within 180 days of the notice — and the notice put the inspection itself at only a few minutes, with about 30 minutes per tire if replacement was required. The pattern generalises: recall remedies from the manufacturer are, as Transport Canada puts it, almost always free of charge, and the expensive version of a recall is the one discovered after the tires are mounted and winter has started.

One distinction keeps the check honest. A vehicle’s VIN identifies the vehicle; it does not identify a set of replacement winter tires bought separately, possibly years after the vehicle, possibly second-hand. That is why the tire check runs off the DOT numbers on the tires themselves, with the VIN check as the vehicle’s parallel record — not as a substitute for reading the sidewalls.

The age check: five years, ten years, and the spare

The last four digits of the code are the date, and the format is fixed: the first two digits are the week of manufacture, from 01 to 52, and the last two are the year. A code ending 2519 was made in the 25th week of 2019. Michelin’s own example reads the same way — a code ending 2214 means the 22nd week of 2014. From that date, two manufacturer milestones govern a stored set. After five years of service, tires should be thoroughly inspected at least once a year by a trained tire professional. And as a precaution, Michelin recommends replacing tires ten years after their date of manufacture, regardless of remaining tread depth or apparent condition — a recommendation that explicitly includes spare tires, the ones most likely to sit unexamined past every milestone.

Age is not the only verdict the sidewall carries into the appointment. Before installation is also when the set gets its winter-fitness read: the peaked mountain with the snowflake, the symbol for tires that meet specific snow traction performance requirements, present on all four; tread depth clear of the close-to-4 mm (5/32 in) level Transport Canada advises against using on snow-covered roads; and pressures checked — Transport Canada advises checking tire pressure often, especially before highway driving — once the set is mounted and cold.

The pre-installation procedure, step by step

  1. Turn each tire to its full DOT code. If the outer sidewall shows only a partial code, read the inner sidewall. Record the complete code for all four tires — and for the spare — in a list, one line per tire.
  2. Decode the last four digits of each. Week first, then year. Write the manufacture year beside each code; any tire at or past ten years from manufacture goes on the replace list before anything else is decided, and any tire past five years of service gets a professional inspection on its record.
  3. Search the recall records. Check Transport Canada’s Road Safety Recalls Database, then the tire maker’s recall information for the brand, model and size, comparing the DOT numbers from the list — a recall names tires by those numbers, not by appearance.
  4. Run the vehicle’s parallel check. Use the vehicle manufacturer’s VIN-based recall lookup if one is available, or ask an authorized dealer to confirm by VIN whether any recalls apply or were already repaired.
  5. Verify the winter basics on the same pass. Mountain-snowflake symbol on all four, tread depth measured, no tire carried into the appointment on the strength of looking fine.
  6. Take the record to the appointment. Hand the installer the list. A recalled or expired tire is dealt with as a replacement — recall remedies are almost always free through the manufacturer — not mounted “for now.”

What success looks like: every tire going onto the vehicle has a recorded DOT number that was actually searched, a decoded manufacture date inside the age limits or inspected on schedule, and a tread and symbol check done the same day — so the installed set’s paperwork is as current as its pressures.

If a tire is recalled or too old: the decision

Three outcomes, and only three. A tire named in a recall goes to the manufacturer’s remedy — repaired or replaced under the recall, almost always free — and does not go on the vehicle in the meantime; Transport Canada’s standing advice is to act as soon as a recall notice applies and follow any interim instructions the manufacturer provides. A tire past the ten-year precaution, recalled or not, is retired on age: tread depth does not overrule the date. And a tire between five and ten years old, with no recall against its numbers, goes on only with its annual professional inspection booked or done. Drivers who bought a vehicle used can close the notification gap for next time by registering the vehicle with its manufacturer and keeping that address current — for a separately bought winter set, the equivalent habit is keeping the purchase record with the DOT list, so the maker’s notices have somewhere to land.
