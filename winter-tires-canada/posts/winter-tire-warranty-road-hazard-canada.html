Winter Tire Warranties and Road-Hazard Plans in Canada: What Is and Is Not Covered

A nail in November is not a warranty claim — unless a second, separate plan says it is. The word “warranty” on a tire invoice covers three different promises, and only one of them pays for road damage.

Published October 24, 2026 · Last updated October 24, 2026

← All guides

Every fall, Canadian drivers buy winter tires described as “fully warrantied,” then learn the limits of that phrase at the worst possible moment: standing at a service counter with a cut sidewall in January. The confusion is structural. Three separate layers of protection can attach to the same set of tires — the manufacturer’s limited warranty, a retailer’s road-hazard plan, and a dealer’s paid tire-and-rim plan — and each one covers a different list of failures. Knowing which layer covers what, before anything goes wrong, is the difference between a free replacement and a full-price surprise.

A dealer plan page states the split plainly — a manufacturer’s warranty covers claims about manufacturing defects in workmanship and materials, while potholes, nails and other road conditions are not covered by it at all. What the manufacturer warranty actually covers

The manufacturer’s promise is about how the tire was built, not about what the road does to it. Michelin’s warranty document is representative: the limited warranty covers defects in workmanship and materials for the life of the original usable tread or six years from the date of purchase, whichever comes first. If a covered defect appears early — 2/32 of an inch (1.6 mm) or less of tread worn, and within 12 months of purchase — the tire is replaced free, including mounting and balancing. Past that point, replacement is pro-rated: the owner pays a share based on the percentage of tread worn, plus mounting, balancing and taxes.

Treadwear coverage, where a model carries it, is narrower than the advertising suggests. Michelin requires the claimant to be the original purchaser, requires rotation and inspection every 10,000 to 12,000 km with a completed service record, and requires the original invoice at claim time. Tires delivered as original equipment on a new vehicle carry no treadwear warranty at all. Nexen’s Canadian brochure draws the same boundary from the other side: its warranty does not cover damage due to misuse, abuse or accident, and it applies only to the original purchaser of new replacement tires. A winter tire that wears out early because the alignment was out, or that dies on a pothole, is outside this layer by design.

What road hazard adds — and its fine print

Road-hazard cover exists precisely to fill the pothole gap, and in Canada it comes in three flavours. Some manufacturers bundle a limited version: Nexen’s Total Coverage program replaces a tire damaged by road hazards — cuts, bruises and impact breaks from nails, glass, potholes and debris — free of charge for the first 2/32 of an inch of tread wear or one year from purchase, whichever comes first, with mounting, balancing and taxes still on the owner.

Retailer plans are the most common form. Kal Tire’s Customer Care Plan is included free with a tire purchase for the original purchaser and works at more than 280 locations across Canada. A tire that fails from road-hazard damage such as cuts, bruises and punctures and cannot be repaired is replaced free within the first 30 days, installation excluded; after 30 days the replacement is pro-rated, calculated as the percentage of tread depth used multiplied by the current selling price, plus installation. Repairable flats are fixed free for the life of the tires. TechNet’s shop-network plan shows the paid add-on version: three years of pro-rated coverage capped at $399.99 per tire and ending at 2/32 of an inch of remaining tread, covering punctures, nail and glass damage, bruises, breaks and pothole or curb damage, with first-year flat-changing assistance up to $75 and flat repair up to $25 per incident. Online sellers add their own variant: Quattro’s road-hazard protection covers accidental damage occurring in Canada on eligible tires, requires a set of four, and sets eligibility windows of 12 months with no more than 2/32 of an inch worn, or 24 months with no more than 4/32 worn.

Dealer tire-and-rim plans sit at the premium end. The Toyota plan sold through Canadian dealers runs four years, covers tire and rim repair or replacement with mounting and balancing, valve stems, taxes and towing, extends to seasonal tires — so the winter set is covered, not just the factory set — and charges zero deductible with no mileage limits or pro-rated adjustments. That is genuinely broader than the retailer plans. It is also a paid service contract, and its value depends entirely on price, which is a decision problem, not a coverage problem.

The winter-specific conditions that decide claims

Four conditions decide most denied claims, and two of them are specific to seasonal tires. First, documentation of the seasonal swap itself: Michelin’s treadwear warranty requires documentation of the timing of installation and removal of winter tires each winter to maintain coverage. A winter set with no install and removal records can lose treadwear protection even if the rotations were done. Second, rotation records: Kal Tire requires rotation and inspection every 8,000 to 10,000 km and reserves the right to require the records for a claim; Michelin’s interval and service-record rule is similar. Third, wear pattern: Quattro states that uneven tire wear automatically voids its road-hazard warranty, and Kal Tire warns that abnormal wear from poor alignment can void its plan. A neglected alignment therefore cancels two layers at once. Fourth, the exclusions that surprise people: TechNet’s plan excludes fire, theft, vandalism, off-road use and accidents, and defects are explicitly the manufacturer’s responsibility, not the plan’s — each layer points at the other, and a claim that fits neither definition is simply unpaid.

Our take

The cheapest warranty protection in Canada is a folder. Keep the original invoice, every rotation and alignment record, and the install and removal dates for each winter season. Most denied claims are paperwork failures on damage that was actually covered.

Decision framework: included plan, paid plan, or neither

Work through four questions in order. 1. What did the purchase already include? If the tires came from a retailer whose plan is bundled free, the baseline is already decent: free flat repairs and an early free-replacement window. Paying a dealer for overlapping cover buys mainly the rim coverage and the no-pro-ration terms. 2. How expensive is one tire? Pro-rated plans shrink in value as tread wears, because the owner’s share grows with every 32nd of an inch used. On an inexpensive winter tire, a pro-rated credit late in the tire’s life can be worth less than the plan premium; on a large, expensive fitment, even a pro-rated replacement beats paying full price. 3. Where is the damage risk? Pothole-heavy urban commuting and gravel or construction routes raise the value of road-hazard cover; gentle highway use on well-kept roads lowers it. 4. Are rims in the risk picture? Only the dealer layer routinely covers rims. A vehicle on low-profile tires and expensive alloy wheels is the strongest case for the paid plan; a vehicle on inexpensive steel winter wheels has little rim value to protect. If the answers are included plan, modest tire price, low damage risk and steel wheels, the paid plan is hard to justify. Any two answers pointing the other way make it a reasonable buy — at a negotiated price, with the exclusions read first.

FAQ

Does a manufacturer warranty cover a puncture? No. Manufacturer warranties cover defects in workmanship and materials. Punctures, cuts and impact damage are road hazards, covered only by a road-hazard plan from the manufacturer’s program, the retailer, or a dealer contract.

Is a road-hazard replacement always free? Rarely for long. Typical Canadian terms are free replacement only in a short early window — 30 days at Kal Tire, or the first 2/32 of an inch of wear or one year under Nexen’s program — then pro-rated by tread used, with installation and taxes usually extra.

Can a claim be denied for maintenance? Yes. Missed rotations, missing service records, abnormal or uneven wear, and — for winter tires — missing installation and removal documentation are all stated grounds in Canadian plan terms.

Does a dealer plan cover winter tires bought elsewhere? Plans differ. The Toyota plan described here extends to seasonal tires and covers tires purchased and installed at a dealer; a set bought from an independent shop may fall outside a dealer contract. Read the eligibility line before assuming the winter set is covered.
