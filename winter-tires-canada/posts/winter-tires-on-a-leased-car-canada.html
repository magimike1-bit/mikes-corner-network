Winter Tires on a Leased Car in Canada: What to Return, What to Keep

The confident rule that a leased car must go back on its exact factory tires is not what published Canadian lease guides say. They differ by brand, by rim, and even by the month of the return.

Published November 7, 2026 · Last updated November 7, 2026

← All guides

Ask around and a confident answer comes back quickly: a leased car has to go back on its exact factory tires, so winter tires are a problem. Published Canadian lease guides do not support that as a universal rule. Some lessors accept winter tires at return under stated conditions. Others accept them only in winter months. One asks for non-winter tires, then carves out a Quebec exception. The rule that governs a return is the one in that vehicle's lease agreement and its lessor's wear and tear guide, and those documents differ by brand.

That distinction matters before any money is spent. A winter set bought the wrong way can create a return problem that a different mounting choice would have avoided entirely.

General information only: lease terms vary by lessor and by agreement. Read the tire clauses in your own lease and wear and tear guide before buying, and treat the brand examples below as examples, not as your contract.

What three published Canadian guides actually say

Start with the two clearest tread numbers, because both come from the lessors themselves. Ford Canada's wear and tear guide sets the floor at 3.2 mm of tread in all grooves: below that, a full replacement charge applies. Nissan Canada's end-of-lease guide uses the same depth, 3.2 mm, measured at the tire's minimum-thickness point. A set that is legal to drive on can still fall short of a lease floor, so the return check uses the lessor's number, not the road rule.

Read that table as a warning against generalising. Ford and Lincoln publish an original rim condition. Nissan publishes a calendar condition. MINI publishes a non-winter default. None of the three approaches can be borrowed and applied to another brand's lease.

The Quebec exception, and why return timing counts

The MINI checklist adds the detail most summaries miss. In accordance with the Quebec Highway Safety Code, during the prescribed winter period MINI Financial Services will accept installed winter tires if the non-winter tires are placed in the vehicle, in tire bags. The checklist marks that exception as applicable in the province of Quebec only.

Two practical points follow.

First, the date on the return appointment can change what counts as acceptable. Nissan's window is the plainest example: the same winter set is accepted in February and not accepted in May. Second, keeping the other set complete, undamaged, and easy to present is part of the return plan in Quebec, not an afterthought. Tire bags and the stored set belong on the checklist months before the appointment, not the night before.

Why the factory set is worth protecting

Every guide above judges the tires that are on the car at return. The simplest way to pass that judgement is to return the factory set with its tread largely intact, which is what a separate winter set achieves: winter kilometres, salt, and pothole season accumulate on the winter tires, while the factory set sits in storage.

That is a decision framework, not a guarantee. It works only if three conditions hold:

  • The factory set is stored properly and comes back complete, with its original wheels where the guide expects them.
  • The factory set still meets the lessor's tread floor and has no sidewall damage when it goes back on.
  • The winter set itself meets the vehicle manufacturer's size, load index, and speed rating requirements while it is on the car.

If the lease will be bought out at the end, the calculus changes and return condition matters far less. If the car is going back, protecting the factory set is usually the safer default, subject to what the agreement actually says.

Winter tires on their own rims versus swapping onto the factory rims

There are two ways to run a winter set on a leased car. The tires can share the factory rims, which means a shop removes and remounts tires twice a year. Or the winter tires can live on their own rims, which means each seasonal visit is a wheel swap, and the factory tires are never taken off their rims at all.

Collected Canadian prices, checked on November 7, 2026, put a swap of tires already on rims at $40 to $80 for a set of four, and mounting and balancing a set not on rims at $80 to $160. Cansumer's five-year breakdown makes the comparison concrete:

Those are collected figures, not a quote for a specific car, and a short lease changes the arithmetic. On a two or three year lease the second set of rims may not pay back before the car goes back, unless the rims fit the next vehicle or can be sold. Run the same calculation with the remaining term, not with a five year horizon that the lease does not have.

Cost is only half the comparison. Cansumer also notes that limiting how often a tire is removed and mounted reduces the chance of damaging the bead, and that repeated mounting stresses the bead and sidewall. For a leased car there is a second, simpler benefit: the factory wheels stay assembled and untouched, so refitting them for a return appointment is a swap, not a remounting job booked against a deadline. Note the Ford and Lincoln wording again here, though. Their no-charge condition is winter tires left on the original rim, which is a different return picture from a winter set arriving on its own steel rims with no factory set presented. What the lessor wants to see at return decides which setup is safer, and that answer is in the guide, not in general advice.

Do's and don'ts for a leased car on winter tires

  • Do read the tire section of the lease agreement and the wear and tear guide before buying a winter set, and keep both documents with the car's records.
  • Do keep the factory set complete and stored as a set, with any damage noted and dealt with early.
  • Do book the complimentary or pre-return inspection where the lessor offers one. Nissan, for example, describes scheduling its inspection from 90 days up to 14 days before lease end, and Ford frames its self-assessment the same way: early enough to correct problems before the final inspection.
  • Do not return the car on a mismatched set, a set below the lessor's tread floor, or tires with sidewall damage, plugs, or exposed cords. Each of those is named as chargeable in the guides above.
  • Do not assume a winter return is fine outside a stated seasonal window, and do not return on studded tires where the guide lists them as excessive, as Nissan's does.
  • Do not discard, sell, or lend out the factory set during the term. A missing factory set turns a routine return into a replacement bill.

One boundary is worth stating plainly. Mounting and balancing tires is shop work with proper equipment, and any question about whether a specific tire, wheel, or repair satisfies a lease clause belongs with the lessor or the returning dealer, answered in writing where a charge could follow. A tire shop can judge condition; only the lessor can judge its own contract.

The return-day checklist

  1. Confirm from the agreement which set the car should be returned on, and whether a seasonal window or a provincial exception applies to the return date.
  2. Measure tread on the set going back, at the lowest point, and compare it with the lessor's floor, 3.2 mm in both the Ford and Nissan guides.
  3. Check every sidewall for cuts, bulges, plugs, and exposed cords, and confirm the set matches in type, size, and rating.
  4. Refit the required set in good time before the appointment, including the spare and tools where the guide lists them.
  5. In Quebec, if returning on installed winter tires under an exception like MINI's, place the non-winter tires in the vehicle in tire bags.
  6. Keep the inspection report and return receipt with the lease paperwork.

Handled that way, winter tires and a lease are not in conflict. The conflict only appears when a brand-specific rule is assumed instead of read.
