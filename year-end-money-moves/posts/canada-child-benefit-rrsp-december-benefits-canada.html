Canada Child Benefit and Benefit Clawbacks: How a December RRSP Contribution Changes Next Year's Benefits

The contribution does not have to happen in December, and the benefit does not change in January. What matters is the tax year the deduction is claimed in — and that effect can be priced, exactly, before a dollar moves. General information only — not tax or financial advice.

First, who should not make this move

Start with the stop line, because for a large group of families this strategy returns nothing on the benefits side, and December is a bad month to learn that after the money has moved.

Skip it, on benefits grounds, if any of these hold:

  • Your adjusted family net income is already at or below $38,237. Below that threshold the Canada Child Benefit (CCB) is paid at the maximum with no reduction. A deduction cannot restore a reduction that does not exist.
  • You have no RRSP deduction room. The 2026 limit is 18% of prior-year earned income up to $33,810, plus unused room carried forward — and only contributions within that limit can be deducted. Your exact figure is on your notice of assessment or in CRA My Account.
  • Next year's income will be much higher than this year's. A deduction does not have to be claimed in the year the contribution is made; it can be carried forward. A deduction banked for a higher-income year may be worth more in tax than the benefit gain it would buy this year.
  • The contribution would empty your cash buffer. Money inside an RRSP is not an emergency fund. If the $5,000 is the household's only liquid reserve, the benefit arithmetic below is irrelevant — keep the cash.

If none of those apply, the move is worth pricing. Here is the machinery.

The one line that runs the benefits system

The CCB is a tax-free monthly payment, and it does not have to be reported on a tax return. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) sizes it from one number: adjusted family net income, or AFNI. AFNI starts with family net income — line 23600 of your return, plus line 23600 of your spouse or common-law partner's return — then subtracts any Universal Child Care Benefit and registered disability savings plan income received, and adds back any amounts of those that were repaid. For most families today, AFNI is close to the two line 23600 figures added together.

Line 23600 is total income (line 15000) minus the deductions claimed on lines 20700 to 23500. That placement is the whole story. An RRSP deduction is claimed on line 20800 — inside that range — so every dollar of RRSP deduction lowers line 23600 by a dollar, and lowers AFNI by a dollar. A non-refundable credit works differently: it reduces the tax payable, but it sits below line 23600 and leaves net income exactly where it was. Only deductions move the benefits number; credits do not.

The same line 23600 base is used to work out the GST/HST credit — replaced in July 2026 by the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit, with the same calculation structure, per the CRA — the social benefits repayment, and some other credits. Filing a return is what puts a household into these calculations at all: the CRA determines CCB entitlement each year when the return is filed, and filing automatically considers the filer for the groceries benefit and related provincial and territorial programs, even in a year with no income.

The rate table: what a dollar of deduction buys

Once AFNI passes $38,237, the CCB is reduced by a percentage of the income above that threshold. The percentage depends on how many eligible children are in the family, and it steps down at $82,847, where a fixed amount plus a smaller percentage applies. These are the CRA's published rates for the July 2026 to June 2027 payment period:

Read the table as a price list. Inside the first band, each $1,000 of deduction buys back $70 of CCB a year for a one-child family, $135 for two children, $190 for three, and $230 for four or more — paid across a full benefit year, on top of whatever the same deduction saves in tax. Above $82,847 the same $1,000 buys back less: $32, $57, $80 or $95 by the same family sizes. The maximum CCB itself, before any reduction, is $8,157 per year for each child under 6 and $6,883 per year for each child aged 6 to 17, for the July 2026 to June 2027 period. A CCB payment can also carry the child disability benefit — up to $3,480 per eligible child in that period — and related provincial and territorial programs the CRA administers, which is one more reason the family net income figure deserves attention in December.

A worked example: $5,000 in, priced over a full benefit year

Take a two-parent family with two children under 6 and a 2026 AFNI of $70,000 before any RRSP decision. The illustration uses the CRA's currently published maximums and thresholds; the amounts for the benefit year their 2026 return will actually drive are indexed, so treat the dollars as the shape of the answer, not a promise.

  • Maximum CCB: 2 × $8,157 = $16,314 per year.
  • Reduction at $70,000 of AFNI: $70,000 − $38,237 = $31,763 above the threshold; at the two-child rate of 13.5%, the reduction is $4,288. Annual CCB: $16,314 − $4,288 = $12,026.
  • Now claim a $5,000 RRSP deduction on the 2026 return. Line 23600 falls by $5,000, so AFNI falls to $65,000. The excess over the threshold is $26,763; the reduction is 13.5% of that, $3,613. Annual CCB: $16,314 − $3,613 = $12,701.
  • Difference: $675 more CCB across the benefit year — about $56 more per month — from the benefits side alone. The tax saving on the same $5,000 deduction is separate, and depends on the contributor's marginal rate.

Two cautions belong with that $675. First, the timing: CCB payments are recalculated every July from the previous year's AFNI. Payments from July 2026 to June 2027 rest on 2025 income; a deduction claimed on the 2026 return changes payments from July 2027 to June 2028. A December 2026 contribution is a decision about benefits that arrive starting seven months later and run for twelve. Second, the example sits neatly inside one band. A deduction large enough to cross the $82,847 or $38,237 boundary prices in two pieces, at two different rates — the table, not the single percentage, is the tool.

December or the first 60 days: separating the two decisions

The most repeated error in this area is treating December 31 as the RRSP deadline. It is not. Contributions made in the first 60 days of 2027, up to and including March 1, 2027, can still be deducted on the 2026 return, within the deduction limit. December 31 is a hard deadline for other year-end items — FHSA contributions and charitable donations among them — but the RRSP is not one of them, and unused RRSP room carries forward rather than expiring.

So December's real job is narrower: estimate the year's income while there is still time to act, and decide the size of the deduction the 2026 return should carry. The contribution itself can wait until January or February, when 2026 income is a known figure instead of a forecast. What cannot wait past March 1, 2027 is the pairing of contribution and deduction year. Contribute after that date, or claim the deduction in a later year, and the benefit effect moves with the deduction — a deduction claimed on the 2027 return reprices benefits for July 2028 to June 2029, not the year in between.

The decision framework: (1) Add up both partners' expected line 23600 figures for 2026. If the total is at or below $38,237, stop — there is no CCB reduction to buy back. (2) Locate the total in the rate table and note the percentage for your number of children; that percentage, applied to a contribution, is the annual benefit gain. (3) Confirm RRSP room on the notice of assessment before sizing anything. (4) Decide the deduction year deliberately: claim in 2026 for the July 2027 benefit year, or carry the deduction forward if a higher-income year is coming — knowing the benefit gain moves with that choice. (5) Make the contribution by March 1, 2027 at the latest, and keep the receipt with the return it belongs to.

One household shape deserves a specific note: with shared custody, each parent receives 50% of what they would have received with full custody, calculated on their own AFNI. The deduction decision in a shared-custody household prices against one parent's family income, not a combined household figure, and the other parent's return does not dilute it.

The decision, stated plainly

A December RRSP contribution changes next year's benefits only through the deduction claimed on this year's return, and only for families whose income sits inside the reduction bands. Inside those bands, the gain is not vague: it is the table rate for your family size, multiplied by the deduction, paid over a full benefit year starting the following July. Price it with the table, check it against the stop lines at the top of this article, and let the contribution date — December, January or February — be the least important part of the decision.
