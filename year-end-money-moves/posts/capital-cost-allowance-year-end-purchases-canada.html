Year-End Equipment Purchases and CCA: Available-for-Use Beats the Invoice Date

"Buy it before December 31" is only half the rule. Capital cost allowance starts when the asset is available for use — and the first year is deliberately throttled. General information only — your accountant's advice wins.

The pool system in one paragraph

Capital cost allowance (CCA) is how Canadian tax law lets a business deduct the cost of durable equipment over time. Assets are grouped into classes — pools of similar property — and each class carries a prescribed rate applied on a declining-balance basis: the deduction is a percentage of what remains undeducted (the undepreciated capital cost, or UCC), so it is larger in the early years and tapers off. Published summaries put the familiar rates at 20% for most furniture and fixtures, 30% for automotive equipment, and 4% to 10% for most buildings. Two features matter at year-end: you may claim any amount up to the maximum for the year (claiming less is allowed), and if an asset is later sold for more than the class's remaining UCC, the excess can come back into income as recapture. CCA is a timing system, not a gift — which is exactly why its two timing rules deserve a December of their own.

Timing rule one: available for use

CCA generally cannot be claimed until the taxation year the property becomes available for use. Signing a purchase agreement, paying a deposit, or even paying in full does not start the clock. For most property other than a building, the available-for-use date is generally the earlier of the date you first use the property to earn income, or the second tax year after the year you acquired it — a backstop that exists so assets stuck in prolonged setup do not sit in limbo forever. Buildings have their own version of the test, generally tied to when at least 90% of the building is used for its intended purpose, with the same second-year backstop.

The practical consequence is the one December shoppers miss: order custom equipment in November 2026, take delivery and put it to work in March 2027, and the first CCA claim belongs to the 2027 return — no matter what the invoice says. A purchase that will genuinely be delivered, installed and earning income before the year ends is a different case from one that merely gets paid for. If a supplier cannot commit to a working installation date, price the deal as a next-year deduction, because that is what it is.

Timing rule two: the half-year rule

In the year an asset first becomes available for use, the half-year rule (Income Tax Regulations, Regulation 1100(2)) generally limits the claim to half of the year's net additions to the class. Net additions means the cost of property added during the year minus dispositions from the same class: buy $50,000 of Class 8 furniture and sell $10,000 of old furniture from the same class, and the net addition is $40,000 — of which $20,000 forms the first-year base, with the other half folded into the pool for the following year. The logic is averaging: purchases land throughout the year, so a full first-year deduction on every one of them would overstate the period of use. Note that enhanced first-year incentives for some property have existed in recent years and the rules in this area change; confirm the current treatment for your asset class with the CRA's CCA pages or your accountant rather than assuming either the plain half-year rule or an enhancement applies.

One more limiter for new businesses: if the fiscal period is shorter than 365 days, the CCA claim is prorated by days. A worked example from the CRA's self-employed guidance pattern: a claim of $3,500 in a 214-day fiscal period becomes $3,500 × 214 ÷ 365 = $2,052.

The two-year math, worked once, slowly

Purchase$20,000 of equipment, Class 8 (20%), available for use this year—

Read the table the way a cash-flow plan should: a $20,000 December purchase produces a $2,000 first-year deduction under the plain half-year rule — one-tenth of the sticker price, not the sticker price. Anyone selling year-end equipment on a "write it all off this year" pitch is describing a rule that does not exist for ordinary equipment. The deduction is real; it is simply spread, and the spreading is the plan.

The year-end purchase checklist

  1. Confirm the class and rate before you buy. The wrong class means the wrong rate — and the wrong rate is exactly what gets adjusted on review. Identify the CCA class from the CRA's class list while the purchase is still a plan.
  2. Get the in-service date in writing. Delivery, installation and first income-earning use — the supplier's commitment on those dates decides which return the deduction lands on.
  3. Run the net-additions number. Add this year's purchases in the class, subtract this year's dispositions in the same class, halve the result for the first-year base, then apply the class rate. That is the honest first-year figure for the cash-flow plan.
  4. Prorate if your fiscal period is short. Days in the period ÷ 365, applied to the computed claim.
  5. Decide how much to claim — it is a choice. CCA up to the maximum may be claimed, or less. Claiming less preserves UCC for later years; claiming the maximum is not compulsory, and in a low-income year it may not be the best use of the deduction.
  6. Keep the file: invoice, delivery record, in-service evidence, class note. The available-for-use date is a fact you may need to show, and December paperwork is easier to keep than March paperwork is to reconstruct.

What success looks like: for every year-end purchase you can state its class, its rate, its available-for-use date with evidence, and the exact first-year claim the half-year rule produces — and none of those four numbers came from the salesperson.
