The Employer Match Year-End Check: Claiming Every Matched Dollar Before December 31

An employer match left unclaimed in December does not carry forward. How group RRSP and pension matching, vesting and payroll timing actually work, and the year-end check.

Published 2026-10-21 · Last updated 2026-10-21

Disclaimer: General information only — not tax, financial or retirement advice. Matching formulas, vesting rules and payroll cut-offs are set by your own plan documents. Read them, or ask your plan administrator, before changing contributions.

Somewhere in your benefits package there may be a line worth more than any raise you will negotiate this year: your employer will add money to your retirement savings if you add yours first. Matching is routinely described as free money, and the description survives because it is mostly true — with one catch that December exposes. The match is calculated against contributions made through payroll in the plan year, under your plan's formula, and the unclaimed portion does not roll into January as a balance owing. A personal RRSP contribution enjoys the famous first-60-days window — for the 2025 tax year, contributions up to March 2, 2026 can still be deducted against 2025 (s3). Do not let that familiar deadline lull you: it governs when a contribution can be deducted, not whether your employer will match it. The match runs on the payroll calendar, and the payroll calendar ends in December.

How the match actually flows

In a group RRSP, your contribution leaves your paycheque and is deposited with the plan provider, and your employer's matching amount follows the plan's formula — commonly a percentage of salary, or a fraction of your own contribution up to a cap. The recording detail matters at year-end: for group plans, contributions processed through payroll are generally recorded based on the date funds are deposited with the plan provider, and receipts can be issued separately for each contribution period (s1). Translation: the last one or two paycheques of December are the border. A contribution change requested too late to reach the final payroll deposit belongs to next year's match calculation, whatever your intentions were. Sun Life's deadline guidance makes the operational point plainly for RRSP deadlines generally: because of processing times, contribute several business days ahead, or set up the contribution to run automatically (s3). In December, 'several business days ahead' means the conversation with payroll happens in November or the first days of December — not on the 30th.

What an unclaimed match is worth

Run the arithmetic on a published worked example. On a $90,000 salary, in a plan matching 50 cents per dollar up to 5% of salary, an employee contributing the full matched amount over two years accumulates about $13,500 before investment returns — $9,000 of their own money plus $4,500 of employer match (s2). Scale that to the December question. An employee who contributed only 3% of salary all year in a plan matching up to 5% has left 2% of salary of their own contributions — and the employer money attached to them — unclaimed for the entire year. There is no mechanism that pays it retroactively in January. The year-end check exists to catch that gap while one or two paycheques remain to close at least part of it, and to set the contribution rate correctly for the full year ahead instead of repeating the leak.

The year-end match check

  1. Find your plan's formula. From the plan booklet or administrator: the match rate, the cap (as a percentage of salary or of your contribution), and any waiting period before matching starts.
  2. Pull your year-to-date contributions. Your latest pay stub or the provider portal shows what you have actually contributed this year — not what you meant to contribute when you enrolled.
  3. Do the gap math. Compare your contribution percentage against the matched cap. Any percentage point below the cap is unclaimed employer money for every remaining paycheque, and for the year already gone.
  4. Check the vesting rule before you act on job plans. Vesting decides your entitlement to the employer's portion: under cliff vesting, leaving before the cliff means the employer contributions are forfeited, while your own contributions are always yours (s2). If a job change is on your January horizon, the vesting date — not the match rate — is the number that matters, and it belongs in that decision.
  5. Raise the contribution with payroll- processing time to spare. Submit the change early enough to land in the final December payroll deposit (s1, s3). Even one fully matched paycheque beats a perfect intention that processes in January.
  6. Set next year's rate in the same sitting. The durable fix is a contribution rate at the matched cap from the first January paycheque, so next December's check is a confirmation, not a rescue.

What success looks like: your year-to-date contribution percentage equals your plan's matched cap, you know your vesting date and status, and your January rate is already set — three facts you can state without opening a single document by the time you finish.

Two honest cautions

First, matched contributions still consume RRSP room: employer contributions to a group RRSP count toward your total limit, and the plan's pension adjustment reflects them. Claiming the full match is almost always right; piling unmatched lump sums on top without checking your notice of assessment is how over-contributions happen. Second, a match you cannot afford to reach is information, not failure. If contributing to the cap would break the monthly budget, raise the rate partway, schedule the next increase for your next raise, and know the size of what remains unclaimed. A plan you can state beats a guilt you cannot.

Our take: Of every move on this site, this is the only one that is literally someone offering you money with a December expiry. Check the formula, check your year-to-date, check vesting, and move the payroll change while December paycheques remain. January you will be glad November you read the booklet.

If you are nowhere near the cap

Plenty of readers will run the check in this article and find a gap too large to close in December: contributing at 1% against a 5% matched cap, in a year when the budget genuinely has no slack. The year-end move is still worth making, at whatever size is honest. A final-paycheque increase of even one percentage point claims match money that was otherwise certainly lost, and — more valuable — it changes the default. Contribution rates persist by inertia in both directions: the rate you set in December is the rate January inherits, and the raise you receive in spring lands on a higher base if you direct a share of it to the plan the week it arrives, before lifestyle quietly absorbs it. Sequence the climb in writing, on one page: today's rate, the rate after the spring raise, the rate at the cap, and the month each step takes effect. Show the page to nobody; it is not a promise, it is a plan, and plans survive moods. What does not survive is the vague intention to 'deal with retirement in the new year', which is how a second December arrives with the same gap and one less year to close it in.

FAQ

Can I claim last year's unclaimed match with a lump sum in January? The first-60-days window affects deductibility, not matching (s3). Matching follows your plan's payroll-based formula and periods (s1); assume unclaimed match from a closed plan year is gone unless your plan documents say otherwise.

Is the employer's match taxed? Employer contributions to a group RRSP are a taxable benefit added to your income and offset by the RRSP deduction the contribution generates — one more reason the receipts from the plan provider matter at tax time (s1).

What happens to the match if I leave my job? Your own contributions are always yours. The employer's portion depends on vesting: unvested amounts under a cliff or graded schedule can be forfeited on departure (s2). Read your plan's vesting terms before resigning, not after.
