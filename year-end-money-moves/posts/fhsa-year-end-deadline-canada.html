FHSA Year-End Deadline in Canada: Open, Contribute, Claim

One account, three separate December decisions — and two carry-forwards that are constantly confused with each other. General information only — not tax advice.

Two carry-forwards that are not the same thing

Most FHSA confusion at year-end comes from a single word: carry-forward. The First Home Savings Account actually has two of them, they follow different rules, and mixing them up leads to opposite mistakes — contributing too much in one case, and rushing a deduction that never needed rushing in the other.

The first is participation room: how much you are allowed to put in. Room is $8,000 in the year you open your first FHSA. If you contribute less than your room in a year, the unused amount carries forward — but only up to a maximum of $8,000. That cap means the most room you can ever have in a single year is $16,000: this year's $8,000 plus one year's worth of carried-forward room. The CRA's own worked example makes the arithmetic concrete: contribute $5,000 in the year you open the account, and the following year you can contribute $11,000 — $8,000 of new room plus the $3,000 you did not use.

The second carry-forward is the deduction: how much of what you already contributed you choose to claim on a tax return. Contributions you made but did not claim can generally be carried forward and claimed in a future year — and that carry-forward survives even beyond the closure of the account itself. There is no $8,000 annual cap described for this one. The lifetime ceiling is different: over your lifetime, the most you can deduct as an FHSA deduction is $40,000, and amounts transferred in from an RRSP reduce that deductible total because RRSP-to-FHSA transfers are not themselves deductible.

The three December decisions, in order

Decision one: open the account. FHSA room does not accumulate in the background the way TFSA room does. Participation room begins in the year you open your first FHSA, and in that first year the carryforward is $0 — you start with exactly $8,000 of room. Opening an account before December 31, even without contributing a dollar that day, starts the room for that year. Waiting until January means that year's $8,000 simply never exists. This is the cheapest year-end move on this page: it costs nothing but paperwork.

Decision two: contribute by December 31. The FHSA contribution period runs from January 1 to December 31 of the same year. A contribution made in January or February belongs to the new year, full stop. What you contribute by December 31 is what can support a deduction connected to that year's participation — and it is also what starts the second decision below.

Decision three: choose the deduction year — later, calmly. A contribution may be deducted on the return for the year of contribution or for a future year. If 2026 was a low-income year — a slow year in the business, a parental leave, a career change — claiming the deduction immediately may waste much of its value, and the rules do not force your hand. Contribute in December to protect the room; decide the claim when the year's income is actually known. One reporting duty does attach to the opening year, though: you must file Schedule 15 for the year you opened your first FHSA, even if you contributed nothing, so the CRA knows the account exists.

The first-60-days trap

Anyone who has made an RRSP contribution in February and deducted it on the prior year's return will find the FHSA's rule genuinely surprising: contributions made in the first 60 days of a year cannot be deducted on the previous year's return. There is no grace window to rescue a missed December. A contribution made on January 15, 2027 is a 2027 contribution — deductible for 2027 or a later year, never for 2026.

The decision framework: ask three questions separately. (1) Is an account open? If not, opening before December 31 is the deadline that matters most. (2) How much room do I have, and how much of it do I want to use this year? Contribute that amount by December 31. (3) Is this year's income the right year to absorb the deduction? If not, contribute anyway and carry the claim forward. Conflating questions 2 and 3 — skipping a contribution because the deduction year looks wrong — is the expensive error, because the room decision and the deduction decision were never the same decision.

The December procedure

  1. Find your real room before contributing. Your unused FHSA contributions and participation room appear on your latest notice of assessment or reassessment, on Form T1028, or in your CRA account under the Savings and pension plans tab. Room is a single limit across all your FHSAs, covering contributions and RRSP transfers combined — two accounts do not mean two limits.
  2. If no account exists, open one before December 31. This starts $8,000 of room for the year even if the first contribution happens later.
  3. Contribute up to the room you intend to use, in time for the institution to record it in 2026. Do not guess at processing cut-offs in the last week of December; contribute early enough that the date on the institution's record is unambiguous.
  4. Stay inside the room. Amounts above your participation room are an excess FHSA amount, taxed at 1% of the highest excess amount for each month the excess remains. An excess can be corrected with a designated withdrawal, or a designated transfer back to an RRSP where that applies, using Form RC727 through your financial institution — a designated amount is not included in income. Excess taxes are reported on forms RC728 and RC728-SCH-A, with any tax payable due by June 30 of the following year.
  5. Record the deduction decision separately. Write down, for tax season: total contributed in 2026, total you plan to claim for 2026, and the remainder being carried forward. Remember two non-deductible categories while you reconcile: contributions made after a first qualifying withdrawal cannot be deducted in any year, and an excess amount you designated as a withdrawal cannot be deducted either.
  6. File Schedule 15 if this was your opening year — even with a zero contribution — when you file the 2026 return.

What happens if you never buy the home

Opening early is less risky than it feels because the exit is orderly. An FHSA can remain open for up to 15 years, or until December 31 of the year you turn 71, whichever comes first. If no qualifying home is ever bought, unused savings can be transferred to an RRSP rather than withdrawn — so the down-payment fund becomes retirement savings instead of a tax problem. That backstop is exactly why the opening decision deserves more weight than the contribution decision in a year when cash is tight: the room started in 2026 can be filled later, within the carry-forward cap, and the money is not trapped if plans change.

What success looks like

On January 2, you can state three numbers without logging in anywhere: your FHSA was open before year-end (or deliberately was not), the amount you contributed in 2026 against the room you confirmed, and the amount of deduction you are carrying forward on purpose — not by accident. If the third number is a choice rather than a surprise, the December work is done.

FAQ

Can a January contribution be deducted for the year that just ended? No. First-60-days contributions belong to the new year for FHSA purposes; the RRSP's carry-back does not exist here.

I contributed but my income was low this year. Did I waste the contribution? No. The contribution used room in the year it was made; the deduction itself can be claimed in a future year instead, and unclaimed amounts carry forward even beyond the account's closure.

How much can I contribute next year if I only put in part of my room? Your new $8,000 plus the unused room carried forward, to a maximum carry-forward of $8,000 — so no more than $16,000 of room in any single year, subject to the $40,000 lifetime limit.
