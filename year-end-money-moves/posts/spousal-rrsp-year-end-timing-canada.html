Spousal RRSP Year-End Timing: Why December Beats the First 60 Days

The general RRSP can wait until March. The spousal RRSP cannot — not because of the deduction, but because of a calendar-year clock most couples never see. General information only — not tax or financial advice.

The one RRSP move where December genuinely matters

The standard year-end advice is correct for a personal RRSP: contributions made in the first 60 days of 2027 can still be deducted on the 2026 return, so there is rarely a reason to rush in December. A spousal RRSP breaks that advice. In a spousal plan, the higher-income partner contributes to an RRSP owned by the other partner. The contributor uses their own RRSP deduction room and claims the deduction; the money belongs to the annuitant spouse, who controls the account and decides when to withdraw. Done patiently, retirement withdrawals are taxed in the lower-income spouse's hands instead of the contributor's — the entire point of the arrangement.

The guardrail is the CRA's attribution rule. If the annuitant withdraws from a spousal RRSP, the amount taxed back to the contributor is the lesser of the withdrawal and the contributor's total contributions to any of the spouse's spousal RRSPs in the year of the withdrawal or either of the two preceding calendar years. Two features of that rule drive everything in this guide: it counts contributions across all of the spouse's spousal plans, not just the one the money came out of, and it runs on calendar years, not on months elapsed. A contribution's clock does not care whether it was made on January 2 or December 30 of its year — which means December contributions start their clock almost a year earlier than January ones carrying the same deduction.

The attribution calculation: one day, one full year

MoneySense's Ask a Planner column works the example in its sharpest form: contribute to a spousal RRSP on December 31, 2026, and the withdrawal window opens on January 1, 2029 — two years and one day later. Make the identical contribution one day later, on January 1, 2027, and the earliest attribution-free withdrawal moves to January 1, 2030. The column's conclusion is blunt: when making spousal RRSP contributions, make them before the year-end instead of in the first 60 days of the new year. An accounting firm's year-end planning note makes the same point from the other side — a January or February contribution can still be deducted on the prior year's return, so the deduction is identical; what changes is only the year the spouse's withdrawals become taxable in the spouse's own hands, and it changes by a full year.

Price that year of flexibility honestly. The household benefit of a spousal RRSP is the gap between the contributor's marginal tax rate when the deduction is claimed and the annuitant's marginal rate when the money is withdrawn. As a purely illustrative calculation — using round, hypothetical rates, not any province's actual brackets — a $10,000 contribution deducted at a 40% marginal rate saves $4,000 now; withdrawn years later by a spouse at a 25% marginal rate, it costs $2,500, a household gain of $1,500 on that $10,000. Attribution does not reduce that eventual gain; it postpones the date the gain becomes available, and it does so by calendar years. Couples planning withdrawals in a specific year — an early retirement at 60, a sabbatical, a parental leave — are the ones for whom a December slip becomes a real cost: the withdrawal they planned for 2029 lands in the contributor's higher-rate hands instead. Couples with no withdrawal planned for a decade lose nothing but optionality, which is still worth a free December signature.

Spousal RRSP or pension splitting? The decision framework

Since pension income splitting arrived, a fair question is whether the spousal RRSP still earns its complexity. The two tools operate at different ages and on different income. Pension splitting is a paper election: at 65 or older, a couple can allocate up to 50% of eligible pension income — RRIF withdrawals included — to the lower-income spouse on Form T1032, with no money changing hands. But it caps at 50%, it does not reach RRSP lump-sum withdrawals at all (only RRSP annuity payments qualify as eligible pension income), OAS is never eligible, and CPP has its own separate sharing process. Before 65, the RRIF route to splitting is essentially closed, which is exactly the window where a lopsided RRSP balance hurts a couple retiring early. The framework that follows: if the household's income gap will persist into retirement and withdrawals before 65 are plausible, the spousal RRSP does work pension splitting cannot — it moves the actual capital, in full, not a 50% paper allocation. If both partners will retire after 65 with similar incomes anyway, the spousal plan adds little beyond what the T1032 election already provides, and simplicity wins. The tools also combine: spousal contributions during the working years, then the splitting election fine-tuning the retirement years.

Three structural details the December rush should not trample: contributions to a spousal RRSP and the contributor's own RRSP share one deduction limit — for 2026, the lesser of 18% of the previous year's earned income and $33,810, plus unused room. The contributor can keep contributing to a spousal RRSP until December 31 of the year the annuitant turns 71, even if the contributor is already past 71 — one of the few ways a household keeps an RRSP deduction alive. And once a spousal RRSP becomes a RRIF, attribution does not apply to the minimum RRIF payment, only to amounts withdrawn above the minimum.

The year-end checklist: work it in this order

  1. Confirm the contributor's deduction room first. Spousal and personal contributions draw on the same limit printed on the contributor's CRA notice of assessment. Contributing beyond it invites over-contribution penalties that no attribution cleverness offsets.
  2. Decide the split between personal and spousal contributions. Only the spousal portion carries the attribution clock, so households often direct the December contribution to the spousal plan and let any personal RRSP top-up wait for the first-60-days window, when 2026 income is final.
  3. Make the spousal contribution before December 31, 2026 — received, not initiated. The reward for the December date is the January 1, 2029 opening of the attribution-free window. A contribution that settles in January earns the same deduction and a window a full year later.
  4. Keep spousal and personal plans separate. Attribution looks at contributions to any of the annuitant's spousal RRSPs, so merging a spouse's personal RRSP into the spousal plan can pull an old balance into a fresh clock. Separate accounts keep the dates legible.
  5. Record the last contribution date in writing. The household's earliest clean withdrawal date is January 1 of the second year after the last contribution year. Future contributions restart the clock — note the current date somewhere both partners will find it in 2029.
  6. Note the exceptions, do not plan around them. Attribution does not apply for the year the contributor dies or when either spouse is a non-resident at the time of payment — context for an accountant, not a strategy.

What success looks like

By January 2, 2027, the household can state: the amount contributed to the spousal RRSP for 2026 and the date it was received, the contributor's remaining deduction room, and the earliest attribution-free withdrawal date — January 1, 2029 if the contribution landed in December. The deduction itself was never at risk; it would have survived until March. What December bought was a year of freedom at the other end of the plan, for the price of moving a signature a few weeks earlier.
