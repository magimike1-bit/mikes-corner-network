Year-End Capital Gains Timing in a Non-Registered Account: Realizing Gains vs Losses Before December 31 in Canada

The December move most people should not make is selling a winner purely to beat a tax-rate increase that is not happening. The moves worth making are quieter. General information only — not tax or financial advice.

First, the move that is usually wrong

Start with who should close this page. If the plan is to realize a large capital gain in December 2026 because a higher inclusion rate is supposedly arriving, the plan rests on a rule that does not exist. If the plan is to sell a holding you still want, at a gain, purely because the calendar is turning, the tax tail is wagging the portfolio. And if a sale would push a one-off gain into the highest bracket you are likely to face in the next several years — a year with a bonus, a business sale, or severance in it — realizing that gain in December is the expensive option, not the clever one.

What remains after those exclusions is a real but narrower decision, and it runs in both directions: sometimes a gain should be pulled into 2026 on purpose, and sometimes the same gain should be pushed into January 2027. The rest of this piece is the arithmetic and the calendar for telling the two apart. Everything here concerns non-registered (taxable) accounts only — gains and losses inside an RRSP, TFSA, or FHSA are not reported, so there is no timing decision to make inside them.

The rate fact that settles half the arguments

Canada has no separate capital gains tax rate. A capital gain is the proceeds of disposition minus the adjusted cost base (ACB) and the costs of selling, and a portion of it — the inclusion rate — is added to your income and taxed at your marginal federal and provincial rates, reported on Schedule 3 with the taxable amount flowing to line 12700 of the return. The CRA's inclusion-rate table lists that portion as one-half (50%) for every year from 2001 through 2025.

The confusion has a specific history worth retiring. An increase to two-thirds (66.67%) was proposed, complete with a $250,000 annual threshold for individuals in much of the coverage. It was deferred to a January 1, 2026 start, and then cancelled outright in March 2025. The two-thirds rate never applied to a completed tax year, and year-end advice built around beating it — or around sheltering gains under a $250,000 threshold — is planning around a ghost. For a gain realized in 2026, half is taxable, the same as a gain realized in January 2027. Timing still matters, but it matters because of your income in each year and the loss balances on your file, not because the rate itself changes at midnight.

When realizing a gain in December is the deliberate move

You are carrying unused net capital losses. A net capital loss on your CRA record can be applied against taxable capital gains, and your notice of assessment carries the balance. Losses must be applied in order — earlier years' losses before later years' — and a loss balance that sits unused while gains are deferred year after year is an asset earning nothing. If a position was going to be sold in the next year or two anyway, realizing the gain in a year when carried-forward losses can absorb the taxable half can make the sale effectively tax-free, and December 31 is the last day to put that gain into 2026.

2026 is an unusually low-income year. The taxable half of a gain lands on top of your other income and is taxed at the marginal rates it reaches. A year with a career gap, a parental leave, or a slow year in the business can absorb a gain at lower rates than a normal year will. Splitting works the same way in reverse: a large position does not have to be sold in one tax year. Selling part in December and the rest in January spreads the gain across two years of brackets and two years of income-tested calculations, instead of stacking it into one.

A loss and a gain belong to the same year. This is where the gain side meets the loss side. An allowable capital loss in a year must first be applied against that year's taxable capital gains before any remainder becomes a net capital loss to carry elsewhere. Realizing a planned gain in the same year as a harvested loss lets the two cancel directly on one return — cleaner than carrying the loss back on Form T1A to amend the tax picture of 2023, 2024, or 2025, even though that carry-back (three years back, or forward indefinitely) remains the fallback for losses that outlast the year's gains.

The deferral row is the one with a guaranteed payoff, and it needs no special circumstances: selling in January instead of December defers the tax on the gain by a full year, because the gain belongs to 2027 instead of 2026. That is the standard reason year-end advice tells holders of appreciated positions to wait. It stops being the right answer when one of the December rows above is worth more than a year's deferral — an expiring low-income year and an unused loss balance are both worth more.

The decision framework: (1) Pull your unused net capital loss balance from your latest notice of assessment. A balance large enough to absorb the taxable half of the gain makes December realization the default. (2) Compare 2026 income with your expected 2027 income, honestly. The gain belongs in the lower year. (3) If the position is large, price the split — part in December, part in January — before pricing an all-in-one-year sale. (4) Whatever you decide, run the trade against the settlement clock below, not against December 31 itself. A decision made on December 30 that settles in January is a January decision, whatever the intention was.

The settlement clock and the deemed-disposition traps

The CRA's year for a securities sale is set by the settlement date, not the trade date. Canada moved to T+1 settlement in May 2024: a trade settles one business day after it is placed, so a trade executed on December 31, 2026 will typically settle in January and count toward 2027. For 2026 planning, trades entered on or before December 30, 2026 are the ones that settle by year-end — and brokerages publish their own December deadlines, which are the dates to work to in practice. The same clock governs loss sales, which is why the harvesting window and the gain window close on the same day.

Two traps sit next to the clock. The first is the in-kind transfer: moving investments from a non-registered account into a TFSA is a deemed disposition. If the holding has gained, the transfer realizes the gain in the year of the transfer — which can be a deliberate December move or an accidental one, depending on whether anyone checked the ACB first. If the holding has lost value, the loss cannot be claimed on the transfer at all, so a losing position should never be moved in-kind when the loss itself has value. The second trap is the superficial loss rule, which matters when a December plan touches both sides of the ledger: a loss is denied if you or an affiliated person — a spouse or common-law partner, or a corporation you control — buys the same or identical property in the window from 30 days before the sale to 30 days after it and still holds it at the end of that window. The denied loss is not gone; it is usually added to the ACB of the replacement property. But it does not offset anything on the 2026 return, and a gain realized to meet it will be taxed in full.

What success looks like

  • Every 2026 disposition was a decision, not a default. Each gain in the non-registered account was realized because a loss balance, a low-income year, or a planned split made 2026 the right year — or it was deferred to January on purpose.
  • No trade rests on the cancelled inclusion-rate increase. The arithmetic for every sale uses the 50% inclusion rate in both years.
  • Trades were placed against the settlement date. Anything meant to count for 2026 was entered by the brokerage's December deadline, on or before December 30, 2026 under T+1.
  • No in-kind TFSA transfer was made blind. Gainers were transferred knowingly or not at all; losers were sold, never transferred, when the loss had value.
  • The paperwork matches the plan. Dispositions are recorded for Schedule 3, the loss balance on the notice of assessment is updated in your notes, and any remaining net capital loss is earmarked: carry back on Form T1A within the three-year window, or forward to the next deliberate gain.
