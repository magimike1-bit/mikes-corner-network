Year-End Review of Insurance and Beneficiary Designations: The December Paperwork Check

Five records, five different holders, one hour in December. The names on file decide where the money goes — this is the check that makes sure those names are still the right ones. General information only — not financial, tax, or legal advice.

The trap: the form on file wins, and it never updates itself

Five is the number to start with. A typical household's death-benefit instructions live in at least five separate records: a personal life insurance policy, group insurance through work, an RRSP, a TFSA, and a will. They are held by different institutions, they were filled in at different moments in life, and no life event reaches into any of them to make an update. Designations do not change when you marry, separate, or have another child.

In most cases, the beneficiary named on an insurance policy decides who receives the money — not the will. The will usually comes into it only when the estate is the beneficiary, or when the will names the policy specifically and changes the designation, which provincial law can allow. Registered accounts work the same way: a beneficiary named directly on the plan is paid directly, outside the estate. So a will rewritten last spring can sit beside an RRSP form from a decade ago, and the form is the instruction the institution follows. The year-end review of insurance and beneficiary designations exists to close exactly that gap, while closing it is still cheap and simple.

What to pull before you check anything

The check runs on records, not memory. Gather:

  • Every insurance policy — personal life insurance, plus the group coverage booklet or statement from work. Each policy is checked separately; a change sent to one insurer says nothing about the others.
  • Every registered account statement — RRSP, RRIF, and TFSA, at every institution. Old accounts at former employers' plan providers belong on the pile.
  • The current will — not to change the designations with it in this sitting, but to read the two side by side and spot where they point at different people.
  • Any separation agreement or court order that touches insurance or beneficiaries. Some designations cannot be changed freely, and an agreement may require coverage to be kept or a beneficiary to stay named.
  • Last year's version of this list, if one exists. The point of the December timing is that next December's check takes minutes because this one left a record.

Two of those rows deserve a slower read. On the RRSP row: at death, the holder is treated as having disposed of the plan at fair market value, and that value is fully taxable as income in the year of death. Where the beneficiary is a qualifying survivor — a spouse or common-law partner, or a financially dependent child or grandchild — a direct transfer to the survivor's own registered plan can defer the tax, with the transfer completed in the year the survivor receives the proceeds or within 60 days after that year ends. Name anyone else and there is no deferral: the beneficiary receives the plan, and the estate pays the tax on it.

That split is the finding to watch for. A designation that sends the RRSP to one person and leaves the estate — and its other beneficiaries — to fund the tax bill is a common way for an estate plan to quietly become unfair.

On the TFSA row: a successor holder steps into the account itself. The TFSA continues to exist, its value at death and the income earned afterward stay sheltered from tax, and becoming successor holder does not use up the survivor's own contribution room. A beneficiary, by contrast, receives a payment and the account ends. Both are legitimate choices; they are not interchangeable, and the form on file records which one was chosen — possibly years ago, possibly by default.

The December paperwork check, step by step

  1. List every record. One line per policy and account: holder institution, type, and where the designation is recorded. If a record cannot be found, that is the first finding — a policy nobody can locate cannot be claimed quickly, and someone trusted should know where the information is stored and whom to contact.
  2. Confirm each designation with the institution that holds it. The beneficiary information held by the insurer or plan provider is what will be acted on, so confirm it directly with the company rather than relying on a copy in a drawer. Ask for the primary beneficiary, any contingent beneficiary, and the percentages, exactly as recorded.
  3. Check the revocable/irrevocable status. A revocable beneficiary can generally be changed by the policy owner — note that only the owner can name or change a beneficiary, not the person insured. An irrevocable designation normally needs the beneficiary's written consent to change. If you do not know which type you have, ask before planning around an assumption.
  4. Deal with minors explicitly. A minor generally cannot receive and manage insurance proceeds directly; the Government of Canada notes a trust, trustee, or administrator may be appropriate, and provincial rules differ. With no trustee named on a policy, the money may be held by the provincial public guardian or the court until the age of majority. If a child is named anywhere, this step is the one that needs professional advice, not a form alone.
  5. Read the designations against this year's life. The standard triggers: marriage, separation or divorce, a birth or adoption, the death of a named beneficiary, a child reaching adulthood, starting or selling a business, a new or updated will, a major change in coverage, and approaching retirement. Any one of them in 2026 means at least one record deserves a fresh look.
  6. Make changes through the holder's own process, and get confirmation. Changes are completed using the insurer's or institution's required forms and process. Designations can also be made or changed in a will in some circumstances, but that route needs the institution's records to actually be updated and confirmed. File the confirmation with the policy, dated.
  7. Write the one-page record. Institution, account or policy type, primary and contingent names, percentages, revocable or irrevocable, date confirmed, and date of any change submitted. Store it securely with the will. This page is next December's starting point.

The decision framework, if a name is wrong: ask three questions in order. (1) Can it be changed by the owner alone? If the designation is revocable, yes; if irrevocable, the named person's written consent is required, and a separation agreement may add its own restrictions — read it before promising anyone a change. (2) Does the change belong on the institution's form or in the will? The form is the direct route; a change made only in the will still has to reach the institution's records. (3) Does the change move a tax bill? On an RRSP or RRIF it can — changing who receives the plan changes who can defer its tax, while the estate's liability stays where it was. Any change that fails question 3 deserves an accountant's or lawyer's eyes before it is filed.

Coverage gaps the same sitting catches

While the policies are out, four coverage facts are worth the extra ten minutes. First, term policies have two dates that matter: the renewal date, when the premium steps up — often sharply — and the conversion deadline, the last day to switch to permanent coverage without a new medical, set by the contract. Both are December-checkable and neither forgives being discovered late. Second, group coverage through work usually ends when the job ends, and often at retirement, while a personally owned policy continues as long as premiums are paid; some group plans allow conversion on leaving, with limited options and a deadline. A job change in 2026 is therefore both a beneficiary event and a coverage event. Third, the review standard the insurers themselves use is simple: check the amount, the years left, the premium, who gets paid, and health and smoking status against life as it is now — lowering coverage is usually paperwork with no new medical, while increasing it means a new application at today's age and health. Fourth, if no beneficiary is effectively named — or the named beneficiary has died with no contingent behind them — proceeds may be payable to the estate, where they can face delays, creditor claims, and probate or estate administration costs that a direct designation avoids.

None of that requires buying anything in December. It requires knowing four dates and five names, and writing them down.

What success looks like on January 1

The check is done when one page exists that did not exist in November: every policy and registered account listed, the primary and contingent beneficiary on file for each confirmed with the institution holding it, the revocable or irrevocable status noted, the term renewal and conversion dates recorded, and any change submitted through the holder's process with its confirmation filed. If a designation turned out to be wrong, it is either fixed or written down as a January task with the reason — consent needed, agreement to read, advice to get. An hour of December paperwork is what stands between a family's intentions and a set of forms filled in for a life that no longer exists.
