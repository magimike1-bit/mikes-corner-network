Year-End TFSA Withdrawals: Timing the December Move for January Room

What is one calendar day worth inside a TFSA? Potentially about a year of contribution room. The CRA adds a withdrawal back to your room on January 1 of the next calendar year, never sooner, so a withdrawal planned for early January often belongs in December instead. General information only, not tax or financial advice.

Two dates, two very different waits

Start with the comparison, because the whole decision sits in it. The rule itself is short: withdrawing money from a TFSA does not create room right away, and the amount comes back as available contribution room on January 1 of the following year. Apply that one rule to three withdrawal dates and the December move explains itself.

Withdrawal date (labelled examples)Room returnsWait
December 15, 2026January 1, 202717 days
December 31, 2026January 1, 20271 day
January 2, 2027January 1, 2028364 days

Nothing about the money changes between the second and third rows. The amount is the same, the account is the same, the reason for withdrawing is the same. Only the calendar moved, and with it a full year of flexibility. If a January expense, a planned account move, or a simple wish to reset the account is already on your list, the withdrawal that funds it earns a December date.

The room formula, before any money moves

The CRA calculates TFSA contribution room as the current year dollar limit, plus any unused room from previous years, plus withdrawals made in the previous year, minus contributions already made this year. The dollar limit for 2026 is $7,000, and it was added to room on January 1, 2026. Written as a checklist for your own worksheet, the calculation looks like this:

ComponentDirectionWhere the number comes from
2026 TFSA dollar limit, $7,000AddSet by the CRA for the year
Unused room carried from earlier yearsAddYour own contribution and withdrawal records
Withdrawals you made in 2025AddYour issuer statements for last year
Contributions already made in 2026SubtractYour issuer statements for this year, across every TFSA you hold

Run this formula before a December withdrawal, not after. The withdrawal itself does not change this year's room at all, which is precisely the point readers miss: money out in December is not room back in December.

A worked example, with the assumptions on the table

Labelled example, with every assumption stated. Suppose a saver has used all available room by the autumn of 2026, so unused room sits at $0. A planned expense of $8,000 lands in January 2027, and the saver expects to rebuild the TFSA during 2027.

  • Withdraw $8,000 in December 2026: on January 1, 2027, the $8,000 returns as room, alongside whatever dollar limit the CRA sets for 2027. The saver can recontribute the full $8,000 during 2027 without touching the new year limit.
  • Withdraw the same $8,000 in January 2027: the room returns on January 1, 2028. Rebuilding during 2027 is limited to the 2027 dollar limit plus any unused room, which in this example is $0.
  • The error version: the saver withdraws $8,000 in December 2026 and, forgetting the rule, recontributes $8,000 later that same month. With $0 of unused room, that is an $8,000 excess, and the CRA taxes an excess at 1 percent per month for as long as it stays in the account: $80 for each month the mistake sits there.

The example is arithmetic, not a forecast. Change the withdrawal amount and every figure scales with it; the dates and the 1 percent rate are the fixed parts.

The December procedure

  1. Calculate your room from your own records. Use the formula above, or the CRA worksheet, Form RC343. Total every TFSA you hold, because available room applies across all of them combined.
  2. Decide the withdrawal date against the comparison table. If the money will be wanted back inside the TFSA during 2027, a December date returns the room on January 1, 2027.
  3. Make the withdrawal through your financial institution. Withdrawals go through the issuer, and depending on the investments held, a withdrawal can generally be made at any time during the year. Leave working days, not hours, between the request and December 31, so the withdrawal lands in the year you intended.
  4. Record the amount and the date in your own file. Your issuer must report the year withdrawals to the CRA by the end of February of the next year, but your January decisions should run on your records, not on a report you have not seen yet.
  5. Hold the recontribution until January 1, unless unused room covers it. The CRA wording is direct: never recontribute a withdrawal in the same calendar year unless you know you have enough unused room.
  6. On January 1, rebuild your room figure. Unused room, plus the December withdrawal added back, plus the new year dollar limit, minus nothing yet. That is the ceiling for 2027 contributions.

When the December move is not worth it

Three readers can skip this entirely. If you hold enough unused room to recontribute the amount anyway, the calendar adds nothing; the room was never the constraint. If the withdrawn money is leaving the TFSA for good, the add back date is a bookkeeping detail, not a benefit. And if the amount is small and no 2027 recontribution is planned, a year of theoretical room is worth very little in practice. The December move pays when a recontribution is genuinely planned for the following year. Otherwise it is tidiness dressed as strategy.

The traps, collected last on purpose

  • Trusting the CRA account figure in December. The CRA instructs savers to calculate room from their own records rather than the information in the CRA account, because that account updates only once a year in the spring, using the previous year transactions, after issuers report at the end of February. A figure that looks current in December may be missing most of the year.
  • Recontributing in the same year. The withdrawal creates no room until January 1. A same year recontribution counts against whatever room you already had, and any excess is taxed at 1 percent per month while it remains.
  • Treating an investment loss as a withdrawal. Changes in the value of TFSA investments do not affect contribution room. A loss does not count as a withdrawal and is never added back, so topping the account back up after a loss uses ordinary room.
  • Moving money between TFSAs by hand. To shift funds from one TFSA to another without tax consequences, the CRA directs savers to use a direct transfer through the receiving institution. Withdrawing it yourself and recontributing it can create an over-contribution by accident.
  • Counting one account and forgetting the other. Room is a single pool across every TFSA in your name. December arithmetic done on one account, while a second account also took contributions this year, is how excess amounts happen.

What success looks like

The move is done when three numbers sit in your records and agree with each other: the room you calculated from your own statements before withdrawing, the withdrawal amount and its December date, and the room you will hold on January 1 once the withdrawal is added back and the new dollar limit lands. If the third number was worked out before the money moved, January becomes a recontribution you planned, not a penalty you meet in the spring.

— Penny Hughes, Money Writer — Saving Desk
